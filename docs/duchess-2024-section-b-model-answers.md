# Model Answer 1 — Minor Characters

## Question

Explore Webster’s presentation of minor characters in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents minor characters in *The Duchess of Malfi* as structurally crucial figures who expose the moral condition of the world surrounding the Duchess. They are not merely decorative or functional. Through characters such as Cariola, Delio, Julia, Castruccio, the Old Lady, Pescara and the Doctor, Webster enlarges the play’s social and moral range beyond the central conflict between the Duchess, her brothers, Antonio and Bosola. Minor characters reveal how corruption spreads through courtly service, marriage, sexuality, patronage, medicine, comedy and spectatorship. They also sharpen the audience’s judgement: some minor figures humanise the Duchess’s private world, while others show how the court turns people into instruments, fools or casualties.

Cariola is one of Webster’s most important minor characters because she gives the Duchess’s marriage a domestic and moral witness. In the secret marriage scene, Cariola’s presence makes the union more than a private fantasy between the Duchess and Antonio. She functions almost as a substitute witness to a socially forbidden but emotionally sincere marriage. Her comment, “Whether the spirit of greatness or of woman / Reign most in her, I know not,” captures the play’s central tension between aristocratic status and female desire. The antithesis between “greatness” and “woman” shows that the Duchess’s identity is divided by social expectation. In a Jacobean context, a widow of high rank could possess some legal independence, but her remarriage still threatened lineage, inheritance and family honour. Cariola’s wondering response helps Webster frame the Duchess’s choice as both admirable and dangerous.

Cariola also humanises the Duchess in suffering. During the imprisonment and execution scenes, she is not a heroic tragic figure in the same way as the Duchess, but her fear makes the horror of the scene more immediate. Her resistance to death — pleading, panicking and clinging to life — contrasts with the Duchess’s composed self-definition, “I am Duchess of Malfi still.” Webster uses this contrast carefully. Cariola’s terror does not diminish her; it makes the Duchess’s stoicism appear even more extraordinary. The scene also prevents the execution from becoming abstractly noble. It reminds the audience that revenge-tragedy spectacle destroys ordinary human beings, including servants whose deaths are politically unnecessary.

Delio performs a different function. He is a minor character who gives the play a reflective and moral frame. At the beginning, his conversation with Antonio helps introduce the court as a place where virtue and corruption can be compared. At the end, he attempts to make “noble use / Of this great ruin,” turning catastrophe into moral instruction. The phrase is balanced but uneasy: “noble use” suggests ethical recovery, while “great ruin” carries the heavier emotional force. Webster uses Delio to supply the kind of closing judgement expected in tragedy, but the closure remains unstable because Delio has been largely absent from the worst violence. His moralising is necessary, yet it cannot repair what the audience has witnessed.

Julia is another significant minor character because she exposes gendered double standards. As the Cardinal’s mistress, she reveals the hypocrisy of a society that punishes the Duchess’s sexuality while concealing male appetite. The Duchess’s remarriage is treated as dynastic scandal, but the Cardinal’s sexual behaviour is protected by secrecy and office. Julia’s language and actions can appear reckless, but Webster uses her to show how women near power are both desiring subjects and disposable bodies. Her poisoning by the Cardinal is especially revealing: he uses intimacy as an instrument of murder, converting the kiss or contact associated with erotic closeness into a mechanism of control. In a Jacobean context, the corrupt churchman would also carry anti-Catholic theatrical associations, but Webster’s wider point is about institutional male impunity.

Castruccio and the Old Lady contribute to Webster’s satiric presentation of the court. Their comic scenes may seem secondary, but they expose the emptiness and performative absurdity of courtly culture. Castruccio’s concern with fashionable travel and superficial sophistication reflects a world in which appearance matters more than moral substance. The Old Lady’s cosmetics and Bosola’s attack on artificial beauty belong to the play’s wider obsession with bodies, surfaces and corruption. Webster’s minor comic figures therefore prepare the audience for the more serious forms of disguise and deception that dominate the tragedy. They show that the court is theatrical long before Ferdinand’s staged torments in Act 4.

Pescara is a minor character who clarifies the politics of patronage and justice. His refusal to grant Antonio’s confiscated lands to Julia, while later giving them to a nobleman, exposes how status governs access to reward. The moment is often brief in performance, but it is contextually rich. Jacobean society depended on patronage networks, and service to powerful figures could determine survival. Bosola’s resentment emerges from precisely this world of dependence and neglected service. Pescara’s role therefore helps Webster show that corruption is not only personal villainy; it is embedded in the distribution of favour, title and property.

The Doctor in Act 5 gives Ferdinand’s madness a social and theatrical frame. By diagnosing “lycanthropia,” he gives a medical name to what the audience has already seen as moral and psychological disorder. The clinical language is significant because it cannot fully contain Ferdinand’s guilt. His condition is more than illness: it is the outward eruption of predatory violence that has existed in his imagination throughout the play. Early modern drama often uses madness to reveal hidden truth, and Webster’s minor medical figure allows the audience to watch a culture attempt to classify the consequences of its own brutality.

Even the executioners, madmen and attendants matter as minor or anonymous figures because they make power visible as a theatrical system. The madmen’s masque turns entertainment into punishment, while the executioners reduce murder to procedure. The Duchess is not killed in a private outburst but through an organised spectacle of intimidation, props, sound and command. Webster therefore uses minor figures to stage the machinery of cruelty. They show that tyranny requires assistants, messengers, performers and servants; evil is distributed through a whole social network.

Ultimately, Webster’s minor characters are essential to the play’s tragic design. Cariola makes the Duchess’s private world emotionally visible; Delio frames the tragedy morally; Julia exposes sexual hypocrisy; Castruccio and the Old Lady satirise courtly surfaces; Pescara reveals patronage politics; the Doctor names but cannot explain guilt; and the anonymous figures of the prison and court show how violence becomes institutional. Webster’s presentation of minor characters therefore extends the tragedy beyond one household. They create a whole Jacobean world in which private virtue is surrounded by social performance, corruption, fear and compromised service.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer reaches high Level 5 because it avoids treating minor characters as a list of small roles. AO1 is sustained through a conceptual argument: minor characters expose the moral, social and theatrical ecology of Malfi. AO2 is developed through analysis of dramatic function, contrast, framing, satire, stage spectacle, dialogue, antithesis, medical discourse and tragic closure. AO3 is embedded through Jacobean widowhood, aristocratic marriage, female reputation, patronage, courtly performance, anti-Catholic stage convention, early modern medicine and revenge-tragedy machinery.

# Model Answer 2 — Setting

## Question

Explore how Webster makes use of setting in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster makes setting in *The Duchess of Malfi* more than background. Setting becomes a dramatic language through which corruption, surveillance, secrecy, imprisonment and moral collapse are made visible. The play moves between court, private chamber, household, prison, ruined religious space and violent final darkness; each setting changes how the audience understands power. Webster’s settings are not neutral places. They shape behaviour, expose political structures and turn private acts into public danger. The tragedy depends on the fact that there is almost nowhere in the world of the play where love, truth or conscience can remain safe.

The court is the play’s first and most important setting because Webster presents it as the source of contamination. Antonio’s opening comparison between a ruler and a fountain establishes a political geography: if the “head” is poisoned, “death and diseases” spread through the whole land. This metaphor turns setting into moral structure. A court should be the centre of order, justice and patronage, but in Webster’s tragedy it becomes the source of disease. In a Jacobean context, the court was imagined as the centre of political authority and social advancement. Webster exploits that expectation by presenting courtly space as morally infected.

The Malfi court is also a setting of surveillance. The Duchess is surrounded by brothers, servants, spies and observers who treat her body and choices as matters of public control. Ferdinand’s instruction that Bosola should be used “for your intelligence” turns the household into an intelligence network. This changes the meaning of domestic space. The Duchess’s palace should be her seat of authority, yet it becomes a place where her privacy is penetrated. Webster uses setting to expose patriarchal power: female rule exists, but it is watched and undermined from within.

The Duchess’s private chamber is central because it briefly offers an alternative to the corrupt court. Her secret marriage to Antonio takes place in a more intimate setting, away from public ceremony and dynastic negotiation. When she says, “I do here put off all vain ceremony,” the adverb “here” matters: this place allows her to strip away the artificial performance of rank. The chamber becomes a space of chosen identity, where the Duchess can speak as woman, lover and moral agent rather than merely as aristocratic widow. However, the secrecy of the setting also reveals danger. In a society where aristocratic marriage involved property, inheritance and reputation, privacy itself becomes politically threatening.

Webster repeatedly shows that private spaces cannot remain protected. Ferdinand’s invasion of the Duchess’s chamber after discovering the marriage transforms intimacy into threat. The stage action is crucial: the brother enters a space associated with female privacy, turning domestic interior into a site of judgement and punishment. His imagined violence against the Duchess and Antonio demonstrates that patriarchal control is spatial as well as verbal. It seeks to enter, inspect and dominate the spaces where female autonomy might exist.

The setting of Rome and the Cardinal’s sphere expands the play from family tragedy into institutional corruption. The Cardinal’s power is associated with religious and political office, but Webster presents that space as morally hollow. His affair with Julia and his later poisoning of her reveal that institutional settings do not protect virtue; they conceal vice. In a Protestant Jacobean theatrical culture, corrupt Catholic figures often carried political and religious suspicion. Webster uses the Cardinal’s setting not simply for anti-Catholic effect, but to show how public sanctity can mask private violence.

The prison setting in Act 4 is the play’s most concentrated use of theatrical space. The Duchess is enclosed, isolated and forced to become spectator to Ferdinand’s torments. The wax figures, severed hand, darkness and masque of madmen transform the prison into a theatre of cruelty. Webster’s setting here is active: it is designed to attack the mind. The Duchess’s line, “I account this world a tedious theatre, / For I do play a part in’t ’gainst my will,” turns the prison into a metaphor for the wider world. She recognises that the court has always forced people into roles; the prison merely makes that coercion visible.

The prison also becomes the setting for the Duchess’s moral victory. Her declaration, “I am Duchess of Malfi still,” gains force because it is spoken in a place designed to strip her of identity. The adverb “still” resists the setting. She remains herself despite confinement, humiliation and approaching death. Webster therefore uses setting dialectically: the more degraded the physical surroundings become, the more clearly the Duchess’s inner sovereignty appears. In the good-death tradition, the manner of dying could reveal spiritual condition. The prison becomes the place where worldly power is exposed as weaker than moral constancy.

The ruined fortification and echo scene in Act 5 give setting a symbolic and structural function after the Duchess’s death. Antonio and Delio move through a space associated with decay, history and broken religious architecture. The echo’s warning, “Thou art a dead thing,” makes the setting seem to speak with the Duchess’s absent voice. Whether the echo is supernatural, acoustic or psychological, Webster uses the place itself to dramatise belated knowledge. Ruins externalise the play’s moral condition: the world has not simply experienced individual deaths, but structural collapse.

Darkness is also a setting in the final act. Antonio’s accidental death occurs in confusion, misrecognition and poor visibility. This is not merely a practical stage device; it expresses the moral darkness produced by secrecy and revenge. Bosola’s mistake shows that in a world of hidden motives and concealed identities, even revenge cannot act accurately. Webster uses the dark setting to challenge the conventions of revenge tragedy. Instead of restoring order through violent justice, the final setting produces waste, accident and tragic absurdity.

The final stage space becomes crowded with dying bodies, belated recognitions and unstable moral statements. Ferdinand, the Cardinal and Bosola die in a setting that resembles revenge-tragedy closure, yet Webster refuses simple restoration. Delio’s closing attempt to make “noble use / Of this great ruin” explicitly turns the whole tragic world into a ruin from which a moral lesson must somehow be extracted. The noun “ruin” gathers together physical setting, political collapse and human loss. Webster’s final setting therefore does not erase disorder; it preserves it as the condition from which any moral judgement must begin.

Ultimately, Webster uses setting as a dramatic map of corruption and resistance. The court is diseased, the household is surveilled, the chamber is invaded, Rome is institutionally corrupt, the prison is theatricalised cruelty, the ruins speak of collapse, and the final darkness produces mistaken revenge. Yet settings also reveal virtue: the Duchess’s chamber allows chosen marriage, and the prison becomes the unlikely place of moral sovereignty. Webster’s tragedy is spatial as well as psychological. It shows that when power corrupts the places where people live, love, worship and die, setting itself becomes an agent of tragedy.

## Examiner Commentary

AO3 is embedded through Jacobean court politics, patronage, aristocratic marriage, patriarchal surveillance, religious institutions, revenge tragedy and early modern death culture.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Minor Characters

Webster presents minor characters as more than functional supports: they expose the social ecology of tragedy, revealing how court corruption, patronage, gendered hypocrisy, service and spectatorship surround and intensify the Duchess’s fall.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Setting

Webster makes setting an active dramatic language: court, chamber, prison, Rome, ruins and darkness all reveal how corrupted spaces produce surveillance, secrecy, psychological torment and unstable revenge-tragedy closure.

For minor characters, do not simply write one paragraph each on Cariola, Delio and Julia. Build an argument about function: witness, frame, satire, hypocrisy, patronage, medicine and machinery of violence.

For setting, do not list locations mechanically. Track how setting becomes active: the court contaminates, the chamber briefly protects, the household surveils, the prison theatricalises cruelty, ruins echo loss, and final darkness produces mistaken revenge.
