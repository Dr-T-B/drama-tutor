# Model Answer 1 — Suffering

## Question

Explore how Webster presents suffering in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents suffering in *The Duchess of Malfi* as both personal anguish and structural exposure. Characters suffer because they are trapped in a corrupt court, but their suffering also reveals the moral nature of that court. The Duchess's suffering is physical, psychological, social and maternal, yet Webster refuses to reduce her to passive victimhood. Her endurance becomes the play's strongest proof of moral identity. By contrast, Ferdinand, the Cardinal and Bosola suffer in ways that expose guilt, paranoia and belated recognition. Webster therefore makes suffering diagnostic: it reveals who possesses integrity and who has been hollowed out by power.

The play's opening suggests that suffering originates in corrupted authority. Antonio's fountain metaphor imagines a court where poison at "the head" causes "Death and diseases" to spread through the land. Suffering is not presented as random misfortune but as the consequence of diseased hierarchy. This is important in a Jacobean context where the court was understood as the centre of political order and patronage. Webster presents that centre as contaminated, so the suffering that follows is systemic. The Duchess's later torment is therefore not merely a private family tragedy; it is the symptom of a political and patriarchal structure.

The Duchess's first suffering is social constraint. Her line, "The misery of us that are born great — / We are forced to woo because none dare woo us," captures the paradox of aristocratic female identity. The noun "misery" is striking because greatness should suggest privilege, yet Webster presents rank as isolation. The Duchess suffers because status denies her ordinary courtship and because gender denies her sexual autonomy. Contextually, aristocratic widows could have property and some independence, but remarriage was closely connected to inheritance, lineage and family honour. Webster makes this pressure emotionally intelligible: the Duchess's agency is real, but it emerges from constraint.

Once the marriage is discovered, suffering becomes an instrument of patriarchal punishment. Ferdinand's language shows that the Duchess's pain is imagined before it is enacted. His wish that her and Antonio's bodies should be burned so their smoke cannot "ascend to heaven" pushes punishment beyond death into damnation. This is not justice; it is an excessive fantasy of control. The religious imagery reveals the brutality of a brother who seeks to dominate not only the Duchess's body but her spiritual meaning. In revenge-tragedy terms, Webster intensifies suffering by making punishment imaginative, theatrical and morally disproportionate.

Bosola's surveillance also produces suffering. He is employed "for your intelligence," and his "masterpiece of cunning" is to make the Duchess "reveal herself." The language of artistry makes betrayal seem like craft. The Duchess's pregnancy, marriage and body become evidence in a hostile court. Suffering here arises from being watched, interpreted and exposed. Webster's method is especially modern in its psychological force: the Duchess is not only physically endangered; her privacy and selfhood are invaded. In a patriarchal culture concerned with female chastity and reputation, exposure itself becomes punishment.

The fourth act is Webster's most concentrated presentation of orchestrated suffering. Ferdinand's "masque of madmen" transforms court entertainment into psychological assault. A masque should signify aristocratic harmony and order; Webster perverts it into spectacle designed to break the Duchess's mind. Ferdinand's phrase "my masterpiece" casts cruelty as art, making him a grotesque dramatist. The Duchess is forced into the role of audience to her own torment. Webster therefore presents suffering as staged, not accidental: power composes it, designs it and watches its effects.

Yet the Duchess's response alters the meaning of suffering. Her repeated claim, "I am not mad yet," framed by images of "molten brass" and "flaming sulphur," shows sanity under apocalyptic pressure. The syntax matters because the repeated negation contains the overwhelming imagery. Her mind holds form while the world around her becomes hellish. Webster draws here on stoic and Christian traditions of endurance, especially the idea that the manner of suffering can reveal spiritual condition. The Duchess's suffering does not erase her; it demonstrates the strength of her self-command.

The line "I am Duchess of Malfi still" is the clearest example of suffering transformed into resistance. The sentence is simple, declarative and almost bare, yet that simplicity is its force. The adverb "still" asserts continuity despite imprisonment, degradation and impending death. The court can strip away household, husband, liberty and bodily safety, but it cannot fully strip away identity. Webster therefore makes suffering a test of essence. The Duchess's status is no longer merely political; it becomes moral.

Webster also presents suffering through maternal tenderness. The Duchess's request that her little boy be given "some syrup for his cold" and that her daughter say prayers before sleep is devastating because it brings ordinary domestic care into the death scene. The detail of "syrup" is small, practical and intensely human. It shifts suffering away from grand revenge-tragedy spectacle and towards the loss of daily love. The audience is made to understand that the brothers' violence does not simply destroy honour or rank; it destroys family, care and childhood security.

Bosola's suffering is different because it is belated and guilty. After the Duchess dies, he recognises, "There's a great spirit gone!" The exclamation suggests that her suffering has finally educated him, but too late. His later line, "Other sins only speak; murder shrieks out," personifies murder as a crime that cannot remain hidden. Bosola's suffering is therefore the pain of recognition after complicity. Webster does not offer him easy redemption. His moral insight is real, but it cannot undo what he has helped to cause.

Ferdinand's suffering is pathological and self-consuming. His fractured line, "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young," suggests guilt, shock and psychological collapse. The short clauses dramatise a mind unable to hold a coherent response. His later lycanthropy externalises the predatory violence that had been present in his language all along. Webster thus makes Ferdinand suffer from the eruption of his own cruelty. The oppressor becomes victim of the disorder he has created, but this does not restore justice; it merely confirms the depth of corruption.

Ultimately, Webster presents suffering as the tragic medium through which truth appears. The Duchess's suffering reveals integrity, Bosola's reveals conscience, Ferdinand's reveals inward monstrosity, and the court's suffering reveals systemic decay. The play is bleak because suffering does not prevent further destruction; however, it is not meaningless. Webster makes the Duchess's endurance morally luminous against the cruelty around her. Her suffering becomes the measure by which the audience judges the whole world of Malfi.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it treats suffering as a structural and moral idea rather than a sequence of painful events. AO1 is sustained through the concept of suffering as diagnostic. AO2 is developed through close analysis of metaphor, syntax, masque, spectacle, declarative speech, domestic detail, personification and fractured clauses. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean court hierarchy, patriarchy, widowhood, revenge tragedy, masque culture, Christian stoicism and the good-death tradition.

# Model Answer 2 — The Final Act

## Question

Explore how Webster makes use of the play's final act in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster makes the final act of *The Duchess of Malfi* a bleak aftermath rather than a simple resolution. The Duchess has already died before Act 5, so the final act cannot function as a conventional climax centred on the heroine's death. Instead, it shows what remains after virtue has been destroyed: guilt, confusion, failed revenge, accidental death and uncertain attempts to extract meaning from ruin. Webster uses the final act to test whether revenge tragedy can restore order. His answer is deeply sceptical. The final act exposes a world in which recognition comes too late and justice is contaminated by error.

The final act first shows that the Duchess's death has not ended the play's moral action. Ferdinand's lycanthropy makes visible the inward corruption that had shaped his behaviour throughout. Pescara's report of Ferdinand howling in the night and carrying a human limb behind Saint Mark's Church is grotesque, but its force is moral as well as sensational. The setting near a church heightens the disorder: sacred space cannot contain the eruption of guilt and violence. The Doctor's diagnosis of "lycanthropia" gives the condition a medical name, but the clinical label cannot fully explain it. Webster uses the final act to show that evil returns upon the body and mind of the oppressor.

The Cardinal's role in the final act exposes institutional corruption collapsing inward. His admission that he has "grown to need / A soldier and a churchman" fuses spiritual and martial power. This is not merely a personal confession; it clarifies what his authority has always been. Religious office and violence are not opposites in his world but partners. In a Jacobean Protestant stage context, the corrupt Cardinal would evoke suspicion of Catholic political power, but Webster's critique is broader: institutions that should guide conscience have become instruments of secrecy, coercion and self-preservation.

The echo scene is one of Webster's most distinctive uses of the final act. Antonio's encounter with the echo of the Duchess's voice makes the dead continue to shape the stage. The line "Thou art a dead thing" is both warning and prophecy. Its ambiguity matters. It may be supernatural, acoustic, psychological or theatrical, but in every case it suggests that meaning arrives too late to save. Antonio stands among "ancient ruins," a setting that makes history and collapse physically present. Webster uses place symbolically: the ruined abbey becomes an external image of a ruined moral order.

Antonio's accidental death is central to the final act's tragic bleakness. His brief line, "Oh, I am gone! / Thou hast killed me," denies him heroic closure. He is killed by the wrong man, at the wrong time, in darkness and confusion. This is not the clean justice expected from a conventional revenge structure. It is waste. Webster uses Antonio's death to challenge the idea that final acts necessarily produce moral order. In this tragedy, the final act does not tidy meaning; it exposes the cost of a world governed by secrecy and misrecognition.

Bosola's response to Antonio's death deepens this structural critique. His fatalistic image that humans are "the stars' tennis-balls," struck which way the heavens please, reduces agency to cosmic sport. The metaphor is strikingly undignified: human suffering becomes a game played by impersonal powers. Contextually, this challenges any simple providential reading of revenge tragedy. If the stars control the action, then moral justice is uncertain. Webster makes the final act feel less like divine judgement than like a chaotic aftermath of human corruption.

The final act also converts Bosola into a compromised avenger. His revenge catalogue — for the Duchess, Antonio, Julia and himself — attempts to impose order by naming the dead. The repeated "for" gives the speech a formal structure, as though revenge can sort the chaos into moral categories. Yet his self-inclusion collapses the difference between victim, avenger and guilty agent. Bosola is avenging crimes in which he is implicated. Webster therefore uses the final act to test revenge tragedy from within: revenge may punish the corrupt, but it cannot purify the avenger.

Ferdinand's death produces a devastating structural echo of the Duchess. His cry, "My sister! O my sister!" and his repetition of the diamond image — "Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust" — return the Duchess's earlier wisdom through the mouth of her persecutor. This echo matters because it shows knowledge arriving too late. Ferdinand finally recognises the truth of suffering and self-destruction only after he has destroyed the person who understood it first. Webster's final act is therefore full of belated understanding. Characters learn, but not in time for learning to redeem action.

The Cardinal's end is even more spiritually hollow. His desire to be "laid by, and never thought of" contrasts sharply with the Duchess's earlier assertion, "I am Duchess of Malfi still." The Duchess insists on identity under pressure; the Cardinal asks for oblivion. This contrast gives the final act a moral architecture. The corrupt want disappearance; the virtuous remain memorable. Yet Webster complicates the closure because remembrance itself cannot reverse loss. The final act honours the Duchess by contrast, but it cannot restore her.

Bosola's metatheatrical recognition, "Such a mistake as I have often seen / In a play," is one of the final act's most important moments. It draws attention to the machinery of revenge tragedy: darkness, mistaken action, delayed knowledge and accumulating bodies. Webster makes the audience aware that they are watching a genre fulfil itself, but in doing so he also exposes the genre's inadequacy. The final bloodshed does not feel like satisfying justice. It feels like a theatrical mechanism producing ruin.

Delio's closing attempt to make "noble use / Of this great ruin" gives the play a fragile final gesture. The phrase is balanced but uneasy. "Noble use" suggests moral recovery, while "great ruin" carries heavier emotional force. His final couplet, "Integrity of life is fame's best friend," offers the Duchess as an enduring model of virtue. Yet the very need to state this suggests that meaning is not secure. Webster gives the final act a conventional moral ending while leaving the catastrophe's weight unresolved.

Ultimately, Webster uses the final act not to resolve the play neatly but to stage the aftermath of destroyed virtue. The Duchess's absence dominates the act. Ferdinand's madness, the Cardinal's collapse, Antonio's mistaken death, Bosola's compromised revenge and Delio's uncertain moralising all circle around what has been lost. The final act is therefore not an appendage after the main tragedy; it is the play's final judgement on revenge, power and memory. Webster shows that a corrupt world may punish itself, but punishment is not the same as restoration.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it treats the final act structurally rather than simply narrating Act 5 events. AO1 is sustained through a thesis about aftermath, belated recognition and failed restoration. AO2 is developed through analysis of reported action, setting, echo, brevity of dying speech, metaphor, repetition, structural echo, metatheatre and closing couplet. AO3 is integrated through revenge tragedy, Jacobean providential thinking, medical discourse, anti-Catholic stage convention, ruined religious spaces and tragic closure.

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Suffering

Webster uses the final act as an aftermath rather than a neat resolution: the Duchess's absence dominates Act 5, while madness, accident, compromised revenge and uncertain moral closure expose the limits of revenge tragedy.
