# Model Answer 1 — Heroism

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents heroism in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents heroism in *Hamlet* as a profoundly unstable category, shaped by the conflict between public action and private conscience. Rather than allowing heroism to mean simple bravery, military force or revengeful decisiveness, Shakespeare places Hamlet inside a revenge tragedy whose conventions require action, yet gives him a moral and intellectual consciousness that repeatedly questions the value of such action. The result is a tragic hero who is heroic not because he acts straightforwardly, but because he cannot act without interrogating the ethical, spiritual and political consequences of action. Through soliloquy, foil characters, antithesis, revenge-tragedy structure and the final movement towards providential acceptance, Shakespeare presents heroism as moral endurance under impossible pressure: Hamlet is heroic, compromised and anti-heroic at once.

At the beginning of the play, Hamlet’s potential heroism is defined by inheritance and obligation. He is a prince, a son and, after the Ghost’s command, a revenger. Yet Shakespeare immediately complicates these roles by presenting a court in which conventional public heroism has been replaced by political performance. Claudius speaks with polished ceremonial control, balancing grief and celebration through antithesis: “with mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage”. The phrase exposes the moral doubleness of the court while also showing Claudius’ political competence. He performs kingship effectively, but Shakespeare makes that effectiveness anti-heroic because it depends on concealment and moral corruption. In this context Hamlet’s first heroic quality is not military action but refusal: he will not accept the court’s false language. His “inky cloak” and insistence that he has “that within which passes show” present him as a figure of inward truth in a world of theatrical surfaces. For an early modern audience concerned with legitimate monarchy, succession and the sacred nature of kingship, Hamlet’s isolation would make his position politically dangerous but morally significant. He resists a diseased political order before he knows the full crime beneath it.

The Ghost’s demand transforms Hamlet from grieving prince into potential revenge hero, but Shakespeare presents this heroic role as spiritually dangerous. The Ghost’s imperative language — “Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder” — belongs to the revenge-tragedy tradition, where blood demands blood and a son’s honour is measured by retaliation. However, Shakespeare’s dramatic method unsettles this convention. The Ghost appears on the battlements at night, a liminal space associated with uncertainty, military threat and supernatural ambiguity. Hamlet’s heroic mission is therefore founded not on secure knowledge but on a potentially demonic apparition. His exclamation, “O cursed spite / That ever I was born to set it right”, reveals the burden of heroic election. The verb “set” imagines heroic action as restoration, yet the adjective “cursed” makes the task feel spiritually contaminated. Hamlet is not a confident revenger entering a clear moral universe; he is a Christian subject asked to commit murder in a world where the afterlife, conscience and damnation matter. Shakespeare therefore presents revenge heroism as both necessary and morally suspect.

Hamlet’s soliloquies make his heroism unusually inward. In “O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I”, Shakespeare uses self-accusation, rhetorical questioning and theatrical comparison to dramatise Hamlet’s struggle with heroic inadequacy. He compares himself unfavourably with the Player, whose fictional passion produces tears, while Hamlet, with a real murdered father, remains inactive. His question, “Am I a coward?”, reduces heroism to the public code of masculine action and honour, but the soliloquy itself proves that the issue is more complex. The repeated interrogatives enact a mind testing itself, and the movement from self-disgust to strategy — “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King” — reveals a different form of heroism: intellectual, theatrical and forensic. Hamlet does not simply fail to act; he uses drama as evidence. Some critics see this delay as paralysis or failure, but Shakespeare’s language allows an alternative interpretation: Hamlet’s hesitation is also a demand for moral certainty. In a play obsessed with seeming, acting and deception, such caution may be the only responsible heroic stance.

Shakespeare develops this debate through foil characters who embody alternative models of heroism. Fortinbras represents martial decisiveness and aristocratic honour. Hamlet’s encounter with Fortinbras’ army in Act 4 forces him to measure himself against conventional heroic action. The soldiers are willing to fight “even for an eggshell”, and Hamlet reflects that “Rightly to be great / Is not to stir without great argument, / But greatly to find quarrel in a straw / When honour’s at the stake.” The syntax is carefully balanced: Hamlet appears to admire Fortinbras’ honour culture, yet the triviality of “eggshell” and “straw” exposes its absurdity. Shakespeare therefore does not simply present Fortinbras as the better hero. Fortinbras is efficient and public, but his heroism may be morally shallow, founded on military spectacle and aristocratic pride. In Renaissance humanist terms, Hamlet’s “noble reason” is what distinguishes man from beast; yet the play asks whether excessive thought disables necessary action. Hamlet’s heroism exists at the point where humanist reflection collides with the revenge code.

Laertes offers a second foil: the impulsive revenge hero who acts instantly after his father’s death. His declaration that he would “dare damnation” presents a form of heroic extremity, but Shakespeare makes it morally reckless. The alliteration gives the phrase force, yet its theology is horrifying: Laertes is willing to sacrifice his soul for revenge. In early modern Christian culture, such willingness would not be admirable in any simple sense. By contrast, Hamlet’s reluctance to kill Claudius at prayer, though often criticised, shows that he thinks in spiritual categories. His reasoning is morally compromised because he wants Claudius’ soul damned, yet it also reveals that revenge cannot be separated from eschatology. Shakespeare uses Laertes to expose the danger of defining heroism as immediacy. Laertes’ speed makes him manipulable; Claudius converts his grief into a plot. Hamlet’s delay is destructive, but Laertes’ action is equally corrupted. Neither man can occupy the revenge-hero role without moral damage.

Horatio provides a quieter model of heroism based on stoicism, loyalty and witness. He is not a revenger, soldier or ruler, yet his constancy gives the play a moral centre. Hamlet praises him as one “whose blood and judgment are so well commingled”, a phrase that values emotional control and rational balance. In a play where Claudius performs politics, Laertes performs fury and Hamlet performs madness, Horatio’s heroism lies in ethical steadiness. His attempt to drink from the poisoned cup at the end might appear traditionally heroic as an act of loyal self-sacrifice, but Hamlet stops him because Horatio’s true heroic function is survival and testimony: “Absent thee from felicity awhile… / To tell my story.” Shakespeare therefore shifts heroism from action to narration. In tragedy, the hero dies, but the witness preserves meaning. Horatio’s survival ensures that Hamlet’s compromised life is not reduced to scandal or political chaos.

Hamlet’s own heroism reaches its most tragic form when he moves from self-division towards providential acceptance. After his return from England, his language changes. He no longer speaks with the same frantic self-laceration; instead, he says, “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” and later, “The readiness is all.” These statements do not make Hamlet passive. Rather, Shakespeare presents a hero who has accepted the limits of human control. In a Christian providential framework, heroic greatness may lie not in mastering events but in readiness for death and judgement. The phrase “The readiness is all” is stark, monosyllabic and rhythmically final, stripping heroism of theatrical flourish. Hamlet’s acceptance contrasts with earlier revenge rhetoric. He does not enter the final duel as a triumphant avenger but as a man who has learned that death cannot be outplanned. This makes his final heroism tragic rather than conventional.

Nevertheless, Shakespeare refuses to make Hamlet purely heroic. His cruelty to Ophelia, killing of Polonius and callous dispatch of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern complicate any celebratory reading. His language can be misogynistic, violent and theatrically excessive. A critical reading of Hamlet as anti-hero is therefore persuasive: his inwardness does not prevent collateral damage, and his moral intelligence does not save others from suffering. Yet this compromised quality is central to Shakespeare’s tragic design. Hamlet is not a saint but a tragic protagonist whose greatness is inseparable from error. Aristotelian ideas of tragic flaw can be applied, but Shakespeare’s play resists reducing Hamlet to a single hamartia such as indecision. His flaw is bound up with his virtue: he thinks too deeply for the revenge role he has inherited, but that thinking is also what gives him moral stature.

The final scene fuses heroic action with tragic waste. Hamlet kills Claudius only after Gertrude, Laertes and he himself are poisoned; revenge comes too late to restore the state. Yet his final acts are not meaningless. He forces Claudius to drink from the poisoned cup, exposes the hidden corruption of the court and gives his “dying voice” to Fortinbras, attempting to secure political continuity. Fortinbras’ closing command that Hamlet receive a soldier’s funeral — “Bear Hamlet like a soldier to the stage” — imposes a conventional martial heroic frame on him. However, the audience knows that this is only partly adequate. Hamlet has not been heroic chiefly as a soldier. His heroism has been intellectual, moral and theatrical: he has exposed corruption, endured uncertainty and confronted death. The phrase “to the stage” also reminds us that heroism in *Hamlet* is always performed and interpreted. Shakespeare ends not by solving the question of Hamlet’s heroism but by making the audience judge its meaning.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents heroism in *Hamlet* as tragic because no available heroic code is sufficient. Martial honour risks emptiness, revenge risks damnation, political effectiveness risks corruption, and moral thought risks paralysis. Hamlet’s heroism lies in his refusal to inhabit any of these roles simply. He is a prince who distrusts power, a revenger who questions revenge, a thinker forced into violence and a dying man who achieves readiness rather than victory. Shakespeare’s presentation of heroism is therefore neither celebratory nor dismissive. Hamlet becomes heroic precisely because his conscience makes heroism difficult.

## Examiner Commentary

### AO1

This is a sustained, conceptual argument that avoids reducing heroism to bravery. It maintains a clear line of interpretation: Shakespeare presents heroism as morally unstable and tragic. The answer uses precise literary terminology, including soliloquy, foil, antithesis, revenge tragedy, providence and tragic structure. Expression is fluent and evaluative throughout.

### AO2

AO2 is central. The answer analyses Shakespeare’s dramatic methods, including the Ghost’s staging, Hamlet’s soliloquies, rhetorical questioning, foil characters, imagery of honour, syntax, blank verse movement and the final theatrical framing of Hamlet as a soldier. It avoids quotation-dropping by linking language and dramatic method to conceptual meaning.

### AO3

Context is integrated rather than bolted on. The answer uses revenge tragedy, early modern honour culture, Christian conscience, monarchy, succession, Renaissance humanism and providential belief to explain why Hamlet’s heroic role is conflicted. Context is used to sharpen interpretation, not as background information.

### AO5

The answer offers a strong debate: Hamlet may be read as flawed tragic hero, morally intelligent hero, failed revenger, anti-hero and modern inward hero. Fortinbras, Laertes and Horatio are used to test competing definitions of heroism. The answer sustains ambiguity rather than forcing a simplistic judgement.

# Model Answer 2 — Settings

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare makes use of settings in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare makes use of settings in *Hamlet* not merely as physical locations but as moral, political and psychological architecture. Elsinore, the battlements, the court, the closet, the playhouse space and the graveyard all externalise the diseased condition of Denmark and the inward crisis of Hamlet himself. Settings in the play are rarely neutral: they expose surveillance, corruption, uncertainty, mortality and theatricality. Through spatial contrast, public and private staging, liminal scenes, props, entrances and exits, and repeated imagery of disease and decay, Shakespeare turns place into dramatic meaning. The tragedy moves from the haunted margins of the state to the enclosed court, then to the democratic openness of the graveyard, before returning to a courtly duel space that becomes a theatrical trap. Setting therefore shapes the play’s whole tragic structure.

The opening battlements establish setting as a space of political anxiety and supernatural uncertainty. Shakespeare begins not inside the royal court but on the exposed edges of Elsinore at night. The guards’ fear, the darkness and the repeated questioning — “Who’s there?” — create a liminal atmosphere in which national security and metaphysical doubt overlap. The battlements are a military setting, appropriate to the threat from Fortinbras, but they are also a threshold between life and death when the Ghost appears. For an early modern audience shaped by Protestant suspicion of ghosts but still aware of Catholic ideas of purgatory, this setting would make the apparition deeply ambiguous. Is the Ghost a truthful spirit, a demonic temptation or a sign that Denmark’s political order has been disturbed? Shakespeare makes the physical edge of the castle a symbolic edge between visible and invisible worlds. Setting therefore initiates the play’s central uncertainty.

Elsinore itself functions as a diseased court and political prison. Marcellus’ statement that “Something is rotten in the state of Denmark” converts the kingdom into a decaying body politic. The setting is not simply a castle; it is a contaminated organism. Shakespeare’s disease imagery makes place moral: the corruption of Claudius’ murder spreads through the state’s political and familial structures. Hamlet later says, “Denmark’s a prison”, and although Rosencrantz challenges this as subjective, the line reveals how Shakespeare makes setting psychological. Elsinore is both objectively corrupt and subjectively imprisoning. Its walls enclose Hamlet within surveillance, memory and obligation. A critical reading of Elsinore as a carceral space is persuasive because so many scenes involve watching, overhearing and managing appearances. The court is a place where privacy collapses.

Shakespeare makes particular use of court interiors as spaces of performance and surveillance. Claudius’ court is ceremonial, polished and public, but beneath its formal language lies concealment. The setting demands performance: Claudius performs legitimate monarchy, Gertrude performs stability, Polonius performs counsel, and Hamlet refuses to perform grief in the approved manner. This is why his “inky cloak” matters dramatically. In a court setting obsessed with surfaces, costume becomes an extension of place. The court tries to convert grief into spectacle and obedience, but Hamlet insists on inwardness: “I have that within which passes show.” Early modern court culture depended on counsel, patronage and visible hierarchy, and Shakespeare exploits this context to make Elsinore a place where political survival requires theatrical self-management. Setting becomes a system of behaviour.

The repeated use of hidden listeners develops this courtly setting into an architecture of surveillance. Polonius sends Reynaldo to spy on Laertes; Claudius and Polonius watch Hamlet and Ophelia; Polonius hides behind the arras in Gertrude’s closet. Shakespeare’s staging makes the audience repeatedly aware of bodies concealed within rooms. This gives the setting a double structure: visible conversation and hidden observation. The arras is especially important as a prop because it turns the domestic interior into a dangerous political space. When Hamlet kills Polonius, believing him to be Claudius, the private chamber becomes a fatal extension of court espionage. Settings in *Hamlet* do not simply contain action; they produce action by encouraging deception and misrecognition.

Gertrude’s closet is one of the play’s most charged private settings. It appears to offer intimacy, but Shakespeare makes it morally and psychologically violent. Hamlet’s command, “Look here upon this picture, and on this”, uses visual props or imagined portraits to turn the room into a site of moral comparison. The closet setting enables a confrontation that could not happen in the public court: Hamlet attacks Gertrude’s sexuality, marriage and conscience while the Ghost’s reappearance intensifies the uncertainty between supernatural truth and psychological projection. The setting exposes the collision between family, monarchy and sexuality. In an early modern patriarchal context, a queen’s remarriage was never merely private; it had dynastic and political consequences. Shakespeare uses the closet to show that the personal chamber is also a state chamber. Private space cannot escape public corruption.

The Mousetrap scene shows Shakespeare making use of a temporary playhouse setting inside Elsinore. Hamlet turns the court into an audience and the stage into a trap: “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King.” This is setting as metatheatre. The court watches a performance, but the real drama is Claudius watching himself being watched. Shakespeare uses this layered theatrical space to explore truth and performance. The play-within-the-play briefly reverses Elsinore’s surveillance system: Hamlet becomes director and observer, while Claudius becomes the exposed spectator. For audiences in the early modern theatre, this scene would intensify awareness of theatricality itself; the actual audience watches an audience watching a play. Setting therefore becomes epistemological: it is the means by which Hamlet tests truth in a world of appearances.

England, although largely off-stage, is also an important setting because Shakespeare uses it as a political elsewhere associated with diplomacy, execution and state power. Claudius sends Hamlet to England not as exile but as a concealed death sentence. Because England remains off-stage, it functions as a threatening extension of Claudius’ reach. The audience does not need to see England for it to matter; its distance makes it a space of political manipulation. Hamlet’s return from the voyage marks a structural shift in the play. Having survived the attempted execution and rewritten the commission, he comes back changed, speaking more of providence than theatrical strategy. England therefore functions as an unseen setting of transformation, where Hamlet moves from reactive delay towards acceptance of mortality.

The graveyard is perhaps the most radical setting in the play because it shifts the tragedy into a comic, democratic and philosophical space. The gravediggers’ songs and riddling prose disrupt courtly decorum. Unlike Elsinore, where rank is obsessively performed, the graveyard levels human beings materially. Skulls erase hierarchy. Hamlet’s meditation on Yorick — “Alas, poor Yorick!” — makes setting and prop inseparable: the skull becomes a memento mori object through which Hamlet confronts the physical reality of death. The graveyard setting belongs to a long memento mori tradition, reminding audiences that worldly status ends in bodily decay. Yet Shakespeare also uses comedy here, making death ordinary rather than sublime. The setting forces Hamlet to move beyond theatrical imaginings of death into tactile contact with mortality.

The graveyard also changes Hamlet’s relation to heroism, love and identity. When Ophelia’s funeral enters the space, private grief becomes public contest. Hamlet and Laertes compete in exaggerated declarations of love, and the grave becomes a stage for masculine rivalry. Shakespeare therefore refuses to make the graveyard purely philosophical. It is both levelling and theatrical. The setting exposes how even death can be absorbed into performance. However, it also prepares Hamlet for the final scene. After handling Yorick’s skull and recognising that even Alexander may return to dust, Hamlet is more ready to accept the limits of human control. The graveyard is thus a structural pivot from uncertainty to readiness.

The final duel returns the play to the court, but Shakespeare transforms the courtly setting into a lethal theatrical trap. The duel appears to be a formal aristocratic entertainment, governed by ceremony, weapons and public spectatorship. In reality, it is a poisoned performance arranged by Claudius and Laertes. Props — the foils, the poisoned cup, the pearl — become instruments of hidden murder. Shakespeare’s use of setting here gathers the play’s earlier concerns: courtly performance, surveillance, deception, corrupted ritual and death. The public space that should confirm order instead reveals disorder. Gertrude’s drinking from the cup, Laertes’ exposure of the plot and Hamlet’s killing of Claudius all happen in a setting where appearance and reality finally collapse into one another.

Critics often read Elsinore as a projection of Hamlet’s mind, and this is persuasive to the extent that the castle’s confinement, decay and surveillance mirror his psychological condition. However, Shakespeare’s settings are not only subjective. They are also political structures. Elsinore is diseased before Hamlet describes it as a prison; Claudius’ murder has objectively corrupted the state. A more complete interpretation is that Shakespeare makes setting both inward and outward: Denmark is a political body, a theatrical stage and a mental prison simultaneously. This multiplicity is what gives the play its dramatic density.

Ultimately, Shakespeare makes settings in *Hamlet* active agents of tragedy. The battlements produce supernatural doubt; the court produces performance and surveillance; the closet produces moral confrontation; the Mousetrap produces theatrical exposure; England produces off-stage political threat; the graveyard produces philosophical reckoning; and the duel space produces fatal revelation. Shakespeare’s use of setting is therefore architectural rather than decorative. Each space shapes what can be known, said, hidden or performed. By the end of the play, Elsinore has become a theatre of death in which the corruption first sensed on the battlements is publicly exposed. Settings make the tragedy visible.

## AO5 interpretation bank

### Heroism

- **Flawed tragic hero:** Hamlet has moral and intellectual greatness, but his delay, cruelty and misjudgement produce suffering.
- **Morally intelligent hero:** Hamlet’s hesitation is not cowardice but ethical resistance to revenge-tragedy brutality.
- **Failed revenger:** Hamlet fails the conventional revenge code because he delays until the court is already destroyed.
- **Anti-hero:** Hamlet’s self-consciousness, theatricality and cruelty complicate heroic admiration.
- **Modern inward hero:** Hamlet’s heroism lies in consciousness, doubt and existential honesty rather than martial action.
- **Fortinbras as contrast:** Fortinbras represents conventional military heroism, but Shakespeare may expose its emptiness.
- **Horatio as stoic hero:** Horatio offers a quieter heroism of loyalty, judgement and witness.

### Settings

- **Elsinore as prison:** The castle confines Hamlet psychologically and politically.
- **Elsinore as diseased body politic:** Corrupt monarchy infects the whole state.
- **Settings as projections of mind:** Spaces externalise Hamlet’s grief, suspicion and mortality.
- **Settings as surveillance structures:** Rooms, arrases and court spaces enable spying and manipulation.
- **The graveyard as levelling space:** Death reduces rank, ambition and identity to matter.
- **The court as theatrical stage:** Political life becomes performance, role-play and concealment.
- **The Mousetrap as truth-space:** A staged fiction reveals political reality.
