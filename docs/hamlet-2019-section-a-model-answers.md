# Model Answer 1 — Deception

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents the theme of deception in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents deception in *Hamlet* not merely as a repeated action but as the governing condition of Elsinore. The tragedy begins in a world where appearances have already replaced truth: a murderer can perform kingship, a court can perform loyalty, and grief itself is policed as theatrical excess. Across the play, deception becomes both a symptom of political corruption and a method of survival within it. Claudius’s hidden murder generates a culture of surveillance and performance, but Hamlet’s attempt to expose that deception requires him to become a theatrical deceiver himself. Shakespeare therefore refuses a simple moral division between truth and falsehood. In a revenge tragedy that should promise certainty, deception makes truth necessary, dangerous and unstable.

From the opening scenes, Shakespeare links deception to political performance. Claudius’s first public speech presents him as a skilled theatrical ruler who converts moral disorder into ceremonial fluency. His balanced antitheses — “with mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage” — compress grief and celebration into a smooth rhetorical formula. The syntax appears controlled, but its control is morally suspect: Claudius uses public language to disguise the unnatural speed of his marriage and the violence behind his accession. In early modern political culture, the body of the king was meant to embody legitimacy and order, but Shakespeare presents a monarch whose authority is founded upon concealment. Claudius’s royal performance is therefore Machiavellian: he understands that power depends not only on action but on the management of appearances. His deception is not private hypocrisy alone; it infects the state because the court’s public language is made to conceal private crime.

Hamlet’s response to this world is shaped by his insistence on the difference between seeming and being. His reply to Gertrude — “Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not ‘seems’” — establishes him as a character hostile to theatrical falsity. The monosyllabic correction of “seems” into “is” creates a sharp antithesis between appearance and inward truth, while the repetition of “seems” exposes Hamlet’s disgust at a court that treats mourning as costume. Yet Shakespeare immediately complicates Hamlet’s position because this enemy of performance soon chooses performance as his own defensive strategy. When he announces that he may “put an antic disposition on”, deception becomes tactical. The verb “put” suggests costume, role and deliberate theatrical assumption. Hamlet is not simply mad; he stages madness as a dramatic instrument. This makes him a truth-seeker, but also a character whose methods begin to resemble the disguising world he condemns.

The Ghost intensifies the play’s uncertainty about deception because it appears to reveal the hidden truth while also raising the possibility of spiritual danger. Its image of fratricidal murder — “The serpent that did sting thy father’s life / Now wears his crown” — fuses biblical allusion, political usurpation and bodily corruption. The serpent recalls Edenic temptation, making Claudius’s deception seem primal and satanic. Yet early modern audiences would also have recognised competing Protestant and Catholic anxieties about ghosts, purgatory and demonic illusion. If the Ghost is a moral authority, it exposes deception; if it is a demonic temptation, it may itself be a deceptive theatrical apparition. Shakespeare uses this ambiguity to destabilise revenge-tragedy certainty. The genre conventionally gives the revenger a command, but *Hamlet* makes the command epistemologically unstable: before Hamlet can act, he must determine whether revelation is itself another form of deception.

Polonius embodies the normalisation of deception as court practice. His language repeatedly turns human relationships into objects of strategy. He sends Reynaldo to spy on Laertes by using “indirections” to “find directions out”, a phrase whose verbal paradox makes deception sound like administrative method. The court does not merely hide truth; it produces truth through manipulation. Polonius’s spying on Hamlet and Ophelia extends this surveillance culture into intimate life. Ophelia is positioned as an instrument through which male authority can observe and decode Hamlet. This is especially significant in a patriarchal context where daughters were expected to obey fathers and protect family honour. Shakespeare stages Ophelia’s obedience not as simple weakness but as evidence of how deception is structurally enforced. Her visible presence becomes a trap designed by others, so deception is not only verbal but spatial and theatrical: characters are placed, watched and interpreted.

Rosencrantz and Guildenstern deepen this pattern because they are presented as instruments of a manipulative court. Claudius and Gertrude summon them under the language of friendship, but their function is investigative. Hamlet quickly detects the falsity of their performance, asking whether they were “sent for”. The repeated pressure of his questioning turns ordinary social exchange into interrogation. Shakespeare uses prose here to create a flexible, evasive texture: friendship becomes conversational performance rather than genuine intimacy. Their betrayal matters because it shows that deception in Elsinore colonises even old companionship. Hamlet’s later treatment of them can be read as just punishment for political espionage, but Shakespeare also invites a harsher interpretation: Hamlet becomes increasingly willing to manipulate, rewrite and dispose of others within the same deceptive political logic that has trapped him.

The “Mousetrap” is Shakespeare’s most sophisticated exploration of counter-deception. Hamlet uses theatre to expose political theatre: “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King.” The rhyming couplet gives his plan a decisive theatrical neatness, while “catch” suggests both detection and entrapment. Metatheatre becomes a moral instrument, allowing Hamlet to test whether performance can reveal truth rather than conceal it. This is one of the play’s central paradoxes: because Elsinore is deceptive, direct accusation is impossible, so Hamlet must use staged illusion to produce authentic reaction. Claudius’s interruption of the play confirms his guilt dramatically rather than legally. Shakespeare therefore suggests that theatrical representation can reach truths unavailable to public speech. Yet the scene also keeps deception morally ambiguous: Hamlet’s discovery depends on manipulation, spectacle and psychological pressure.

Claudius’s prayer scene exposes the gap between performed authority and inward guilt. Alone, he admits “O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven”, and the olfactory image of corruption strips away the polished public rhetoric of Act 1. The verb “smells” makes sin physically unavoidable; deception can hide crime from the court but not from conscience or heaven. In a Christian context, the scene invokes repentance, judgement and the state of the soul. However, Claudius cannot truly repent because he retains “my crown, mine own ambition, and my queen”. The possessive triad reveals that his guilt is intellectually recognised but morally unresolved. Shakespeare’s dramatic irony is acute: Hamlet assumes Claudius is fit for salvation at the very moment when the audience hears the emptiness of his prayer. Deception here operates not only between characters but within the self. Claudius knows the truth, but he cannot transform knowledge into moral action.

Gertrude’s role in the play’s deception remains deliberately ambiguous. She may be innocent of the murder, complicit in the corrupt marriage, or engaged in self-protective denial. Hamlet’s accusation in the closet scene forces her to confront a truth she has avoided: “Thou turn’st mine eyes into my very soul.” The image of inward vision suggests that deception has become a failure of self-knowledge. Yet Shakespeare gives Gertrude no full soliloquy, so her interiority remains partially withheld. Some interpretations therefore view her as morally weak and sexually compromised; others see her as a politically pragmatic woman whose survival depends on accommodation within a patriarchal court. Shakespeare sustains this ambiguity by making Gertrude emotionally responsive but epistemologically opaque. She is central to the play’s concern with appearance and truth precisely because the audience cannot fully know what she knows.

The tragedy’s final movement shows deception collapsing into open violence. The poisoned cup and poisoned rapier turn courtly ceremony into murder disguised as sport. Claudius’s last plot is theatrically arranged as a fencing match, suggesting that deception has become the normal language of monarchy. Laertes participates in the hidden plot, but his confession before death helps restore moral visibility: “The King, the King’s to blame.” The repetition has a stark clarity absent from the evasive language of the court. Hamlet’s late acceptance that “the readiness is all” also marks a shift away from manipulative control towards providential submission. After a play dominated by surveillance, staging and counter-staging, Hamlet no longer tries to master appearances. Yet the cost of truth is catastrophic. Elsinore can only be purged when its deceptions are exposed through death.

Shakespeare therefore presents deception as the tragic atmosphere of *Hamlet*. Claudius’s original concealment creates a diseased political world in which spying, performance and self-deception become habitual. Hamlet’s tragedy is that he must use deceptive theatricality to discover truth, and in doing so he becomes implicated in the methods he opposes. The play can be read as a revenge tragedy about a moral truth-seeker in a corrupt court, but also as a drama that distrusts the revenger’s certainty and exposes the ethical danger of performance. Deception in *Hamlet* is not merely lying; it is the collapse of stable distinctions between seeming and being, theatre and truth, conscience and action.

# Model Answer 2 — Hamlet and Gertrude

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents the relationship between Hamlet and Gertrude in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents Hamlet and Gertrude’s relationship as the emotional and moral pressure-point of the tragedy. Hamlet’s grief for his father becomes inseparable from disgust at his mother’s remarriage, while Gertrude remains deliberately ambiguous: mother, queen, possible sinner, political survivor and victim of male interpretation. Their relationship is therefore not simply domestic. It connects private grief to dynastic instability, sexuality to legitimacy, and filial love to moral violence. Shakespeare makes their bond central to Hamlet’s disillusionment because Gertrude’s remarriage seems to him to prove that love, memory and female constancy are all forms of “seeming”. Yet the play also questions Hamlet’s judgement, exposing the misogyny and psychological excess through which he interprets his mother.

Hamlet’s first soliloquy establishes Gertrude as the focus of his moral disgust before he even knows of Claudius’s murder. His wish that “this too too solid flesh would melt” presents grief as a bodily crisis, but the soliloquy quickly turns from self-dissolution to female accusation: “Frailty, thy name is woman.” The abstract noun “Frailty” becomes personified as womanhood itself, showing how Hamlet generalises from Gertrude to all women. The line is rhetorically powerful but ethically unstable. It reveals Hamlet’s pain, but it also exposes his misogynistic tendency to convert personal betrayal into universal judgement. In an early modern patriarchal culture that prized female chastity and obedience, Gertrude’s remarriage would have been morally and socially troubling; however, Shakespeare makes Hamlet’s response excessive, not merely principled. His language turns his mother’s sexuality into a symbol of cosmic corruption.

Gertrude’s marriage to Claudius also carries political significance. As queen, her remarriage helps stabilise Claudius’s accession and gives his kingship a public appearance of continuity. Claudius’s court speech attempts to absorb her into ceremonial order, presenting the marriage as a legitimate union after mourning. Hamlet, however, interprets the marriage as moral disorder: “O most wicked speed, to post / With such dexterity to incestuous sheets.” The compressed movement from “speed” to “post” and “dexterity” suggests indecent haste, while “incestuous sheets” fuses sexuality with contamination. Although the marriage is not incestuous in the modern biological sense, early modern Christian teaching often treated marriage to a brother’s widow as spiritually and legally problematic. Shakespeare therefore makes Gertrude’s relationship with Claudius disturb both private memory and public legitimacy. To Hamlet, she has not merely remarried; she has helped make corruption look lawful.

Yet Shakespeare does not allow the audience to see Gertrude only through Hamlet’s disgust. Her emotional opacity is one of the play’s most important dramatic effects. She speaks relatively little compared with Hamlet, Claudius or Polonius, and she is not given a soliloquy that would fully disclose her conscience. This absence creates interpretive uncertainty. Gertrude may be morally weak, erotically dependent and evasive; equally, she may be politically pragmatic in a dangerous court where female power is mediated through marriage. Her early request that Hamlet “cast thy nighted colour off” can sound emotionally shallow, but it can also be read as an attempt to restore social order and protect him from public isolation. Shakespeare uses her limited speech to make her difficult to judge. She is surrounded by men who interpret her, command her or accuse her, but she is rarely allowed to define herself.

The closet scene is the dramatic centre of Hamlet and Gertrude’s relationship because it transforms suppressed disgust into direct confrontation. Shakespeare stages the scene in a private chamber, but the privacy is contaminated from the start by Polonius’s hidden presence. The relationship between mother and son is therefore placed inside the wider court culture of surveillance. Hamlet enters with imperatives, accusations and visual contrasts: “Look here upon this picture, and on this.” The deictic repetition of “this” makes the scene intensely theatrical, as if Hamlet is forcing Gertrude to read a staged moral emblem. Old Hamlet is idealised through hyperbolic imagery, while Claudius is degraded into corruption. The visual method matters: Hamlet does not simply argue; he compels Gertrude to see. The scene becomes an attempted moral re-education through theatrical display.

Gertrude’s response suggests that Hamlet’s confrontation has some inward effect: “O Hamlet, thou hast cleft my heart in twain.” The violent verb “cleft” implies that Hamlet’s language has penetrated her conscience, dividing her inward self. This line supports a reading of the closet scene as moral purgation, in which the son forces the mother to recognise sin. However, Shakespeare also makes the scene disturbing because Hamlet’s moral purpose is entangled with verbal aggression and psychological domination. His command that she should not go to Claudius’s bed again turns filial correction into regulation of female sexuality. A feminist or sceptical reading might see the scene less as righteous exposure than as emotional violence: Gertrude becomes the object upon which Hamlet projects grief, disgust and misogyny. Shakespeare refuses to settle the matter because the scene is both morally charged and theatrically excessive.

The Ghost’s intervention in the closet scene further complicates the relationship. Its instruction to Hamlet to “step between her and her fighting soul” suggests that Gertrude is spiritually vulnerable rather than simply corrupt. The phrase implies inner conflict, not complete guilt. Crucially, Gertrude cannot see the Ghost, so Shakespeare creates a staging ambiguity: Hamlet may be receiving supernatural correction, or Gertrude may be witnessing a breakdown that confirms his madness. Her response — “Alas, he’s mad” — sharply changes the scene’s interpretive frame. For Hamlet, the Ghost validates his mission; for Gertrude, it exposes his psychological crisis. This split perspective makes the relationship tragic because mother and son no longer inhabit the same reality. Their bond is fractured not only by remarriage and accusation but by incompatible perceptions of truth.

Gertrude’s role after the closet scene remains ambiguous but not insignificant. When she reports Hamlet’s killing of Polonius, she describes him as “Mad as the sea and wind”. This simile may protect Hamlet by framing the killing as madness rather than treasonous intention; it may also protect herself by simplifying a morally terrifying encounter. Shakespeare gives Gertrude language that can be read in two directions. She partly obeys Hamlet by not revealing every detail of their confrontation, yet she remains inside Claudius’s political structure. Her position is constrained: as queen and wife, she cannot simply detach herself from the court. This reflects the gendered limits of female agency in the world of the play. Gertrude has influence, but her influence is precarious and mediated through men.

In the final scene, Gertrude’s death gives her relationship with Hamlet its last tragic significance. Her drinking from the poisoned cup can be staged as accident, defiance, maternal instinct or belated resistance to Claudius. When she says “I will, my lord; I pray you pardon me” before drinking, the courteous phrase may seem minor, but in performance it can carry a flicker of independence. She acts against Claudius’s warning, whether knowingly or unknowingly. Her final cry — “The drink, the drink! I am poisoned” — exposes the murderous deception of the court and helps reveal Claudius’s guilt. If Gertrude has been morally opaque throughout the play, her death becomes a final moment of tragic disclosure. She cannot fully explain herself, but her body becomes evidence of the corruption around her.

Shakespeare therefore presents Hamlet and Gertrude’s relationship as central to the play’s tragic structure. Hamlet’s disgust at Gertrude’s remarriage intensifies his sense that the world is morally diseased, but his judgement is also compromised by misogyny, grief and theatrical excess. Gertrude, meanwhile, is never reduced to a single moral category. She may be weak, pragmatic, self-deceiving, innocent of murder, guilty of sexual and emotional betrayal, or victimised by patriarchal interpretation. The relationship matters because it turns political corruption into intimate anguish. Through Hamlet and Gertrude, Shakespeare shows that tragedy does not only destroy kingdoms; it deforms the language of love, memory and kinship.
