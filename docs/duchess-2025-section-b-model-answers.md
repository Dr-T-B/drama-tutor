# Model Answer 1 — Cruelty and Violence

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of cruelty and violence in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents cruelty and violence in *The Duchess of Malfi* as both physical and theatrical. The play's violence is not limited to death or bodily harm; it includes surveillance, psychological manipulation, verbal degradation, staged spectacle and the systematic destruction of privacy. Webster's most disturbing insight is that cruelty in Malfi often appears as performance. Ferdinand designs suffering as if it were courtly entertainment, the Cardinal hides cruelty behind institutional authority, and Bosola turns service into a form of violation before recognising its moral cost. The Duchess's tragedy therefore exposes a court where violence has become habitual, aestheticised and structurally protected.

The roots of cruelty are planted in the court's corrupt political order. Antonio's opening image of a prince's court as a "common fountain" that can be poisoned "near the head" suggests that violence spreads from authority itself. The metaphor frames later cruelty as systemic rather than exceptional. In a Jacobean court culture shaped by patronage, hierarchy and dependence on powerful nobles, Webster presents violence as something enabled by rank. Those at the top do not merely commit cruelty; they create the conditions in which others must serve it.

Ferdinand's cruelty begins in language before it becomes theatrical action. His obscene comparison of female sexuality to the boneless "lamprey" reduces the Duchess to a body governed by appetite. This language is already a form of violence because it strips her of dignity before any punishment takes place. The misogynistic imagery reveals that Ferdinand's supposed concern for family honour is contaminated by sexual disgust and possessiveness. In a context where female chastity and aristocratic bloodline carried enormous social significance, Webster exposes how easily patriarchal anxiety turns into cruelty.

Ferdinand's imagination repeatedly exceeds the language of justice. His fantasy that the Duchess and Antonio should be burned so that their smoke cannot "ascend to heaven" imagines punishment beyond bodily death. The hellish imagery makes his violence spiritually invasive: he wants control not only over bodies but over afterlife and meaning. This is a defining feature of Webster's revenge-tragedy world. Violence no longer restores order; it becomes excessive, self-feeding and blasphemously ambitious. Ferdinand tries to occupy the position of divine judge, and the extremity of that fantasy marks the moral disorder of the court.

Webster also presents cruelty through surveillance. Ferdinand employs Bosola "for your intelligence," turning the Duchess's household into a monitored space. Bosola's later phrase, "This was my masterpiece of cunning — / To reveal herself," aestheticises espionage. The word "masterpiece" is chilling because it turns betrayal into art. The Duchess's body becomes something to be interpreted, trapped and exposed. This is a form of gendered violence: the court's power enters her private life and makes maternity into evidence. Webster therefore shows that cruelty can operate through watching before it operates through striking.

The fourth act makes cruelty explicitly theatrical. Ferdinand's plan to send a "masque of madmen" perverts a courtly entertainment form into an instrument of mental assault. A masque traditionally presented aristocratic order, ceremony and spectacle; Webster converts it into anti-order. Ferdinand even calls it his "masterpiece," casting himself as dramatist and the Duchess as unwilling audience. The dramatic method is crucial. Webster stages cruelty as a performance within a performance, forcing the theatre audience to confront its own appetite for spectacle. Violence becomes not only an action but an aesthetic event.

The hand-and-ring episode is another example of cruelty through stagecraft. Ferdinand presents what appears to be Antonio's hand, with the ring the Duchess gave him. The cruelty lies in the corruption of intimacy. A wedding ring should symbolise union; Ferdinand turns it into a false sign of loss. The darkness of the scene enables his control over perception. He does not simply deceive the Duchess; he designs a sensory world in which she must suffer. Webster's violence here is psychological, symbolic and theatrical at once.

Bosola's rhetoric in the execution scene shows how cruelty can be intellectualised. His grotesque body imagery — describing flesh as decay, "worm-seed" and perishable matter — attempts to reduce the Duchess's living identity to decomposition. This draws on memento mori traditions, but Webster makes the language coercive rather than spiritually instructive. Bosola is not helping the Duchess prepare for death; he is trying to impose a degrading interpretation of her body. The Duchess's resistance, especially "I am Duchess of Malfi still," shows that cruelty can injure the body without fully controlling meaning.

The Duchess's death exposes the court's cruelty most clearly because her dignity contrasts so sharply with the violence around her. Bosola's statement that "Strangling is a very quiet death" is chillingly understated. The line reduces killing to a technical matter of sound, showing how normalised cruelty has become. By contrast, the Duchess's request that her child be given "some syrup for his cold" restores domestic tenderness to the centre of the scene. Webster makes the audience see what the violence destroys: not merely a political figure, but a mother, wife and moral intelligence.

After the Duchess's death, cruelty rebounds upon its agents. Ferdinand's line "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young" is fractured and evasive, suggesting guilt, shock and psychological collapse. His later lycanthropy literalises the bestial violence that has always shaped his imagination. Bosola's recognition that "Other sins only speak; murder shrieks out" marks a belated moral awakening. Yet Webster refuses easy redemption. Recognition arrives only after irreversible damage. The final act's accumulating deaths show that violence spreads, misfires and consumes even those who attempt to redirect it towards justice.

Ultimately, Webster presents cruelty and violence as the operating language of a corrupt court. Violence appears in imagery, surveillance, spectacle, deception, rhetoric and physical death. The Duchess's moral victory lies not in escaping violence, but in refusing to let it define her. Webster's tragedy is therefore not merely bloody; it is ethically investigative. It asks what kind of society turns cruelty into performance, and what kind of selfhood can survive when power controls the stage, the body and the story.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it defines cruelty and violence broadly but precisely: physical, verbal, psychological, symbolic and theatrical. AO1 is sustained through a conceptual argument about cruelty as courtly performance. AO2 is developed through close analysis of metaphor, animal imagery, hell imagery, surveillance language, masque, props, darkness, understatement and fractured syntax. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean patriarchy, aristocratic honour, patronage, revenge tragedy, masque culture, memento mori and the good-death tradition.

# Model Answer 2 — Deception

## Question

Explore how Webster presents the theme of deception in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents deception in *The Duchess of Malfi* as a symptom of a society where truth cannot safely appear in public. The play is full of secrecy, disguise, false appearances, staged signs and deliberate misreadings. Yet Webster makes an important moral distinction. The Duchess's concealment of her marriage is ethically sincere, because it protects love in a hostile world; the court's deceptions are coercive, because they protect power and manufacture suffering. Deception is therefore not one simple moral category. Webster uses it to ask who must hide the truth, who has the power to expose it, and who benefits from making others believe a lie.

The Duchess's secret marriage is the central deception, but Webster presents it sympathetically. When she tells Antonio, "I do here put off all vain ceremony," she rejects the public performance of rank in order to create a private truth. The phrase "put off" suggests the removal of costume or social mask. Paradoxically, the play's most sincere relationship must be hidden. In a Jacobean aristocratic context, marriage affected inheritance, lineage and reputation; a duchess marrying beneath her status would be understood as a public matter, not merely private choice. Webster makes deception necessary because social hierarchy makes honesty dangerous.

The brothers' attitude to secrecy is hypocritical. The Cardinal warns that the Duchess's "darkest actions" and even her "privat'st thoughts" will come to light. The escalation from actions to thoughts suggests a power that wants to abolish female interiority. Yet the Cardinal himself relies on secrecy to maintain his public authority while pursuing private appetite and political manipulation. Webster therefore exposes deception as gendered. Male secrecy is protected by office and rank; female secrecy is treated as scandal.

Bosola is the play's main instrument of deceptive observation. Ferdinand orders that he be used "for your intelligence," and Bosola's service depends on appearing as a household servant while acting as spy. This doubleness makes him dramatically compelling. He often tells the truth about court corruption, yet his social role is built on concealed betrayal. His "masterpiece of cunning" is to make the Duchess "reveal herself," a phrase that shows deception working by forced exposure. The spy's lie creates the conditions in which another person's truth can be weaponised.

The pregnancy plot shows how deception attaches to the female body. The Duchess's ambiguous language about being "rid of it" allows pregnancy to be disguised as illness or inconvenience. This is not merely trickery; it reveals that maternity itself has become unspeakable under surveillance. The court's patriarchal gaze forces the Duchess to conceal a bodily truth that should belong to marriage and family. Webster's drama therefore presents deception as a survival strategy under gendered pressure.

Ferdinand's deceptions are more openly cruel because they create false realities. The hand-and-ring episode is the clearest example. He presents what seems to be Antonio's hand, with the ring the Duchess gave him. This is deception by object, darkness and staged perception. The ring, a symbol of marital truth, becomes a false sign of death. Webster's use of props matters because the lie is not just spoken; it is materially staged. Ferdinand turns theatre itself into a deceptive apparatus, controlling what the Duchess can see and what she must imagine.

The masque of madmen continues this pattern of staged deception. A masque should signify courtly order and aesthetic harmony, but Ferdinand transforms it into psychological coercion. The form itself becomes deceptive: entertainment disguises punishment. When the Duchess says, "I account this world a tedious theatre, / For I do play a part in't 'gainst my will," she recognises the larger social deception. The court is a stage on which people are forced into roles. Webster's metatheatrical language makes deception not only a plot device but a condition of social life.

The Duchess's resistance depends on plain truth. After the false signs, disguises and staged performances surrounding her, "I am Duchess of Malfi still" has extraordinary force because it is direct, declarative and irreducible. The sentence strips away the court's attempts to rename her as fallen woman, disobedient sister or political embarrassment. Webster contrasts the complexity of deception with the simplicity of self-knowledge. The Duchess's truth survives even when public circumstances are controlled by others.

Bosola's late recognition reveals the limits of exposure. His work as spy uncovers facts: the pregnancy, the marriage, the vulnerability of the Duchess. But facts are not the same as truth. When he declares, "There's a great spirit gone!" he finally recognises the moral meaning he had missed. Webster therefore distinguishes revelation from understanding. A corrupt court can expose secrets while remaining blind to value. Bosola's tragedy is that he only understands the Duchess after his deceptions have helped destroy her.

The final act shows deception collapsing into confusion. Antonio's accidental death results from darkness, mistaken action and incomplete knowledge. Bosola's dying comment that the error is "such a mistake as I have often seen / In a play" turns deception into metatheatre. Webster exposes the machinery of tragedy itself: secrecy, misrecognition and staged darkness generate catastrophe. The Cardinal's final uncertainty about whether death is real or "feigning" completes the point. A life of deception leaves even final sincerity suspect.

Ultimately, Webster presents deception as both a defensive necessity and a destructive instrument. The Duchess hides love because the world cannot tolerate it; the brothers and Bosola manipulate appearances to control, expose and punish. Through secret marriage, spying, ambiguous bodily signs, staged objects, performance and mistaken action, Webster shows that deception is built into the structure of Malfi's court. The tragedy is that truth exists, but the social world is so corrupt that truth must either hide or be used against those who speak it.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it distinguishes protective secrecy from coercive deception. AO1 is sustained through a clear argument about truth, visibility and power. AO2 is developed through close analysis of dramatic irony, equivocation, props, darkness, masque, declarative speech, metatheatre and mistaken action. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean marriage politics, aristocratic lineage, patriarchal surveillance, courtly performance, patronage and gendered reputation.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Cruelty and Violence

Webster presents cruelty and violence as the language of a corrupt court: physical suffering is only one part of a wider system of verbal degradation, surveillance, staged spectacle, psychological pressure and patriarchal control.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Deception

Webster presents deception as morally divided: the Duchess conceals sincere love because truth is unsafe, while the court uses deception to surveil, manipulate and punish. The tragedy exposes a society where truth exists but cannot safely appear.

## Exam warning

For cruelty and violence, avoid simply cataloguing brutal moments. Track the full system: language, surveillance, stagecraft, props, spectacle, death and aftermath. For deception, do not treat all concealment as morally equal. The Duchess's secrecy protects sincere love; the court's deception protects power.
