# Model Answer 1 — Claudius

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents Claudius in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents Claudius as both a conventional fratricidal villain and a disturbingly competent political performer. He is never merely an emblem of evil. Instead, Shakespeare makes him theatrically persuasive: a king whose public rhetoric is polished, whose political instincts are sharp, and whose private conscience is sufficiently alive to make his corruption psychologically complex. Across the play, Claudius becomes the centre of Denmark’s moral disorder because his crime against blood, monarchy and God contaminates the court’s language, rituals and relationships. Yet the dramatic force of his presentation lies in the contradiction between surface order and inward rot: Claudius can govern, speak and calculate effectively, but he cannot purify the sin on which his kingship depends.

Claudius’s first appearance establishes him as a ruler who controls public performance. His opening speech is a carefully balanced act of political theatre: “Though yet of Hamlet our dear brother’s death / The memory be green,” he announces, before moving rapidly from mourning to remarriage and diplomacy. The concessive “though yet” acknowledges grief only in order to manage and contain it. Shakespeare’s use of antithesis in “With mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage” compresses emotional opposites into one syntactic structure, making Claudius sound measured, almost statesmanlike. However, the neat rhetorical balance is also morally disturbing. Grief and celebration are not reconciled; they are manipulated. In an early modern context, where kingship was linked to divine order and legitimate succession, Claudius’s command of ceremony becomes suspicious because it overlays an unnatural transfer of power. One reading sees him as the play’s Machiavellian ruler: not a chaotic tyrant, but a politician who understands that authority depends on controlling appearances.

This political competence makes Claudius a more unsettling antagonist than a simple stage villain. He deals briskly with the threat from Fortinbras, dispatching ambassadors and asserting Denmark’s diplomatic position. His blank verse is controlled and public-facing, suggesting that Shakespeare presents him as a ruler who can perform the duties of monarchy. This creates an interpretive complication: Claudius may be morally illegitimate, but he is not politically incompetent. Some audiences might even find him more practically suited to rule than Hamlet, whose intelligence repeatedly turns inward. However, Shakespeare ensures that this effectiveness is ethically compromised. Claudius’s authority is founded on fratricide and usurpation, so his political fluency becomes a form of concealment. The court may look ordered, but the Ghost’s later revelation — “The serpent that did sting thy father’s life / Now wears his crown” — redefines Claudius’s kingship as a satanic parody of legitimacy. The biblical echo of Cain and Abel is crucial: Claudius’s murder of his brother violates family, nation and divine law at once.

Shakespeare develops Claudius through the contrast between public control and private guilt. In public, he rebukes Hamlet’s grief as “unmanly,” “impious” and “a fault to heaven,” using religious language to police Hamlet’s mourning. This is dramatically ironic, since Claudius himself is the true offender against heaven. The speech exposes his hypocrisy, but also his skill at turning moral vocabulary into political pressure. He presents Hamlet’s grief as a threat to social order, not as a legitimate response to bereavement. In this sense, Claudius’s language is coercive: it defines normality in ways that protect his own position. Shakespeare’s presentation of him therefore reflects anxieties about court culture and surveillance, where public conformity matters more than inward truth.

Claudius’s use of surveillance intensifies this sense of a corrupted political order. He recruits Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to “draw him on to pleasures” and discover Hamlet’s “unknown afflicts,” turning friendship into espionage. Later, he uses Polonius and Ophelia in the staged encounter with Hamlet, creating a court in which private emotion is watched, managed and weaponised. These surveillance scenes are not incidental plot devices; structurally, they show how Claudius’s crime changes Denmark into a state of suspicion. The king’s sin produces a political atmosphere in which language becomes indirect and relationships become instrumental. A critical reading of Claudius as the corrupting centre of Elsinore is therefore persuasive: his original act of hidden violence generates a whole dramatic system of concealment.

The prayer scene is Shakespeare’s most complex presentation of Claudius because it gives him moral self-knowledge without moral transformation. Alone, he admits, “O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven,” and the sensory verb “smells” makes sin almost physically corrupt. The adjective “rank” links his guilt to the play’s wider imagery of decay: Denmark’s political disease is also spiritual putrefaction. His question — “Is there not rain enough in the sweet heavens / To wash it white as snow?” — draws on Christian imagery of cleansing and forgiveness, but the syntax turns hope into uncertainty. Claudius understands the theology of repentance, yet he cannot fulfil it because he will not surrender “my crown, mine own ambition, and my queen.” The possessive triad exposes the material attachments that bind him to sin. In early modern Christian thought, repentance required contrition, confession and amendment of life; Claudius can confess internally, but he cannot renounce the profits of the crime.

This soliloquy complicates the view of Claudius as a flat Machiavellian villain. He is capable of introspection, and his guilt is not mere fear of punishment. Nevertheless, Shakespeare refuses to redeem him. The final couplet — “My words fly up, my thoughts remain below: / Words without thoughts never to heaven go” — divides language from inward truth. The rhyming closure sounds epigrammatic, but it confirms spiritual failure. Claudius can still produce elegant language, yet eloquence cannot save him. The scene also creates dramatic irony because Hamlet enters while Claudius appears to be praying. Hamlet’s decision not to kill him “now” because his soul might go to heaven is based on a misreading: Claudius is not spiritually cleansed at all. Shakespeare therefore makes Claudius’s conscience theatrically visible to the audience while keeping it hidden from Hamlet, increasing the tragic pressure.

Claudius’s relationship with Gertrude further reveals his mixture of political calculation and human attachment. His marriage to her consolidates his power, and to Hamlet it appears incestuous and morally diseased. Yet Shakespeare does not present the relationship simply as loveless manipulation. Claudius’s request that Gertrude leave the chamber after Polonius’s death, and his anxiety after Hamlet’s violence, suggest both possessiveness and genuine fear. This ambiguity matters. If Claudius is only a cold usurper, the play’s moral pattern is simple; if he is capable of attachment, his villainy becomes more troubling because ordinary human feeling coexists with murderous self-preservation. A strong AO5 reading should therefore hold both possibilities together: Gertrude may be a political prize, but she is also part of the emotional world Claudius refuses to relinquish.

As the play moves towards catastrophe, Shakespeare makes Claudius increasingly dependent on manipulation rather than public legitimacy. His handling of Laertes is especially revealing. Faced with a young man whose father has been killed and whose sister is dead or dying, Claudius channels grief into revenge. He flatters Laertes’s honour and constructs the poisoned duel, converting another son’s grief into an instrument of state murder. This is revenge-tragedy convention distorted by political opportunism: Claudius does not merely participate in revenge; he engineers it to preserve himself. The poison imagery is structurally fitting because poison is his original method and his final exposure. What began as hidden venom in Old Hamlet’s ear returns as poisoned sword and cup in the public catastrophe.

In the final scene, Claudius’s theatrical authority collapses. His attempt to maintain ceremony during the duel cannot contain the consequences of his concealed crime. The poisoned cup, intended for Hamlet, kills Gertrude; the poisoned weapon exposes the conspiracy; Laertes confesses; Hamlet finally names Claudius as “incestuous, murderous, damned Dane.” The piling of adjectives performs a final stripping away of royal disguise. Claudius is no longer king, husband or statesman, but a damned criminal. Shakespeare’s tragic structure therefore reveals what Claudius has tried to suppress from the beginning: political performance cannot permanently conceal moral disorder.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents Claudius as the play’s most dangerous figure because he unites intelligence, eloquence and corruption. His power depends on the separation of appearance from truth: public order hides private guilt, political competence hides illegitimacy, and religious language hides spiritual failure. Yet the play’s tragedy depends on the fact that Claudius is not simply monstrous. He knows what he has done, understands the possibility of repentance, and still chooses possession over salvation. Shakespeare’s presentation is therefore both morally severe and psychologically subtle. Claudius is a fratricidal usurper, a Machiavellian performer, and a guilty sinner whose self-knowledge makes his damnation more complete.

# Model Answer 2 — Death

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents the theme of death in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents death in *Hamlet* as both an unavoidable physical reality and a metaphysical crisis that human reason cannot fully master. The play begins with death as a disturbance in the afterlife, moves through suicidal longing, murder, revenge and bodily decay, and ends with Hamlet’s bleak acceptance that “the readiness is all.” Death is therefore not only an event in the plot, but the central pressure that exposes the fragility of politics, theology and identity. Shakespeare’s tragic method is to make death at once theatrical, philosophical and material: it appears as ghost, corpse, skull, poison, memory and judgement.

The Ghost’s appearance immediately presents death as unsettled and theologically disturbing. Old Hamlet is dead, but not absent. The opening battlements create an atmosphere of uncertainty, where the boundary between the living and the dead is unstable. When the Ghost describes himself as “confined to fast in fires,” Shakespeare invokes ideas associated with purgatorial suffering, even though Protestant England officially rejected purgatory. This creates an interpretive tension: the Ghost may be a suffering spirit demanding justice, a demonic temptation, or a dramatic embodiment of unresolved political crime. Death, from the beginning, is not simply biological cessation; it is a problem of judgement, memory and truth.

Old Hamlet’s murder also makes death a political corruption. The Ghost’s image of poison poured into the ear is both physically grotesque and symbolically exact: Denmark has been infected through hidden speech and secret violence. Claudius’s fratricide violates family bonds, dynastic succession and sacred kingship. In an early modern context, the murder of a king carried implications beyond private crime because monarchy was imagined as part of divine and national order. The Ghost’s accusation — “The serpent that did sting thy father’s life / Now wears his crown” — fuses biblical imagery with political illegitimacy. Death here produces not closure, but contamination: the dead king’s body is gone, yet the consequences of his murder animate the entire state.

Hamlet’s own first soliloquy presents death as desired release from corrupted life. “O that this too too solid flesh would melt,” he says, imagining the self dissolving out of physical existence. The repeated intensifier “too too” conveys emotional excess, while “solid flesh” makes existence feel imprisoning. Yet the wish is immediately constrained by theology: “the Everlasting had not fixed / His canon ’gainst self-slaughter.” Shakespeare presents suicide as both psychologically attractive and religiously forbidden. This tension is central to the play’s treatment of death. Hamlet’s longing is not merely adolescent despair; it is shaped by Christian doctrine, fear of sin and disgust at a world where funeral and marriage have collapsed into one another.

“To be, or not to be” develops this into a more abstract meditation on mortality. The balanced antithesis of the opening phrase turns existence itself into a question, while the sequence of infinitives — “to die, to sleep” — initially makes death sound like rest. However, the metaphor breaks down when Hamlet imagines “what dreams may come.” Shakespeare’s rhetorical questioning dramatises thought as paralysis: the mind cannot stop investigating death, yet it cannot reach certainty. The phrase “The undiscovered country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” presents death as a border beyond empirical knowledge. A Renaissance humanist reading might stress Hamlet’s sceptical intelligence, refusing easy answers; a theological reading might stress his terror before judgement. Shakespeare holds both possibilities together, making death intellectually fascinating and spiritually terrifying.

Revenge tragedy convention intensifies the problem because Hamlet is commanded to answer death with death. The Ghost’s command places him inside a genre that expects violent retribution, yet Shakespeare complicates the convention by making Hamlet morally and theologically self-conscious. Killing Claudius is not simply a heroic duty; it raises questions about evidence, damnation, justice and the soul. This is most apparent in the prayer scene, where Hamlet refuses to kill Claudius because he thinks the king may be spiritually prepared. “Now might I do it pat,” he says, the monosyllabic directness suggesting opportunity, but his reasoning delays action. The scene shows death as theological timing: to kill a body is also to intervene in the possible destiny of a soul.

Polonius’s death marks a shift from philosophical meditation to grotesque materiality. Hamlet kills him suddenly, almost accidentally, through the arras, reducing the hidden counsellor to “a rat.” The staging matters: death occurs behind a screen, fitting a court built on concealment. Afterwards, Hamlet’s treatment of the corpse is brutally comic. His image of “a certain convocation of politic worms” feeding on Polonius collapses rank, ceremony and political importance into bodily decay. Prose is important here because it lowers the register; death is not sublime but digestive. Shakespeare uses grotesque comedy to puncture aristocratic pride, suggesting that the body’s fate is indifferent to status.

Ophelia’s death presents death through gender, ritual and contested meaning. Her drowning is described indirectly by Gertrude in lyrical, pastoral imagery: “There is a willow grows aslant a brook.” The beauty of the language aestheticises death, but it also raises uncertainty. Did Ophelia die passively, accidentally, or by suicide? The later burial scene makes this ambiguity socially and theologically significant. The priest’s statement that “Her death was doubtful” reflects Christian anxieties about suicide and burial rites. Shakespeare therefore shows death as something interpreted by institutions: church, family and court all attempt to define Ophelia’s death, but none can fully possess its meaning. A feminist reading might argue that Ophelia’s death is shaped by a patriarchal world that gives her little agency in life and then disputes her identity in death.

The graveyard scene is Shakespeare’s most concentrated exploration of bodily mortality. The skull of Yorick functions as a memento mori, a theatrical prop that makes abstract death visible. Hamlet’s “Alas, poor Yorick!” begins with personal memory, but the scene expands into universal levelling. The physical skull collapses the distance between childhood affection and anatomical fact. Hamlet’s later imagining of “Imperious Caesar, dead and turned to clay” reduces imperial greatness to matter that might “stop a hole.” The adjective “imperious” intensifies the fall: even the highest political authority ends as common earth. The gravediggers’ comedy reinforces this levelling effect. Their jokes do not trivialise death; they domesticate it, presenting mortality as ordinary labour rather than courtly abstraction.

This comic handling of death creates a strong AO5 debate. One interpretation sees the graveyard as nihilistic, reducing all human ambition to decay. Another sees it as morally clarifying: by confronting the skull, Hamlet moves beyond disgust and fear towards acceptance. Shakespeare does not resolve the debate simply. The scene is funny, philosophical and horrifying at once. Its power lies in tonal instability: tragedy is interrupted by clowning, but the comedy exposes truths that solemn rhetoric avoids.

By Act 5, Hamlet’s attitude to death has changed. His statement that “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” suggests a turn towards providential thinking. This is not simple confidence; it is a chastened recognition that human calculation cannot control events. When he says “The readiness is all,” Shakespeare presents acceptance not as triumph, but as disciplined vulnerability. Hamlet no longer tries to master death through thought. He acknowledges that mortality arrives whether one anticipates it or not. In the context of Christian providence, this can be read as spiritual maturity; in a bleaker existential reading, it may be merely resignation before a meaningless sequence of accidents.

The final duel presents death as the catastrophic return of the play’s hidden corruption. Poison, the method of Old Hamlet’s murder, reappears in the cup and blade. Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius and Hamlet die in rapid succession, making the court’s ceremonial surface collapse into violent exposure. The stage becomes crowded with bodies, fulfilling revenge-tragedy expectations while also revealing the wastefulness of revenge. The final deaths may be read as purgation: the corrupt court is cleared, and Fortinbras enters to restore political order. Yet Shakespeare also leaves a strong sense of loss. “Good night, sweet prince” gives Hamlet a ceremonial tenderness, but it cannot restore the dead or justify the cost.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents death in *Hamlet* as the force that strips away illusion. It exposes Claudius’s illegitimacy, Hamlet’s intellectual limits, Polonius’s political vanity, Ophelia’s vulnerability and the court’s corruption. Yet death also remains unknowable: the Ghost, the fear of “what dreams may come,” and the unresolved moral status of revenge prevent any easy conclusion. Shakespeare’s tragedy moves from supernatural disturbance to bodily decay and final acceptance, but it never makes death simple. It is punishment, mystery, political consequence, theological terror and physical inevitability at once.
