# Model Answer 1 — Religion

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of religion in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents religion in *The Duchess of Malfi* as both a language of moral judgement and a system repeatedly corrupted by power. The play does not simply reject religion; rather, it contrasts genuine spiritual dignity with institutional hypocrisy. The Cardinal embodies religious authority emptied of conscience, while the Duchess, especially in suffering and death, seems to recover a deeper form of spiritual integrity. Webster therefore uses religion to expose the difference between outward office and inward virtue. In a Jacobean Protestant theatrical context, a corrupt Catholic Cardinal would carry obvious anti-Catholic associations, but Webster's critique is wider than denominational satire: the play attacks any institution that uses sacred authority to disguise appetite, surveillance and violence.

From the beginning, Webster associates the court with religious and moral corruption. Bosola's bitter claim that courtiers "deny God" both in prosperity and adversity presents irreligion as habitual rather than exceptional. The repetition of "deny God" gives the line sermonic force, as though Bosola is delivering a moral indictment of the court before becoming implicated in it himself. Yet the irony is severe: Bosola can identify hypocrisy while still entering Ferdinand's service. Webster thus presents religious language as diagnostically powerful but practically weak when set against patronage and ambition. In a culture in which religion was expected to structure public and private conduct, the court's denial of God marks not just personal sin but collective moral collapse.

The Cardinal is Webster's most obvious figure of religious hypocrisy. As a churchman, he should represent spiritual discipline, restraint and moral authority, yet he is repeatedly associated with secrecy, lust and manipulation. His affair with Julia exposes the gap between public office and private appetite. Webster makes this hypocrisy especially pointed because the Duchess's lawful marriage is punished as scandal while the Cardinal's illicit sexuality is tolerated. The double standard reveals how religious authority can become a shield for male privilege. Jacobean audiences, accustomed to stage Cardinals as symbols of Catholic corruption and political intrigue, would have recognised the anti-clerical force of the portrayal. However, Webster's dramatic purpose is not merely to make the Cardinal a stock villain; it is to show how institutional holiness can become theatrical costume.

Antonio's description of the Cardinal's law as "a foul black cobweb to a spider" develops this religious and institutional critique. The metaphor suggests that authority becomes a trap rather than a moral structure. A cobweb is both dwelling and prison; it protects the predator while entangling the victim. This is an important image for religion in the play because it suggests that sacred and legal forms have been emptied of justice. The Cardinal's churchly status does not restrain corruption; it refines and conceals it. In this sense, Webster presents religion under the pressure of Machiavellian court politics, where forms of piety survive but their ethical content is hollowed out.

Against this hypocrisy, the Duchess's language often presents a more authentic spirituality. Her complaint that she is treated like "a holy relic" exposes how religious idealisation can itself become a form of imprisonment. The simile is striking because relics are revered but immobilised; they are sacred because they are contained. Webster uses this religious image to criticise patriarchal sanctification of female chastity. The Duchess is not irreligious because she rejects this role; rather, she resists a false holiness imposed on women by men who want to control their bodies. In early modern culture, widowhood could be idealised through chastity and memorial devotion to the dead husband. Webster challenges that ideal by allowing the Duchess to insist on being "flesh and blood," not an alabaster monument.

The death scene is the clearest moment where Webster contrasts institutional religion with lived spiritual dignity. Bosola appears in disguise and offers language that resembles consolation, yet this comfort is part of the machinery of execution. When he, as a bellman-like figure, seems to guide the Duchess towards "the shrine of peace," spiritual care is weaponised. Religion here becomes theatrical performance: the executioner borrows the forms of pastoral guidance while preparing murder. This is one of Webster's bleakest dramatic gestures. Sacred language itself has been conscripted into violence.

Yet the Duchess converts this corrupted ritual into an assertion of spiritual sovereignty. Her command, "Pull, and pull strongly, for your able strength / Must pull down heaven upon me," reframes strangulation as a movement towards heaven. The imperative "Pull" gives her an astonishing degree of control over the scene: she commands her own executioners and redefines the meaning of their violence. The image of heaven being pulled down reverses the degradation of death by strangling; what should be humiliating becomes spiritually elevated. Webster here draws on the tradition of the good death, or *ars moriendi*, in which composure before death reveals the condition of the soul. The Duchess's death is not merely pathetic; it becomes the play's strongest counter-image to institutional corruption.

The Duchess's other death-scene lines deepen this spiritual dignity by refusing melodrama. Her claim that death has "ten thousand several doors / For men to take their exits" turns mortality into theatrical and philosophical inevitability. The word "exits" is metatheatrical, but it also suggests a calm acceptance of human limitation. Similarly, her wish that she might hold "some two days' conference / With the dead" makes the afterlife relational rather than doctrinal. She imagines death not through theological abstraction but through conversation, continuity and human fellowship. Webster's religious vision is therefore not located in the Cardinal's office but in the Duchess's composure, tenderness and moral clarity.

Ferdinand's response after her death shows the spiritual collapse of those who tried to claim moral authority over her. His line "I'll tell thee a miracle — / I am not mad" grotesquely misuses religious language. A "miracle" should signal divine intervention, yet here it is attached to a deluded assertion of sanity. Webster's irony is savage: in the world of the play, the miraculous has been reduced to a tyrant's inability to recognise his own disorder. Ferdinand's later lycanthropy and the Cardinal's final terror suggest that religious judgement returns not through institutional justice but through psychological and bodily breakdown.

Ultimately, Webster presents religion as a contested field. Institutional religion, represented by the Cardinal and by the corrupted ritual around the Duchess's execution, is hypocritical, theatrical and complicit in violence. Yet spiritual integrity survives in the Duchess's courage, maternal care and controlled acceptance of death. The play's religious critique is therefore double: it condemns sacred authority when it serves appetite, but it also preserves the possibility of true moral dignity beyond worldly corruption. In *The Duchess of Malfi*, the most religious figure is not the Cardinal in office, but the Duchess at the point of death.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it avoids reducing religion to a simple anti-Catholic point. AO1 is controlled through a central distinction between institutional hypocrisy and spiritual integrity. AO2 is developed through close analysis of repetition, religious imagery, metaphor, disguise, imperatives, death-scene rhetoric and metatheatrical language. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean anti-Catholic stage convention, widowhood, conduct expectations, religious office, patronage, and the *ars moriendi* tradition.

# Model Answer 2 — Excess

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of the theme of excess in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents excess in *The Duchess of Malfi* as the defining condition of a corrupt court: power becomes excessive when it loses moral proportion, desire becomes excessive when it turns into ownership, and revenge becomes excessive when it pursues punishment beyond death. The play repeatedly stages a world where appetite overflows restraint. Ferdinand's imagination is excessive in its sexual disgust and violent fantasy; the Cardinal's hypocrisy is excessive because religious office masks private corruption; and Bosola's belated revenge is excessive because it multiplies death without restoring justice. Webster therefore uses excess not simply for sensational effect, but as a moral and dramatic principle. Excess reveals a society in which boundaries — between justice and cruelty, love and possession, performance and torture — have collapsed.

The first major form of excess is Ferdinand's excessive sexualised imagination. His warning to the Duchess is charged with obscene innuendo, especially when he claims that women are like the part which, "like the lamprey, / Hath ne'er a bone in't." The simile is deliberately grotesque. Rather than offering rational moral advice, Ferdinand reduces female sexuality to a boneless, slippery appetite. The excess is partly linguistic: the image is far more graphic than the situation requires, revealing that Ferdinand's imagination is already corrupted. In a Jacobean patriarchal context, brothers might plausibly claim authority over a widowed sister's remarriage because of inheritance and family honour. Webster, however, pushes that authority into excess, exposing the obscene fantasy beneath the rhetoric of protection.

Ferdinand's response to the Duchess's marriage shows excess becoming punitive and almost theological. When he imagines the bodies of the Duchess and Antonio "burnt in a coal-pit" so that their "cursed smoke might not ascend to heaven," his revenge exceeds death itself. The fantasy seeks not only physical destruction but spiritual exclusion. This is a defining excess of revenge tragedy: punishment no longer aims at justice but becomes an imaginative indulgence in damnation. The hellish imagery of smoke, burning and blocked ascent transforms Ferdinand into a parody of divine judgement. Contextually, early modern audiences would have recognised the seriousness of salvation and damnation; Ferdinand's attempt to imagine control over the afterlife therefore reveals blasphemous overreach.

Webster also presents excess through the court's appetite for surveillance. Ferdinand commands that Bosola be employed "for your intelligence," making spying part of normal court practice. Bosola later describes his discovery of the Duchess's pregnancy as his "masterpiece of cunning." The word "masterpiece" is excessive because it aestheticises surveillance: betrayal becomes art. In a corrupt patronage culture, information has value, and Webster shows how that value produces moral distortion. The Duchess cannot simply live; her body must be interpreted, watched and turned into evidence. Excess here is not only emotional but structural: the court extends its reach beyond action into privacy, pregnancy and inward desire.

The imprisonment scenes intensify excess into theatrical cruelty. Ferdinand's plan to send a "masque of madmen" turns court entertainment into psychological violence. A masque was associated with aristocratic order, spectacle and carefully controlled display, but Webster grotesquely inverts it. Ferdinand calls the torture his "masterpiece," suggesting that he experiences cruelty as artistic creation. This is excessive because the punishment no longer has any rational political function. The Duchess is already powerless; yet Ferdinand continues to manufacture scenes of suffering. Webster makes the audience confront the disturbing pleasure that power can take in staging pain. In revenge tragedy, spectacle is expected, but Webster exposes spectacle itself as morally suspect.

The dead hand episode also presents excess as the manipulation of perception. Ferdinand gives the Duchess what appears to be Antonio's severed hand, bearing the ring she gave him. This is not merely a lie; it is a carefully curated sensory assault. The excess lies in the precision of the cruelty: Ferdinand weaponises intimacy itself, turning the ring and hand into props of psychological torture. The stagecraft matters. Webster makes excess visible through objects, darkness and timing, so that violence becomes theatrical design. The audience is forced to recognise that Ferdinand's power operates like that of a dramatist, arranging false scenes to break another mind.

Bosola's comment that "Strangling is a very quiet death" reveals a different kind of excess: the normalisation of cruelty through understatement. The sentence is chilling because it treats murder as a technical choice. Webster presents a world so excessive in violence that even execution can be discussed in terms of convenience and acoustics. This is not loud excess but cold excess — the bureaucratic reduction of killing to method. The Duchess's response, by contrast, is proportionate, controlled and dignified. Her composure makes the excess around her more visible.

Ferdinand's collapse after the Duchess's death shows excess turning back upon itself. "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young" is built from short, fractured clauses. The syntax suggests that the imagination which produced excessive punishment can no longer bear its own result. The word "dazzle" is particularly ambiguous: it may signal guilt, grief, desire or horror. Webster does not clarify because Ferdinand's excess has exceeded rational explanation. His later lycanthropy literalises the animality that has been present in his language throughout. The report of him carrying a man's leg and howling near Saint Mark's Church gives physical form to a mind that has overrun the boundaries of humanity.

The Cardinal's excess is less flamboyant but equally destructive. He combines religious office with sexual appetite, political manipulation and murder. His admission that he has "grown to need / A soldier and a churchman" collapses sacred and martial identities. This is excessive because it fuses roles that should remain morally distinct. A churchman should guide souls; a soldier uses force. The Cardinal's world erases the difference. In a Jacobean Protestant context, such a figure would evoke suspicion of Catholic power and clerical hypocrisy, but Webster also uses him to show how institutional authority can expand beyond ethical limits.

The final act presents revenge itself as excessive and morally exhausted. Bosola's revenge catalogue — for the Duchess, Antonio, Julia and himself — seems to impose order by naming victims, but it also reveals that revenge has become a sequence of multiplying deaths. His image of humans as the "stars' tennis-balls," struck whichever way the heavens please, reduces human agency to cosmic sport. This is a bleak conclusion to the play's pattern of excess. Violence has exceeded intention; characters die by accident, confusion and overreach as much as by justice. Antonio's accidental death is especially important because it prevents any clean tragic resolution. Webster suggests that in a world governed by excess, revenge cannot restore proportion; it only extends disorder.

Ultimately, Webster presents excess as a moral disease that begins in aristocratic power and spreads through language, spectacle, sexuality, religion and revenge. The Duchess is surrounded by excess, yet her own language is marked by restraint: she dies with dignity, domestic tenderness and spiritual composure. This contrast is crucial. Webster does not make excess attractive; he makes it theatrically compelling but ethically repellent. Through grotesque imagery, violent fantasy, masques, props, fractured syntax and revenge-tragedy bloodshed, he shows that excess is what happens when power loses the capacity for measure. The tragedy exposes a society in which everything is too much — except justice, mercy and love, which arrive too late or not at all.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it defines excess conceptually rather than treating it as a list of violent moments. AO1 is sustained through a clear argument about moral disproportion. AO2 is integrated through close analysis of grotesque simile, hell imagery, surveillance language, masque, props, understatement, syntax and final revenge structure. AO3 is embedded through patriarchy, widowhood, revenge tragedy, Jacobean attitudes to damnation, court masque culture, anti-Catholic stage convention and patronage politics.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Religion

Webster presents religion as divided between corrupt institution and genuine spiritual dignity: the Cardinal's office exposes religious hypocrisy, while the Duchess's death reveals a deeper moral and spiritual integrity that institutional religion fails to protect.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Excess

Webster presents excess as moral disproportion: Ferdinand's sexualised imagination, the court's surveillance, the Cardinal's hypocrisy and the revenge plot all exceed ethical limits, turning power into spectacle, cruelty and self-destruction.
