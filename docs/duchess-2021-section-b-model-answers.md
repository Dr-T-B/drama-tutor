# Model Answer 1 — The Relationship Between the Duchess and Bosola

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of the relationship between the Duchess and Bosola in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents the relationship between the Duchess and Bosola as one of the play's most unstable and revealing dramatic structures. It begins as surveillance, develops into psychological combat, and ends in belated moral recognition. Bosola is employed to expose the Duchess, yet he becomes the character most forced to recognise her value. The relationship is therefore not simply one between victim and villain; it is a tragic encounter between integrity and compromised intelligence. The Duchess repeatedly exposes Bosola's moral poverty, while Bosola's language attempts to reduce her body, rank and spirit to signs he can interpret. Through this relationship, Webster examines patronage, gendered surveillance, class resentment and the possibility — but insufficiency — of late repentance.

Bosola is introduced as a figure already morally damaged by the court before he becomes the Duchess's spy. His complaint that he would hang on powerful men's ears "like a horse-leech" until full, then drop off, presents patronage as parasitism. The simile is grotesque and bodily: service is reduced to feeding. Yet Bosola's own bitterness does not prevent him from accepting Ferdinand's employment. This makes his later relationship with the Duchess structurally corrupt from the start. He enters her household not as a servant offering loyal attendance, but as an agent of aristocratic surveillance. In a Jacobean patronage culture, where advancement depended heavily on service to the powerful, Bosola embodies the moral compromise created by economic dependence.

The Duchess first becomes significant to Bosola not as a person but as a body to be read. Ferdinand orders him to serve "for your intelligence," and Bosola later describes his detection of her pregnancy as his "masterpiece of cunning" because he causes her to "reveal herself." The phrase is chilling because it aestheticises betrayal. "Masterpiece" implies craft, skill and artistic pride, while "reveal herself" makes the Duchess's body a text to be deciphered by male surveillance. Webster uses Bosola to dramatise a patriarchal culture in which female privacy is not recognised as legitimate. Her body becomes evidence, and Bosola becomes both interpreter and violator of that evidence.

The relationship becomes more complex because Bosola is not intellectually crude. He can diagnose court corruption accurately, which makes his betrayal more morally disturbing. His claim that "a politician is the devil's quilted anvil" presents political evil as silently manufactured: sins are forged, but the blows are unheard. The metaphor reveals Bosola's insight into hidden corruption, yet he remains part of the same machinery. Webster therefore creates a painful dramatic irony. Bosola often sees clearly but acts wrongly. His relationship with the Duchess becomes the test of whether perception can produce moral action. For much of the play, it cannot.

The Duchess repeatedly unsettles Bosola because she answers his cynical worldview with composure and moral courage. When captured, she tells him, "Now by my life, I pity you." The line reverses the expected power relation. Bosola appears to hold practical control, yet the Duchess assumes the higher moral position by pitying the man who will help destroy her. The oath "by my life" is especially poignant because her life is precisely what Bosola's service will endanger. Webster makes the Duchess's compassion a form of judgement: she sees Bosola's damaged condition more clearly than he sees her dignity.

In the imprisonment scenes, Bosola's relationship with the Duchess becomes rhetorically violent. His memento mori language — calling her a "box of worm-seed" and a "salvatory of green mummy" — attempts to reduce her living body to future decay. The imagery is grotesque, medicinal and degrading. It denies present identity by insisting on decomposition. Yet the Duchess resists this rhetorical destruction. Her question, "Am I not thy Duchess?" is not merely a demand for rank but an assertion of identity against Bosola's bodily reduction. Webster stages a conflict between two forms of language: Bosola's language of material decay and the Duchess's language of sovereign selfhood.

The disguise of Bosola as a bellman-like figure intensifies the moral ambiguity of their relationship. He offers religious-sounding consolation, promising to bring her to the "shrine of peace," but this spiritual language is part of the apparatus of execution. The effect is deeply unsettling: Bosola borrows the language of care while preparing murder. In early modern death culture, preparation for death carried spiritual seriousness, and Webster perverts that tradition by placing it in the mouth of a compromised servant. Bosola's role becomes theatrical as well as moral. He performs consolation while functioning as an executioner.

However, the Duchess's death forces a change in Bosola's perception. His apostrophe, "Virtuous and noble woman!" and his later recognition that "There's a great spirit gone!" mark a decisive shift from surveillance to reverence. The change is significant because Bosola finally stops reading the Duchess as a body and recognises her as spirit. The word "spirit" matters: it values essence, courage and moral identity rather than rank or physical presence. Yet the recognition is tragically late. Webster refuses to make repentance simple or redemptive. Bosola's moral awakening depends on the very murder he has enabled.

Bosola's later revenge shows that his relationship with the Duchess continues after her death as a burden of guilt. His final catalogue — revenge for the Duchess, Antonio, Julia and himself — suggests that he tries to convert remorse into justice. Yet the structure of the ending complicates this. He kills Antonio by mistake, acts amid confusion, and dies within the same violent machinery he helped operate. His belated devotion to the Duchess cannot restore her life or purify the court. Webster therefore presents his conversion as ethically serious but dramatically inadequate.

Ultimately, the relationship between the Duchess and Bosola is a tragic study of recognition delayed until it is useless. Bosola begins as spy, tempter and verbal torturer; the Duchess remains his moral superior through compassion, self-command and resistance to degradation. Their relationship reveals how a corrupt court turns intelligent servants into instruments of cruelty, and how female integrity can expose that corruption even in death. Webster's bleakest insight is that Bosola comes to see the Duchess truly only after helping to destroy her. The relationship therefore becomes the play's central example of insight arriving too late.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it does not reduce Bosola to a simple villain or the Duchess to a simple victim. AO1 is sustained through a conceptual argument about surveillance, recognition and late repentance. AO2 is developed through close analysis of imagery, disguise, apostrophe, rhetorical conflict, memento mori language and structural transformation. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean patronage, patriarchal surveillance, female chastity, death culture and revenge tragedy.

# Model Answer 2 — Death

## Question

Explore how Webster presents death in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents death in *The Duchess of Malfi* as both theatrical spectacle and moral test. Death is everywhere in the play, but it does not always mean the same thing. For Ferdinand, death becomes an extension of control, a fantasy of punishment that reaches beyond the grave. For Bosola, death begins as a professional task but becomes the trigger for conscience. For the Duchess, death becomes the final space in which identity, dignity and spiritual authority can be asserted. Webster therefore uses death to expose the moral condition of each character. The play's tragedy is not merely that people die, but that death reveals the corruption or integrity that life has concealed.

Webster first frames death as social and political contamination. Antonio's opening fountain metaphor imagines a court in which, if corruption is poisoned "near the head," then "Death and diseases through the whole land spread." Death is not yet an individual event but a symptom of diseased authority. The plural "diseases" suggests a society infected from the top down. This image is important because it prepares the audience to see later deaths as products of a corrupt system rather than accidents. In Jacobean political thought, the health of the ruler and court could be imagined as affecting the whole body politic. Webster makes that idea darkly literal.

Ferdinand's imagination presents death as excessive punishment. After learning of the Duchess's sexuality, he fantasises that her and Antonio's bodies should be "burnt in a coal-pit" so that their "cursed smoke might not ascend to heaven." The violence here exceeds murder. Ferdinand imagines controlling not only bodies but spiritual destiny. The blocked ascent to heaven turns revenge into blasphemous overreach, as if he could prevent salvation itself. In a Christian context where damnation and the afterlife were matters of intense seriousness, Ferdinand's fantasy would appear morally monstrous. Webster presents death, in Ferdinand's mind, as ownership extended into eternity.

The Duchess's imprisonment turns death into theatrical manipulation before it becomes physical fact. Ferdinand shows her what appears to be Antonio's dead hand, with the ring upon it, using a prop to create a false death. The scene is important because Webster presents death as staged perception. Ferdinand does not simply kill; he makes the Duchess believe in death as a psychological weapon. This makes him resemble a perverted dramatist. The use of darkness, bodily fragments and tokens of intimacy transforms the theatre itself into an instrument of torture. In revenge tragedy, stage spectacle often makes death visible; Webster makes false death emotionally destructive before real death occurs.

The masque of madmen similarly turns death into an atmosphere of mental dissolution. Ferdinand's theatrical cruelty surrounds the Duchess with disorder before execution. Yet the Duchess resists this attempt to make death terrifying. Her declaration, "I am not mad yet, to my cause of sorrow," insists on rational self-possession in the face of orchestrated horror. The repeated claim to sanity is crucial. Webster suggests that the anticipation of death reveals not the Duchess's weakness but her strength. Her mind remains intact even as the court attempts to break it.

The Duchess's actual death is presented as a good death, though within a grotesquely corrupted ritual. Her line, "I know death hath ten thousand several doors / For men to take their exits," gives death a theatrical and philosophical form. The word "exits" acknowledges the stage, but it also domesticates mortality: death is one of many doors through which human beings pass. This calm, almost stoic phrasing contrasts sharply with Ferdinand's violent fantasies and Bosola's degrading imagery. In the early modern *ars moriendi* tradition, the manner of dying could reveal spiritual condition. Webster gives the Duchess composure, clarity and verbal control, making her death ethically superior to the lives of those who kill her.

Her command, "Pull, and pull strongly, for your able strength / Must pull down heaven upon me," transforms execution into spiritual ascent. The imperative "Pull" gives her agency at the moment when her body is most vulnerable. Rather than allowing strangulation to define her as degraded, she reinterprets it as heaven descending. This is not passive victimhood. It is an act of rhetorical mastery. Webster makes the Duchess seize interpretive control of her own death, defeating the executioners at the level of meaning even though they defeat her physically.

Webster also makes death intensely domestic. The Duchess's request that Cariola give her little boy "some syrup for his cold" and make her daughter say her prayers before sleep is devastating because it places maternal care beside execution. The detail of "syrup" is ordinary, practical and tender. It reveals that death's cruelty lies not only in physical pain but in the severing of everyday love. Webster's tragedy therefore expands beyond public honour and revenge. The death of the Duchess is also the destruction of a mother and a household. This domestic register would complicate any Jacobean tendency to judge her simply as a disobedient widow, because the scene insists on her humanity.

Bosola's response to death changes after the Duchess is killed. His statement, "Other sins only speak; murder shrieks out," personifies murder as a crime that cannot remain silent. The contrast between "speak" and "shrieks" suggests that death exceeds the court's usual language of concealment. Murder forces itself into sound. Bosola's recognition that "There's a great spirit gone!" similarly marks the moment when death reveals value. He had tried to reduce the Duchess to flesh, decay and social scandal; after death, he recognises her spiritual greatness. Yet Webster makes this recognition tragic because it arrives after irreversible damage. Death instructs Bosola, but too late.

The final act presents death as chaotic rather than providentially clean. Antonio dies accidentally, with the brief cry, "Oh, I am gone! / Thou hast killed me." The simplicity of the line and the mistaken circumstances deny him heroic closure. Bosola's later claim that the mistake is like one he has often seen "in a play" is brutally metatheatrical. Webster exposes revenge tragedy's machinery: deaths occur not as perfect moral justice but through error, darkness and generic momentum. This undermines any easy sense that death restores order. The stage becomes crowded with bodies, but meaning remains unstable.

The Cardinal's and Ferdinand's deaths further reveal the moral consequences of life. The Cardinal asks to be "never thought of," a final desire for oblivion that contrasts sharply with the Duchess's insistence, "I am Duchess of Malfi still." Ferdinand dies echoing the Duchess's diamond image: "Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust." This structural echo makes death a belated recognition scene. The killer repeats the wisdom of the victim, but the knowledge comes too late to save anyone. Webster's ending therefore gives death an ironic intelligence: it clarifies, but only after catastrophe.

Ultimately, Webster presents death as the play's deepest test of meaning. It can be degraded into spectacle, fantasy and murder, but it can also reveal spiritual dignity. The Duchess's death is horrifying, yet it is also the moment when her identity becomes most secure. By contrast, Ferdinand and the Cardinal survive only to expose their inward corruption before dying. Webster uses death to judge the world of the play: those who claim power over death are morally diminished, while the Duchess, who accepts death without surrendering identity, becomes the play's most enduring figure of integrity.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it tracks death as a whole-play structural concern rather than treating the death scene alone. AO1 is controlled through a sustained thesis about death as spectacle, punishment, moral test and revelation. AO2 is developed through metaphor, props, darkness, theatrical language, imperatives, domestic detail, personification and structural echo. AO3 is integrated through revenge tragedy, Jacobean beliefs about death and damnation, the *ars moriendi* tradition, patriarchy, widowhood and the body politic.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Duchess and Bosola

Webster presents the Duchess and Bosola's relationship as a movement from surveillance to belated recognition: Bosola begins by reading and degrading her body for patriarchal power, but her dignity forces him to recognise her spiritual and moral superiority too late.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Death

Webster presents death as both theatrical spectacle and moral judgement: Ferdinand turns death into punishment and control, Bosola is morally awakened by death, and the Duchess transforms death into a final assertion of identity, dignity and spiritual authority.
