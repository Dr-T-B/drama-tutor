# Model Answer 1 — The Relationship Between the Duchess and Bosola

## Question

“Explore Webster’s presentation of the relationship between the Duchess and Bosola in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.”

## Model Answer

Webster presents the relationship between the Duchess and Bosola as one of the play’s most morally unstable dramatic structures: it begins as surveillance, develops into interpretive violation, and ends in belated recognition. Bosola is hired to read, expose and destroy the Duchess, yet he becomes the character most painfully forced to recognise her value. Their relationship is therefore not simply a villain-victim pattern. It is a tragic encounter between female integrity and compromised male intelligence. The Duchess repeatedly exposes Bosola’s spiritual and ethical poverty, while Bosola’s language tries to reduce her body, rank and identity to evidence that can be decoded for patriarchal power. Through their changing relationship, Webster examines Jacobean patronage, aristocratic control, female reputation, widowhood and the revenge-tragedy world in which insight usually arrives too late.

Bosola is introduced as a malcontent whose dependence on patronage has already corrupted his moral judgement before he becomes the Duchess’s spy. His bitter fantasy that he might be “one of their flattering panders” immediately presents service at court as morally degrading. The noun “panders” is sexually and socially contaminating, suggesting that advancement under corrupt rulers requires prostitution of the self. This matters because Bosola’s relationship with the Duchess begins before they properly meet: it is created by Ferdinand’s decision to convert a resentful dependent servant into an instrument of surveillance. In a Jacobean patronage culture, where status and reward depended on the favour of powerful aristocrats, Bosola embodies the danger of intelligence without moral independence. He can diagnose corruption, but economic grievance makes him vulnerable to becoming its agent.

Webster establishes the relationship’s initial asymmetry through Ferdinand’s instruction: “Be sure you entertain that Bosola / For your intelligence.” The command reduces Bosola to a tool of information-gathering and makes the Duchess’s household an espionage space. The word “entertain” is sharply double-edged: it means employ, but it also suggests performance. Bosola will enter the Duchess’s service as a role-player, not as an honest servant. This makes the relationship theatrical from the outset. The Duchess thinks she is receiving a court official into her household; the audience knows that she is being watched. Webster therefore uses dramatic irony to align Bosola with the machinery of patriarchal surveillance, making him not merely a character but a theatrical function: spy, interpreter and disguised threat.

Bosola’s detection of the Duchess’s pregnancy intensifies this relationship because he turns her body into a text to be read. His phrase “This was my masterpiece of cunning” is chilling because it aestheticises betrayal. The noun “masterpiece” gives the deception a perverse artistry, as though the violation of female privacy were a craft achievement. In the apricot episode, Bosola manipulates appetite, physical response and bodily signs to force the Duchess to “reveal herself.” The reflexive phrase implies that her body confesses without her consent. Webster is drawing on early modern assumptions about the legibility of the body, particularly anxieties surrounding female chastity, pregnancy and reputation. In a society where a widow’s sexuality could become a matter of dynastic and patriarchal control, Bosola’s reading of the Duchess’s body is not neutral observation; it is social violence.

The complexity of their relationship depends on the fact that Bosola is not a crude villain. He is perceptive enough to understand the corruption of the court, and this makes his betrayal more disturbing. His image of the “devil’s quilted anvil” suggests hidden political violence: evil is forged silently beneath padded surfaces. The metaphor shows that Bosola understands how power conceals its own brutality. Yet he still participates in that system. Webster creates a powerful dramatic irony: Bosola is intellectually alert but morally inactive. His relationship with the Duchess becomes a test of whether perception can become conscience. For most of the play, it cannot. He reads the Duchess accurately enough to harm her, but not yet generously enough to save her.

When the Duchess confronts suffering, Webster begins to invert the moral hierarchy between them. Her statement, “Now by my life, I pity you,” reverses the apparent power relation. Bosola has practical authority because he serves Ferdinand’s will, but the Duchess claims ethical authority by pitying the man who helps destroy her. The oath “by my life” is especially striking because her life is precisely what Bosola’s service threatens. Webster makes the Duchess’s compassion a form of judgement: she sees Bosola’s damaged condition more clearly than he sees her full humanity. The relationship therefore shifts from simple surveillance to moral exposure. Bosola is no longer only exposing the Duchess; the Duchess is exposing what he has become.

In the imprisonment scenes, Bosola becomes a verbal and theatrical tormentor. His memento mori language reduces the Duchess to future decay: she is imagined as a “box of worm-seed” and a body already turning into corrupted matter. This imagery is grotesque, physical and medicinal; it attempts to strip her of rank, beauty and identity by insisting that all bodies end in decomposition. Yet the Duchess resists this linguistic degradation. Her assertion “I am Duchess of Malfi still” is not empty pride but a dramatic act of self-definition. The adverb “still” is crucial because it insists on continuity under pressure. Bosola’s language says that death will reduce her to matter; the Duchess’s language insists that identity and dignity can survive humiliation. Webster therefore stages their relationship as a conflict between degradation and self-possession.

Bosola’s disguises further complicate the relationship because he performs care while preparing violence. As the tomb-maker and bellman-like figure, he uses the language of spiritual preparation, but the consolation is corrupted by its purpose. In early modern death culture, preparation for death carried religious seriousness; the dying person’s spiritual state mattered intensely. Webster perverts this tradition by putting apparently devotional language in the mouth of a compromised agent. Bosola’s role becomes theatrically unstable: he is servant, spy, moral commentator, death-messenger and executioner’s accomplice. This instability makes the Duchess’s calmness more remarkable. Surrounded by false roles and staged terror, she remains the only character who speaks from a secure moral centre.

The Duchess’s death produces Bosola’s belated recognition. His cry “Virtuous and noble woman!” marks a decisive shift in his perception. He finally names the Duchess in terms of moral worth rather than sexual transgression, bodily evidence or political disobedience. His later declaration, “There’s a great spirit gone!” is even more important because it reverses his earlier materialism. He had tried to reduce the Duchess to flesh, decay and evidence; after death, he recognises spirit. Webster therefore makes the Duchess’s superiority most visible at the moment when patriarchal violence appears to have defeated her. However, the recognition is tragically late. Bosola’s conscience depends on the murder he has enabled.

Bosola’s later revenge is shaped by the afterlife of his relationship with the Duchess, but Webster refuses to make it cleanly redemptive. He tries to act for the Duchess, Antonio, Julia and himself, but his revenge is confused, accidental and morally compromised. His accidental killing of Antonio shows that late remorse cannot restore moral order. In revenge tragedy, the avenger often claims to become an agent of justice, but Bosola never fully escapes the corruption that formed him. He has been spy, interpreter and tormentor before he becomes recogniser and avenger. Webster’s structure makes this sequence essential: recognition is real, but it does not cancel responsibility.

Ultimately, Webster presents the Duchess and Bosola’s relationship as a tragic movement from surveillance to belated reverence. Bosola begins by serving a patriarchal and aristocratic system that treats the Duchess’s body as evidence and her widowhood as a threat to male control. The Duchess, however, forces him to confront a kind of virtue that his cynical worldview cannot absorb. Their relationship is therefore one of the play’s central moral tests. Bosola comes to see the Duchess truly only after helping to destroy her. Webster’s bleak dramatic insight is that in a corrupt court, even intelligence and remorse may arrive too late to become justice.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer reaches high Level 5 because it sustains a conceptual line of argument rather than describing a sequence of plot events. AO1 is secure in the repeated thesis that the relationship develops from surveillance to belated recognition. The written expression is controlled, evaluative and precise, with literary terminology used naturally rather than mechanically.

AO2 is strong because the answer analyses Webster’s dramatic methods: patronage language, dramatic irony, disguise, bodily imagery, memento mori rhetoric, structural development and Bosola’s shifting dramatic function. The answer repeatedly links quotation analysis to theatrical effect.

AO3 is embedded rather than bolted on. Jacobean patronage, patriarchal surveillance, female chastity, widowhood, early modern bodily assumptions, death culture and revenge tragedy are used to sharpen interpretation of the relationship rather than appearing as separate historical paragraphs.

# Model Answer 2 — Death

## Question

“Explore how Webster presents death in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.”

## Model Answer

Webster presents death in *The Duchess of Malfi* as both theatrical spectacle and moral test. Death is everywhere in the play, but it is not presented in one simple form. For Ferdinand, death becomes a fantasy of punishment and spiritual control. For Bosola, death begins as a professional task but becomes the trigger for conscience. For the Duchess, death becomes the final arena in which identity, dignity and spiritual authority can be asserted. Webster therefore uses death to expose the moral condition of each character. The tragedy is not merely that people die, but that death reveals what the corrupt court has attempted to conceal.

From the beginning, death is associated with political and social contamination. Antonio’s opening description of a corrupt court imagines poison spreading from “the head” so that “Death and diseases through the whole land spread.” Death is not first presented as an individual event but as the symptom of diseased authority. The plural “diseases” suggests multiplying corruption, while the body politic metaphor implies that moral disorder at the top infects the whole state. This context is important because Jacobean political culture often imagined rule through images of bodily health and hierarchy. Webster darkens this idea: in Malfi, death spreads because the rulers themselves are diseased.

Ferdinand’s imagination presents death as excessive punishment rather than justice. After learning of the Duchess’s marriage and sexuality, he imagines burning bodies in a “coal-pit” so that their “cursed smoke might not ascend to heaven.” This is more than violent anger. Ferdinand imagines controlling not only bodies but spiritual destiny. The blocked ascent to heaven turns revenge into blasphemous overreach, as if he could interfere with salvation itself. In a Christian culture where death, judgement, damnation and the afterlife carried profound seriousness, Ferdinand’s fantasy would appear morally monstrous. Webster presents his obsession with death as an extension of patriarchal control: he cannot bear the Duchess’s sexual independence, so he imagines punishing her beyond life.

Webster also presents death as staged illusion before it becomes physical fact. Ferdinand’s use of the dead hand and wax figures creates false death as psychological torture. The prop of the hand is intimate and grotesque: it appears to make Antonio’s body fragmentarily present, while the ring turns marriage into evidence of loss. This is a strikingly theatrical method because Ferdinand becomes a perverted dramatist, staging a scene to manipulate the Duchess’s perception. In revenge tragedy, death is often made spectacular on stage; Webster intensifies the convention by making false death emotionally destructive before real death occurs. The audience is forced to watch death become theatre, and theatre become cruelty.

The masque of madmen continues this conversion of death into atmosphere and spectacle. The Duchess is surrounded by disorder before execution, as if Ferdinand wants her identity to collapse before her body is killed. Yet Webster uses this scene to reveal her strength rather than her weakness. Her insistence that she is “not mad yet” asserts rational control against orchestrated terror. Death’s approach does not reduce her to madness; instead, it clarifies her composure. Webster therefore makes the anticipation of death a test of inward order. Ferdinand’s world is chaotic, but the Duchess’s mind remains lucid.

The Duchess’s execution is presented as a grotesquely corrupted version of a good death. Her line “I know death hath ten thousand several doors / For men to take their exits” gives death a philosophical and theatrical form. The image of “doors” domesticates death, making it a passage rather than annihilation, while “exits” acknowledges the stage itself. The Duchess turns death into a transition that can be faced with composure. In the early modern *ars moriendi* tradition, the quality of a person’s death could reveal spiritual readiness. Webster gives the Duchess clarity, courage and verbal control, making her death ethically superior to the lives of those who kill her.

Her command “Pull, and pull strongly” is one of Webster’s most powerful presentations of death because it gives the Duchess agency at the moment of greatest physical vulnerability. The imperative “Pull” makes her seem to direct the executioners rather than merely submit to them. When she says their strength “Must pull down heaven upon me,” she transforms strangulation into spiritual ascent. The image defeats Ferdinand’s earlier fantasy that he could prevent the Duchess’s smoke from ascending to heaven. He imagines blocking salvation; she imagines heaven descending. Webster therefore presents her death as both bodily defeat and rhetorical victory.

Death is also presented through maternity and domestic tenderness, which prevents the Duchess’s execution from becoming merely emblematic. Her instructions about her children — including the request for syrup for her little boy’s cold and prayers before sleep — place ordinary maternal care beside murder. The detail is painfully practical. It shows that death destroys not only rank and political order but also the intimate rhythms of family life. This matters contextually because the Duchess has been judged by her brothers through patriarchal codes of widowhood, chastity and dynastic control. Webster’s death scene resists that reduction by presenting her as mother, household centre and morally attentive human being.

Bosola’s response after the Duchess’s death shows death as a force of recognition. His statement “Other sins only speak; murder shrieks out” personifies murder as a crime that cannot remain silent. The contrast between “speak” and “shrieks” suggests that murder breaks through the court’s normal culture of concealment. Death produces a sound that cannot be managed politically. When Bosola says “There’s a great spirit gone!”, he finally recognises the Duchess in the terms she deserved while alive. Yet Webster makes this recognition tragic because it is uselessly late. Death teaches Bosola, but it cannot undo his service to death.

The final act presents death as chaotic and unstable rather than providentially neat. Antonio’s death is accidental, occurring in darkness and confusion rather than in heroic confrontation. His line “Oh, I am gone! / Thou hast killed me” is starkly simple, almost anti-climactic. Bosola’s reflection that this is “Such a mistake as I have often seen / In a play” is brutally metatheatrical. Webster exposes the mechanics of revenge tragedy: bodies accumulate, but justice is not cleanly achieved. Death does not restore order; it reveals the broken machinery of a world where intention and consequence no longer align.

Ferdinand and the Cardinal’s deaths further show that death reveals inward corruption. The Cardinal’s desire to be “never thought of” is a final attempt at erasure, fitting for a character whose evil has depended on secrecy, calculation and suppression. Ferdinand’s dying language, especially “Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust,” echoes the Duchess’s earlier moral strength but only after catastrophe. This structural echo is devastating because it suggests that insight has passed from victim to murderer too late to save either. Webster’s final deaths do not offer easy consolation. The court is punished, but the innocent have already been destroyed.

Ultimately, Webster presents death as the play’s deepest instrument of judgement. Death can be degraded into spectacle, fantasy and patriarchal punishment, but it can also reveal dignity, conscience and spiritual endurance. The Duchess’s death is horrifying, yet it is the point at which her identity becomes most secure: “I am Duchess of Malfi still.” By contrast, Ferdinand, the Cardinal and Bosola survive only to expose the corruption and guilt that the court has tried to hide. Webster’s tragedy therefore presents death not as simple closure, but as a force that clarifies value after the world has already destroyed it.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer reaches high Level 5 because it treats death as a whole-play structural concern rather than focusing only on the Duchess’s execution. AO1 is sustained through a conceptual argument about death as spectacle, punishment, moral test and revelation. The answer remains sharply focused on the wording of the question throughout.

AO2 is strong because the answer analyses imagery, props, darkness, staged spectacle, imperatives, declarative speech, personification, metatheatricality, structural echo and revenge-tragedy ending. It repeatedly explains how Webster’s dramatic methods shape audience response.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Duchess and Bosola

Webster presents the Duchess and Bosola’s relationship as a tragic movement from surveillance to belated recognition: Bosola begins by reading and degrading her body for patriarchal power, but her dignity forces him to recognise her spiritual and moral superiority too late.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Death

Webster presents death as both theatrical spectacle and moral judgement: Ferdinand turns death into punishment and control, Bosola is morally awakened by death, and the Duchess transforms death into a final assertion of identity, dignity and spiritual authority.
