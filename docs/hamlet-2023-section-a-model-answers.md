# Model Answer 1 — Grief

## Question

> Explore Shakespeare’s presentation of grief in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents grief in *Hamlet* not as a single emotion but as a destabilising force that exposes the corruption of family, court and self. From the opening court scene, Hamlet’s mourning resists the political demand to forget: his grief is private sorrow, but it is also a moral protest against a world in which death is managed through ceremony, succession and performance. Across the play, Shakespeare contrasts forms of mourning: Hamlet’s inward, philosophical and delayed grief; Ophelia’s silenced and socially unreadable grief; Laertes’ public, explosive and action-driven grief; and Gertrude’s ambiguous grief, which is often displaced into political obedience or maternal anxiety. The tragedy suggests that grief becomes dangerous when it is denied proper expression, corrupted by revenge, or absorbed into a diseased political order.

In the first court scene, Shakespeare immediately makes grief a conflict between authenticity and performance. Claudius’s language attempts to regulate mourning through balanced political rhetoric: he claims to remember King Hamlet with “wisest sorrow” while also celebrating his marriage to Gertrude. The oxymoronic control of “defeated joy” presents grief as something the court converts into statecraft. Hamlet’s response, however, rejects this public management of emotion. When Gertrude tells him to “cast thy nighted colour off”, her imperative treats mourning as costume, as though grief could be removed like a garment. Hamlet’s answer — “Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not ‘seems’” — establishes a central distinction between external show and inward truth. The emphatic monosyllables and antithesis between “seems” and “is” make grief the first sign of Hamlet’s resistance to a court of appearances. In early modern mourning culture, black clothing, funeral rites and public lamentation had recognised social forms; yet Shakespeare makes Hamlet insist that these forms are inadequate because they cannot “denote” the inner reality of loss. His grief is therefore not merely excessive sadness but a claim that the court’s moral language has become false.

Shakespeare deepens this grief in Hamlet’s first soliloquy, where mourning becomes disgust with existence itself. “O that this too too solid flesh would melt” begins with an exclamatory cry that dramatises the pressure of grief on the body. The repeated “too too” suggests excess: Hamlet experiences life as unbearable materiality. Yet Christian doctrine forbids suicide, and Hamlet’s wish that the “Everlasting” had not “fixed / His canon ’gainst self-slaughter” places grief within a theological structure. Shakespeare therefore presents Hamlet’s sorrow as both emotional and spiritual: he is trapped between a longing for dissolution and a religious prohibition against self-destruction. The imagery of an “unweeded garden” that “grows to seed” shifts grief from private mourning to a wider perception of moral decay. Denmark’s corruption is felt first through Hamlet’s bereavement. His father’s death has not simply wounded him; it has changed the way the world appears. This supports a critical reading of Hamlet’s grief as a form of moral perception: he sees rottenness before he can prove it.

The Ghost converts Hamlet’s grief into revenge obligation, intensifying the tragic danger. Before the Ghost speaks, Hamlet’s mourning is melancholic and inward; after the revelation of murder, grief becomes a command to action. The Ghost’s imperative “Remember me” transforms filial mourning into a duty of revenge. Shakespeare’s repetition of memory language is crucial: Hamlet must not merely mourn his father but preserve him against the court’s attempt to forget. In the context of revenge tragedy, the dead father’s demand is conventional, but Shakespeare complicates the convention by making revenge morally and religiously unstable. The Ghost may be a purgatorial spirit, a devilish tempter, or a truthful victim of murder; this ambiguity matters because Hamlet’s grief is asked to become violence before certainty is complete. “The time is out of joint” expresses this burden through metaphor: grief has become cosmic dislocation. Hamlet does not simply miss his father; he feels forced to repair a broken moral universe. This explains why his grief produces delay rather than straightforward revenge. It is not weakness alone, but an awareness that mourning has been contaminated by uncertainty, sin and political corruption.

Shakespeare also presents grief as theatrical and self-questioning, especially through soliloquy and the player episode. In “O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I”, Hamlet compares his own grief with the actor’s imagined emotion for Hecuba. The rhetorical question “What’s Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba” exposes Hamlet’s anxiety that theatrical grief may be more powerful than real grief. The contrast is paradoxical: the player can weep for fiction, while Hamlet, who has genuine cause, remains trapped in language. Shakespeare uses this moment to interrogate the relationship between feeling and performance. Some critics read Hamlet’s grief as excessive melancholy, a disabling inwardness that prevents action; others see his self-accusation as evidence of an ethical consciousness unwilling to reduce grief to revenge spectacle. The play’s metatheatrical method allows both views. Hamlet’s grief is authentic, but it is also fascinated by the problem of how authenticity can be staged, spoken and proven.

Ophelia’s grief offers a devastating contrast because it is socially silenced before it becomes visible as madness. Unlike Hamlet, Ophelia is not granted sustained soliloquy; her grief is fragmented into songs, flowers and disrupted syntax. After Polonius’s death and Hamlet’s rejection, her language breaks into symbolic fragments: songs of death, betrayal and sexual loss. Shakespeare’s dramatic method here is radically different from Hamlet’s philosophical speech. Ophelia’s grief is not organised into argument; it appears as lyrical disintegration. This reflects patriarchal conditions in which female suffering is mediated through male authority. Ophelia has been instructed by Laertes, Polonius and Claudius, but when those structures collapse, her grief has no legitimate public grammar. Her madness may therefore be read as a tragic exposure of what the court has suppressed. A feminist critical reading would emphasise that Ophelia’s grief is not weaker than Hamlet’s but less socially authorised. Hamlet can philosophise, accuse and delay; Ophelia can only sing, distribute flowers and be interpreted by others.

Laertes’ grief functions as a foil to Hamlet’s because it is public, immediate and politically dangerous. His entrance after Polonius’s death brings rebellion into the court: “O heat, dry up my brains” presents grief as physical combustion, obliterating reason. The imperative energy of his language contrasts sharply with Hamlet’s interrogative mode. Where Hamlet asks, debates and stages tests, Laertes demands revenge: “Let come what comes, only I’ll be revenged / Most thoroughly for my father.” Shakespeare makes this decisiveness attractive because it appears to answer the paralysis associated with Hamlet’s mourning. Yet Laertes’ grief is also manipulable. Claudius exploits it, turning filial sorrow into a weapon for court politics. This complicates any simple reading that action is superior to delay. Through Laertes, Shakespeare presents grief that moves swiftly into revenge as morally vulnerable. In revenge tragedy, the demand for blood may appear honourable, but here it becomes a route into poison, treachery and political exploitation.

Gertrude’s grief is more ambiguous, and Shakespeare’s presentation of her forces the audience to confront the difficulty of reading mourning from the outside. Hamlet condemns her remarriage as emotional betrayal — “Frailty, thy name is woman” — but his misogynistic generalisation may reveal his own grief as much as Gertrude’s guilt. Her description of Ophelia’s drowning, “Too much of water hast thou, poor Ophelia”, is one of the play’s most lyrical accounts of death, and its elegiac softness suggests genuine sorrow. Yet Gertrude is also implicated in the court’s desire to moderate or redirect grief. Shakespeare keeps her inwardness partially inaccessible. This dramatic withholding means that Gertrude becomes a test case for Hamlet’s own problem: can grief, love or guilt ever be known with certainty? AO5 debate is especially productive here. Some interpretations see Gertrude as morally evasive, unable to mourn properly because she has chosen political security; others see her as trapped within patriarchal monarchy, attempting survival in a court whose emotional rules are set by men.

The graveyard scene transforms grief by placing it within the material fact of mortality. The skull of Yorick turns mourning into physical encounter with decay. Hamlet’s language moves from personal memory — “I knew him, Horatio” — to universal meditation on death’s levelling power. The prose setting, clownish banter and handling of bones create a tonal mixture that prevents grief from remaining purely solemn. Shakespeare uses the stage object of the skull to make death theatrically concrete: mortality is not abstract doctrine but something held in the hand. When Hamlet leaps into Ophelia’s grave and declares “I loved Ophelia”, grief becomes competitive performance, especially against Laertes. The scene exposes the theatricality of mourning: both men attempt to measure grief through extravagant expression. Yet the grave itself reduces these gestures to mortality. In Christian and Renaissance contexts, the memento mori tradition invited contemplation of death as moral preparation; Shakespeare adapts this tradition to show Hamlet moving from disgust and paralysis towards acceptance.

By the final act, grief has not disappeared, but Hamlet’s relationship to death has altered. “The readiness is all” marks a profound shift from the suicidal desire of the first soliloquy. The aphoristic compression suggests spiritual and tragic composure. Hamlet no longer seeks to melt away, nor does he imagine that he can control the consequences of revenge. Instead, he accepts providence, contingency and mortality. This does not mean grief is resolved in any simple sense. The stage is filled with the deaths produced by unresolved mourning: Ophelia, Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius and Hamlet himself. But Hamlet’s final movement suggests that grief can become readiness when it ceases to demand absolute control. The tragedy’s ending is therefore both destructive and clarifying. Grief exposes corruption, generates revenge, silences the vulnerable, and distorts love; yet it also leads Hamlet towards a final recognition of human limitation.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents grief as the emotional engine of *Hamlet* and as the force through which the play examines truth, performance, revenge, gender and mortality. Hamlet’s mourning begins as resistance to “seeming”, but the play shows that grief cannot remain pure once it enters a corrupt court. It may become melancholy, madness, rebellion, manipulation or philosophical insight. The tragedy of unresolved mourning is that private sorrow becomes inseparable from public disorder. Shakespeare’s achievement is to make grief both intensely personal and structurally tragic: in Elsinore, the dead are not properly mourned, and therefore they do not remain dead.

### AO2

### AO3

Context is integrated rather than bolted on. The answer uses early modern mourning customs, Christian attitudes to suicide, revenge tragedy, patriarchy, monarchy, succession and Renaissance inwardness to illuminate Shakespeare’s dramatic choices.

### AO5

The answer offers debate throughout: Hamlet’s grief as authentic moral perception versus excessive melancholy; Ophelia’s madness as weakness versus silenced grief; Laertes as attractive man of action versus manipulable revenger; Gertrude as evasive versus constrained. These interpretive routes are relevant and controlled.

# Model Answer 2 — Laertes

## Question

> Explore Shakespeare’s presentation of Laertes in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents Laertes as a figure of emotional directness, aristocratic honour and moral vulnerability. Although he is often read as Hamlet’s revenge foil, he is more than a structural contrast: he is a son, brother, courtier and revenger whose values reveal the appeal and danger of decisive action in a corrupt political world. Laertes’ instinct is to act according to the codes of filial duty and masculine honour, but Shakespeare gradually exposes how those codes can be manipulated by Claudius into treachery. His final confession and reconciliation with Hamlet make him tragic rather than merely villainous: he is emotionally sincere, but his sincerity is captured by the corrupt machinery of revenge.

Laertes is first presented as an ambitious young courtier negotiating family duty and aristocratic mobility. His request to return to France situates him within a world of courtly education, travel and masculine advancement. Unlike Hamlet, who is held at Elsinore by Claudius and Gertrude, Laertes is permitted movement. This contrast is significant because it establishes Laertes as socially functional in ways Hamlet is not. He can perform the expected roles of son and courtier without visible crisis. His language to Claudius is formally obedient, suggesting that he understands the etiquette of monarchy and patronage. In the context of an early modern court, where rank, favour and obedience shaped political identity, Laertes appears conventional and adaptable. Shakespeare therefore introduces him as a man at ease within the social structures that Hamlet distrusts.

Laertes’ advice to Ophelia reveals both fraternal concern and patriarchal assumption. He warns her against Hamlet’s love, arguing that Hamlet’s “will is not his own” because he is subject to the “safety and health of this whole state”. Shakespeare’s syntax here places private desire under public obligation: Hamlet cannot love simply as a man because he is also a prince. Laertes shows political intelligence, recognising that royal sexuality is dynastic rather than merely personal. However, his warning also polices Ophelia’s chastity. His imagery of “chaste treasure” and “unmastered importunity” presents female sexuality as property that must be guarded. In early modern patriarchal culture, a woman’s reputation was bound to familial honour; Laertes’ concern is therefore historically intelligible. Yet Shakespeare complicates it through Ophelia’s witty reply that Laertes should not be like a “libertine” preacher who ignores his own counsel. Her response exposes a gendered double standard: Laertes can travel to France and experience sexual freedom, while Ophelia is instructed to preserve chastity as family capital.

This first appearance makes Laertes a foil to Hamlet, but not only in the simple sense that one acts and the other delays. Laertes’ speech is directive and socially assured; Hamlet’s speech is inward, interrogative and philosophically unstable. Laertes thinks within inherited codes: honour, family, reputation and masculine conduct. Hamlet interrogates the reliability of all such codes. Shakespeare uses this contrast structurally to test two versions of tragic masculinity. Laertes’ conventional certainty appears strong at first, especially when compared with Hamlet’s melancholy and delay. But the play later reveals that certainty can be dangerous when it does not pause to examine moral consequence.

Laertes’ return after Polonius’s death radically alters his dramatic function. He enters not as polite courtier but as a public threat to Claudius’s regime. The reported rebellion — the crowd crying “Laertes shall be king” — briefly turns private grief into political crisis. This is crucial to Shakespeare’s presentation of him. Laertes’ grief is not merely emotional; it has public force. His demand, “O thou vile king, / Give me my father”, uses direct address and accusation to collapse the distance between subject and monarch. In a society structured around hierarchy and obedience, this is a dangerous breach of order. Shakespeare stages Laertes as the kind of son Hamlet has imagined he should be: immediate, passionate and willing to confront power. Yet the scene also suggests instability. Laertes’ grief threatens to become rebellion before it becomes justice.

The language of Laertes’ grief is physical and absolute. “O heat, dry up my brains” imagines emotion burning away reason. The imperative “dry up” suggests that he actively invites the destruction of judgement. This is a key moment in Shakespeare’s critique of revenge. Laertes’ grief is sincere, but it defines reason as an obstacle rather than a guide. His later declaration, “To hell, allegiance! vows, to the blackest devil!”, pushes this further. The exclamatory structure and rejection of “allegiance” and “vows” show a man willing to cast off political and religious obligations in the name of filial revenge. In Christian moral terms, this is profoundly dangerous. Revenge tragedy often dramatises the conflict between honour and Christian patience; Laertes embodies the seductive clarity of honour when it overwhelms conscience.

Claudius’s manipulation of Laertes is one of the clearest ways Shakespeare presents him as morally vulnerable. Claudius recognises that Laertes’ grief can be weaponised. His language is calculated, testing whether Laertes loved Polonius “like a son” or merely in appearance. This rhetorical challenge traps Laertes within the performance of masculine honour: to prove grief, he must act. Shakespeare therefore shows how emotional sincerity can be politically exploited. Laertes becomes an instrument of the very king he initially threatens. This is tragically ironic because his desire to avenge his father’s death is redirected by the actual murderer’s successor and manipulator. Although Hamlet killed Polonius, Claudius’s court culture enables the chain of deception that follows. Laertes’ failure is not that he feels too much, but that he allows grief to be defined by Claudius’s corrupt terms.

Laertes’ willingness to use poison marks his moral deterioration. The fencing match should be a public ritual governed by honourable rules, but Claudius and Laertes convert it into disguised assassination. Shakespeare’s imagery of disease and poison connects Laertes to the wider corruption of Denmark. The poisoned rapier becomes a stage object that materialises moral contamination: revenge is no longer open confrontation but secret infection. Laertes’ admission that he will “anoint” his sword with poison gives religiously suggestive language a perverse twist, as though a sacred act has been inverted into murder. In terms of AO5, Laertes can be read as a conventional revenger who exposes Hamlet’s delay; yet Shakespeare also presents him as a warning against revenge ungoverned by ethical reflection. His decisiveness does not purify the court. It reproduces its corruption.

The relationship between Laertes and Ophelia further complicates his presentation. His grief for her is intense, and in the graveyard scene he leaps into her grave in a gesture of theatrical extremity. His command that the earth be piled on him until it makes a mountain is hyperbolic, almost performative. Shakespeare stages grief as rivalry when Hamlet emerges and declares, “I loved Ophelia.” The two men compete to express mourning, turning Ophelia’s burial into a contest of masculine grief. A feminist critical route might argue that Ophelia remains objectified even in death: her suffering becomes a medium through which men display feeling and rivalry. Yet Laertes’ grief should not be dismissed as false. His anguish is real, but Shakespeare shows that even genuine grief can become theatrical, possessive and socially coded.

In the final scene, Laertes’ tragic recognition distinguishes him from a mere antagonist. When the plot turns against him and he is wounded by his own poisoned weapon, he acknowledges the justice of the reversal: “I am justly killed with mine own treachery.” The adverb “justly” is crucial because it signals moral insight. Laertes recognises not only that he has been defeated but that he has become guilty. His final exchange with Hamlet — “Exchange forgiveness with me, noble Hamlet” — restores a moral language absent from the revenge plot. The verb “exchange” suggests reciprocity rather than rivalry. Both men have wronged each other; both are caught in a tragic structure larger than personal intention. In Christian terms, this movement towards confession and forgiveness is significant, though it comes too late to prevent catastrophe.

Shakespeare’s presentation of Laertes therefore asks the audience to judge the value of action itself. Laertes appears to possess what Hamlet lacks: certainty, speed, public courage and emotional immediacy. Yet the play refuses to idealise him. His honour code is socially powerful but ethically unstable; his love for Ophelia is sincere but patriarchal; his filial grief is moving but easily exploited; his revenge is decisive but corrupt. If Hamlet risks paralysis through overthinking, Laertes risks damnation through underthinking. Shakespeare uses this parallel not to make one man simply superior to the other, but to expose the tragic inadequacy of both delay and impulsive revenge in a diseased court.

Ultimately, Laertes is a structurally vital and morally complex figure. He reveals the appeal of decisive revenge while demonstrating its danger. He is not only Hamlet’s foil but a tragic participant in the play’s wider examination of grief, honour, masculinity and corruption. His final confession allows Shakespeare to return from revenge to conscience, suggesting that moral recognition remains possible even at the edge of death. Laertes’ tragedy is that he discovers too late that honour without judgement can become another form of corruption.

## Examiner Commentary

### AO1

This answer maintains a clear conceptual thesis: Laertes is emotionally sincere and socially conventional, but morally vulnerable to manipulation. It tracks him across the whole play without reducing him to a plot device. The argument is controlled, evaluative and expressed with precise terminology.

### AO2

The answer analyses dramatic methods closely, including foil structure, imperative language, public confrontation, hyperbole, staging, poison imagery, dramatic irony, structural parallelism and tragic recognition. It treats Laertes as a theatrical construction, not simply as a character in the plot.

### AO3

Context is used to interpret the play rather than decorate the answer. The response integrates aristocratic honour culture, court politics, patriarchy, female chastity, filial duty, revenge tragedy and Christian morality.

### AO5

Critical debate is sustained: Laertes as conventional revenger, Hamlet’s foil, honour-bound son, morally compromised instrument of Claudius, sincere brother and emotionally manipulable aristocrat. The answer weighs competing interpretations rather than merely listing them.

## AO5 interpretation bank

### Grief

- Hamlet’s grief can be read as authentic moral perception: he sees that the court’s language of order conceals emotional and political corruption.
- Hamlet’s grief can also be read as excessive melancholy: his mourning becomes self-consuming and delays necessary action.
- Grief may be interpreted as paralysis: mourning traps Hamlet in reflection, disgust and theatrical self-surveillance.
- Grief may be interpreted as a route to truth: Hamlet’s refusal of “seeming” allows him to expose the falseness of Elsinore.
- Ophelia’s madness can be read as the consequence of silenced grief within patriarchy.
- Laertes’ grief can be read as a foil for active mourning, but also as proof that action without judgement is corruptible.
- The whole play may be interpreted as a tragedy of unresolved mourning, where the dead are not properly buried emotionally, politically or morally.

### Laertes

- Laertes can be read as a conventional revenge-tragedy revenger, more direct and recognisable than Hamlet.
- Laertes can be read as Hamlet’s foil, exposing Hamlet’s delay and philosophical hesitation.
- Laertes can be read as an honour-bound son whose actions are historically shaped by aristocratic masculinity and filial duty.
- Laertes can be read as a morally compromised instrument of Claudius, allowing sincere grief to become political violence.
- Laertes can be read as emotionally sincere but politically manipulable.
- Laertes reveals both the appeal and danger of decisive action: he acts quickly, but he acts wrongly.
