# Model Answer 1 — Gender Roles

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of gender roles in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents gender roles in *The Duchess of Malfi* as socially enforced performances that the Duchess both inhabits and resists. The play's tragedy is driven by a patriarchal culture that tries to define women as chaste widows, obedient sisters, dynastic vessels and visible symbols of family honour. Against this, the Duchess asserts herself as desiring woman, ruler, wife, mother and moral agent. Webster does not present gender roles as natural; he exposes them as theatrical, political and violent forms of control. The Duchess's challenge to these roles is therefore both intimate and structural: by choosing her own husband, she threatens a whole system of male authority.

The brothers' first warnings establish gender roles as mechanisms of surveillance and sexual discipline. Ferdinand's language is especially revealing. His innuendo that women are like the part which, "like the lamprey, / Hath ne'er a bone in't" reduces female identity to sexual appetite and bodily weakness. The grotesque simile exposes the obscenity within patriarchal protection: Ferdinand claims to guard honour, but his language turns his sister into an object of disgust and fantasy. In a Jacobean context, widows could possess a degree of legal and social independence, but their remarriage remained heavily policed because it affected property, inheritance and family reputation. Webster makes that social anxiety theatrically visible through Ferdinand's excessive rhetoric.

The Duchess immediately challenges the passive gender role assigned to her. Her statement, "The misery of us that are born great — / We are forced to woo because none dare woo us," reveals that aristocratic femininity is a paradox: rank elevates her, but it also isolates her. The passive verb "forced" is crucial. Her initiative is not simply romantic boldness; it is produced by a structure that makes ordinary courtship impossible. When she proposes to Antonio, she reverses expected gender and class positions. "I do here put off all vain ceremony" stages a symbolic removal of aristocratic performance, while "claims you for her husband" gives the Duchess legal and rhetorical authority. Webster therefore makes female agency active, verbal and theatrical.

However, the play also shows that female agency must operate under concealment. The Duchess's marriage is lawful within her chosen moral framework, but it cannot be publicly acknowledged. This secrecy reveals the limits imposed on women by patriarchal reputation. The Duchess's rhetorical question — whether a man who never married would "enjoy / A fairer name in the chronicle" — exposes the gendered double standard. Male reputation is not damaged by remarriage in the same way; female desire is treated as public property. Webster's dramatic method here is argumentative: the Duchess dismantles patriarchal logic from within, using reason against the ideology that seeks to silence her.

The Duchess also resists the ideal of the frozen, sexless widow. Her insistence that she is "flesh and blood" and not an alabaster figure at her husband's tomb rejects the cultural script of perpetual mourning. The antithesis between living body and tomb-monument is a central statement of gender politics in the play. Patriarchy wants to turn the Duchess into a symbol of chastity and family honour; she insists on embodied life. Contextually, early modern conduct expectations often praised female chastity, obedience and constancy, especially in widows of rank. Webster complicates these expectations by presenting the Duchess's remarriage not as moral collapse but as a claim to human fullness.

Webster presents male gender roles as corrupted by the need to dominate. Ferdinand's confrontation with the Duchess after discovering her marriage reveals masculinity as possession, not protection. His claim that she has shaken hands with "Reputation" and made him invisible personifies honour as a male social contract. The Duchess's sexuality, in Ferdinand's imagination, belongs to a world of male reputation from which she has defected. The staging is also significant: Ferdinand enters her private chamber, turning brotherhood into intrusion. Webster exposes patriarchal masculinity as unstable because it cannot tolerate female privacy.

The Cardinal offers a colder version of male power. While Ferdinand's masculinity is fevered and obsessive, the Cardinal's is institutional and hypocritical. His affair with Julia exposes the gendered double standard at the play's centre: male sexual behaviour is managed through secrecy, while female sexuality is punished as scandal. His religious status intensifies the hypocrisy. In a Protestant theatrical culture suspicious of Catholic authority, the Cardinal's role would resonate as institutional corruption, but Webster's broader point concerns gendered impunity. Powerful men can separate public office from private appetite; the Duchess is denied that separation.

The Duchess's language in suffering continues to challenge restrictive gender roles. "Whether I am doomed to live or die, / I can do both like a prince" appropriates masculine sovereignty. The word "prince," rather than princess, matters because it allows her to claim a political and ethical dignity usually coded as male. Later, "I am Duchess of Malfi still" condenses identity into a simple declarative sentence. It refuses the attempt to reduce her to fallen woman, disobedient sister or disgraced widow. Webster makes her final self-definition stronger than the gendered categories used against her.

Motherhood is also presented as a powerful but vulnerable gender role. In the death scene, the Duchess asks that her little boy be given "some syrup for his cold" and that the girl say her prayers before sleep. This domestic register is devastating because it restores ordinary maternal care within a world of political cruelty. Webster refuses to let the Duchess become only a symbol of defiance; she is also a mother whose care is practical, tender and specific. This complicates gender ideology. Her maternal identity is not opposed to her agency; it deepens the human cost of the brothers' attempt to control her.

Ultimately, Webster presents gender roles as socially constructed systems that are most violently defended when they are most fragile. The Duchess's crime, in patriarchal terms, is that she refuses to remain a static emblem of family honour. She speaks, chooses, desires, marries, governs, mothers and dies with self-possession. Male authority responds by surveilling, judging and theatricalising her body. Yet the play's moral centre remains with the woman who resists definition. Webster's tragedy therefore exposes patriarchal gender roles not as natural order, but as a coercive performance sustained by fear of female autonomy.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it treats gender roles as a system of social performance and power, not simply as men versus women. AO1 is sustained through a conceptual thesis and precise paragraph progression. AO2 is developed through close analysis of simile, antithesis, rhetorical question, staging, declarative speech, diction and domestic detail. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean patriarchy, widowhood, inheritance, female chastity, aristocratic reputation, religious authority and gendered double standards.

# Model Answer 2 — Secrets and Lies

## Question

Explore how Webster makes use of secrets and lies in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster makes secrets and lies central to the dramatic structure of *The Duchess of Malfi*. The play is built around hidden marriage, disguised surveillance, false appearances, staged deceptions and delayed revelation. Yet Webster does not present secrecy as automatically immoral. The Duchess's secret marriage is ethically sincere, while the court's lies are coercive and corrupt. This distinction is crucial: Webster uses secrets and lies to expose a society in which truth cannot safely appear in public. In such a world, secrecy may become the condition of love, while official power uses lies to control, punish and destroy.

The Duchess's marriage to Antonio is the play's central secret, and Webster presents it with moral complexity. Her declaration, "I do here put off all vain ceremony," suggests a desire to remove public performance and reach private truth. The marriage scene is intimate, verbally direct and emotionally sincere. Yet the secrecy of the marriage also creates structural danger. In a Jacobean aristocratic context, marriage involved lineage, inheritance, household order and public reputation; a duchess marrying beneath her rank without family sanction would threaten dynastic expectations. Webster therefore creates tension between moral truth and social visibility. The marriage is truthful in intention but dangerous because it must be hidden.

The brothers' world is built on the assumption that female privacy itself is suspicious. The Cardinal's warning that the Duchess's "darkest actions" and even her "privat'st thoughts" will come to light shows how patriarchal power seeks to abolish interiority. The escalation from action to thought is revealing. The court does not merely police what women do; it wants access to desire itself. Secrets are treated as crimes before they are understood. Webster makes this especially sinister because the brothers' own corruption depends on concealment. The public language of honour hides private appetite, hypocrisy and fear.

Bosola is the central instrument through which secrets are converted into evidence. Ferdinand instructs that he be used "for your intelligence," and Bosola later celebrates his "masterpiece of cunning" in making the Duchess "reveal herself." The phrase "reveal herself" is loaded: the Duchess's pregnant body becomes a text that the spy forces into legibility. Webster's dramatic irony is sharp, because Bosola often speaks moral truth about court corruption while acting as the agent of deceit. He is not a simple liar; he is a character whose intelligence is corrupted by service. In the Jacobean patronage system, dependence on powerful patrons could compromise conscience, and Bosola embodies that danger.

The pregnancy plot shows how Webster uses bodily secrecy. The Duchess's equivocal language about being "rid of it" allows her pregnancy to be disguised as illness or physical inconvenience. This is not merely comic deception. It reveals how a woman's body, under surveillance, must speak indirectly. Maternity becomes unspeakable because public acknowledgement would expose the marriage. Webster therefore uses secrecy to show the pressure placed on female embodiment. The Duchess does not lie because she lacks integrity; she lies because the court has made truthful speech impossible.

Ferdinand's deceptions are more destructive because they create false realities. His presentation of what seems to be Antonio's hand, with the Duchess's ring upon it, is a staged lie using props, darkness and emotional memory. The lie works because it corrupts symbols of intimacy. The hand and ring should signify marriage and touch; Ferdinand transforms them into instruments of psychological control. Webster's theatre becomes self-conscious here: lying is staged as a kind of performance, and the audience is made aware of how easily perception can be manipulated. The scene suggests that in a corrupt court, power lies not only by speaking falsely but by controlling what others are allowed to see.

The masque of madmen similarly uses theatrical deception. A masque should be courtly entertainment, ordered and ceremonial, but Ferdinand turns performance into coercion. The Duchess's line, "I account this world a tedious theatre, / For I do play a part in't 'gainst my will," exposes the larger truth beneath the staged lie. Everyone in the court is forced into roles, but the Duchess is most painfully aware of the coercion. Webster therefore uses theatricality to question social identity itself. Secrets and lies are not just plot devices; they reveal the whole court as a system of performance.

The Duchess's integrity is clearest when lies no longer protect her. In the fourth act, she refuses the identities imposed on her. "I am Duchess of Malfi still" is a direct statement of being, stripped of evasion. This line matters because it appears after so much concealment, disguise and manipulation. Webster contrasts the court's complicated lies with the Duchess's final simplicity. Her truth is not dependent on public confirmation. Even when the brothers control her body and circumstances, they cannot control the sentence by which she defines herself.

Bosola's late recognition also turns on the collapse of deception. After the Duchess's death, he declares, "There's a great spirit gone!" The spy who once forced her to reveal herself finally recognises what his methods could not properly read. He had uncovered facts — pregnancy, marriage, vulnerability — but failed to understand truth: the Duchess's moral greatness. Webster thus distinguishes revelation from understanding. Secrets may be exposed, but exposure is not the same as knowledge. This is one of the play's most sophisticated ideas about truth.

The final act shows lies and misunderstandings multiplying into tragic waste. Antonio is killed by mistake, and Bosola's dying line that it is "such a mistake as I have often seen / In a play" makes the drama metatheatrical. Webster points to the machinery of theatrical plotting itself: hidden movement, mistaken identity, darkness and delayed information. The court's habits of secrecy finally make meaningful action almost impossible. Even revenge becomes confused because characters cannot act on stable knowledge.

Ultimately, Webster uses secrets and lies to show a society where truth is morally precious but socially unsafe. The Duchess's secrecy protects love, while the court's lies protect power. Bosola's spying exposes facts but misses meaning; Ferdinand's staged deceptions create suffering; the final act reveals how a world built on concealment collapses into confusion. Webster's tragedy is therefore not simply that people lie, but that a corrupt society makes truth dangerous and then punishes those who conceal it in order to survive.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it distinguishes between ethical secrecy and corrupt lying. AO1 is controlled through a clear conceptual argument about truth, visibility and power. AO2 is developed through close analysis of dramatic irony, disguise, equivocation, props, darkness, stage performance, declarative speech and metatheatre. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean marriage politics, aristocratic lineage, patriarchal surveillance, female chastity, patronage and courtly performance.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Gender Roles

Webster presents gender roles as coercive social performances: the Duchess is expected to remain an obedient sister, chaste widow and dynastic symbol, but she resists by speaking, desiring, marrying, governing, mothering and dying with self-possession.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Secrets and Lies

Webster uses secrets and lies to expose a corrupt society in which truth cannot safely appear: the Duchess's secrecy protects sincere love, while the court's lies and staged deceptions protect power and produce tragic confusion.
