# Model Answer 1 — Imagery and Symbolism

## Question

Explore Webster's use of imagery and symbolism in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster's use of imagery and symbolism in *The Duchess of Malfi* is not decorative; it is the main dramatic language through which the play diagnoses corruption, gendered control, bodily vulnerability and moral disorder. The imagery repeatedly makes hidden structures visible. Court corruption appears as poisoned water, crooked trees and diseased bodies; patriarchal control appears through relics, cages, animals and staged objects; the Duchess's integrity is repeatedly expressed through images of diamonds, light and spiritual endurance. Webster's symbolic method therefore turns the stage into a moral anatomy: the audience is made to see what the court tries to conceal.

The opening imagery establishes corruption as systemic before the plot fully develops. Antonio compares a prince's court to "a common fountain" from which "pure silver drops" should flow, but if a "cursed example poison't near the head," then "Death and diseases through the whole land spread." The metaphor gives the play its first symbolic structure: power is a source, and corruption is a poison that travels downward. The phrase "near the head" links literal source-water to political headship, implying that aristocratic corruption contaminates the whole body politic. In Jacobean political thought, the court was imagined as a centre of order and patronage; Webster reverses that ideal by presenting the court as an infected spring. Imagery here is structural, not incidental: it frames later suffering as the symptom of a diseased system.

Antonio's natural imagery of Ferdinand and the Cardinal as "plum trees that grow crooked over standing pools" extends the same diagnosis. The brothers are "rich" and "o'erladen with fruit," but only "crows, pies, and caterpillars" feed on them. This is an image of fertility corrupted into waste. Fruitfulness should imply public benefit, yet the fruit attracts scavengers and parasites. The adjective "crooked" suggests moral deformation, while "standing pools" imply stagnation. Webster's imagery therefore presents aristocratic wealth as organic but diseased, outwardly abundant but inwardly rotten. The audience is taught to distrust visible magnificence, because in Webster's court, splendour hides decay.

Animal imagery is especially important in Webster's presentation of corruption and control. Bosola imagines himself as a "horse-leech" hanging on the ears of powerful men, reducing court service to blood-sucking dependence. Ferdinand later calls the Duchess an "Excellent hyena" and imagines hunting her "by owl-light." These images do more than insult. They show how the court dehumanises people in order to justify cruelty. Ferdinand's animal imagery often tells the audience more about his own predatory imagination than about the Duchess. The "hyena" image projects deception and monstrosity onto her, but the nocturnal setting and hunting language belong to Ferdinand's own moral darkness. In the context of revenge tragedy and Jacobean fascination with moral monstrosity, Webster uses animals to collapse the boundary between aristocratic refinement and bestial appetite.

Webster also uses religious and monumental imagery to explore control of the Duchess's identity. The Duchess asks why she alone must be "cased up like a holy relic," a simile that exposes reverence as imprisonment. A relic is valued because it is enclosed, preserved and displayed; the Duchess recognises that patriarchal idealisation turns female virtue into containment. This is reinforced by her rejection of being an alabaster figure kneeling at her husband's tomb. The contrast between "flesh and blood" and "alabaster" symbolises the central conflict between living female desire and the static widowhood expected by patriarchal society. In a Jacobean context, aristocratic widows could be praised for chastity and memorial loyalty, but Webster allows the Duchess to challenge that symbolic script.

Jewellery imagery becomes a counter-symbol of female agency. The Duchess's claim that "Diamonds are of most value" when they have passed through many jewellers' hands radically reworks the language of female value. In patriarchal chastity culture, a woman's sexual history could be treated as loss of worth. The Duchess's metaphor reverses that assumption, suggesting that experience may increase value rather than diminish it. Later, "Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust" turns the same symbolic field towards suffering. The diamond is hard, precious and enduring, yet it is shaped by its own substance. Webster therefore uses the diamond both for defiant self-worth and for tragic vulnerability. The Duchess's strength is also the condition of her suffering.

The hand-and-ring episode shows Webster's symbolic stagecraft at its most disturbing. Ferdinand presents what seems to be Antonio's hand, with the Duchess's ring upon it. The hand is a bodily fragment, but it is also a corrupted emblem of marriage. A ring should symbolise union, fidelity and mutual recognition; Ferdinand turns it into a prop of psychological torture. The symbol of intimacy becomes evidence of loss. This is characteristic of Webster's dramatic method: objects on stage are not neutral props but symbolic instruments through which power manipulates perception. The darkness of the scene matters too. Ferdinand controls what the Duchess can see and what she must imagine, turning stage space itself into a symbolic prison.

The masque of madmen develops the symbolism of theatre itself. A masque would normally be associated with elite courtly order, spectacle and controlled harmony, but Ferdinand's "masque of madmen" perverts that aristocratic form into psychological violence. Webster transforms courtly performance into a symbol of social disorder. The theatre within the theatre no longer produces celebration; it produces fear. The Duchess's response — "I account this world a tedious theatre, / For I do play a part in't 'gainst my will" — expands the symbol beyond one scene. The world itself becomes a coercive stage on which identity is forced rather than freely performed. In a Renaissance theatrical context shaped by the idea of the world as a stage, Webster darkens the convention: performance is not human freedom but social entrapment.

Death imagery and body symbolism dominate the fourth act. Bosola's language reduces the body to "worm-seed," "green mummy," "crudded milk" and "fantastical puff-paste." These images draw on memento mori traditions, but Webster makes them aggressive rather than spiritually instructive. Bosola is not guiding the Duchess towards holy reflection; he is trying to degrade her selfhood by defining her body only through decay. The Duchess resists this symbolic reduction through language of identity and dignity. "I am Duchess of Malfi still" becomes powerful precisely because it opposes the decay imagery with a bare declarative sentence. Symbolically, Webster sets bodily decomposition against moral continuity.

The ending extends the play's imagery into cosmic and architectural symbolism. Bosola's claim that human beings are "the stars' tennis-balls" reduces human agency to objects struck by indifferent forces. The image is bleakly playful: cosmic power becomes sport, and human suffering becomes a game. The echo scene, in which the Duchess's voice seems to return with "Thou art a dead thing," turns architecture into a symbol of belated warning. Whether the echo is supernatural, acoustic or psychological, Webster makes space itself speak. Finally, Delio's phrase "this great ruin" symbolises both physical catastrophe and moral collapse. The play ends amid ruins, asking whether meaning can be built out of destruction.

Ultimately, Webster's imagery and symbolism create the play's moral architecture. Poisoned fountains, crooked trees, animals, relics, diamonds, staged objects, masques, decaying bodies, stars and ruins all expose a world where appearance is never innocent. The strongest symbolic contrast is between the court's imagery of decay and the Duchess's language of identity. Webster's tragedy depends on this opposition: corrupt power can manipulate bodies, objects and spectacles, but it cannot fully destroy the Duchess's symbolic force. She becomes, in death, the figure through whom the audience judges the diseased world that destroyed her.

# Model Answer 2 — Features of Revenge Tragedy

## Question

Explore how Webster makes use of features of revenge tragedy in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster makes extensive use of revenge tragedy in *The Duchess of Malfi*, but he also unsettles the genre. The play contains many recognisable revenge-tragedy features: a corrupt court, surveillance, secrecy, violent spectacle, madness, disguise, delayed retaliation, multiple deaths and a final catastrophe. Yet Webster complicates the form by making revenge morally unstable and structurally wasteful. Unlike a straightforward revenge plot, the Duchess is destroyed before the final revenge mechanism fully begins, and Bosola's later attempt at vengeance produces accident as much as justice. Webster therefore uses revenge tragedy to expose a world where violence does not restore order; it multiplies error, guilt and ruin.

The corrupt court is the foundation of the revenge-tragedy world. Antonio's opening image of the court as a poisoned fountain establishes the social disease from which later violence grows. If a "cursed example" poisons the source "near the head," then "Death and diseases through the whole land spread." This is exactly the kind of morally diseased political environment in which revenge tragedy thrives. In early modern revenge drama, courts are frequently places of secrecy, lust, faction and hidden violence. Webster follows that convention, but he makes corruption feel systemic rather than merely individual. The Aragonian court does not simply contain villains; it produces the conditions in which revenge becomes thinkable.

Bosola fulfils the revenge-tragedy role of the malcontent, although Webster makes him more ethically conflicted than a simple instrument of revenge. His bitterness about patronage, his disgust with courtiers and his vivid moral commentary mark him as an outsider-insider: he serves the court while despising it. His "horse-leech" simile presents court service as parasitic dependence, and his later image of the politician as "the devil's quilted anvil" shows his ability to diagnose concealed evil. In revenge-tragedy terms, Bosola is the commentator on corruption; however, Webster complicates the role by making him complicit. His insight does not prevent his participation in surveillance and murder.

Webster also uses secrecy and surveillance, central features of revenge tragedy, to drive the tragic action. Ferdinand hires Bosola "for your intelligence," and Bosola later calls his discovery of the Duchess's pregnancy his "masterpiece of cunning." Revenge tragedy often depends on plots, counterplots and hidden knowledge, but Webster gives surveillance a specifically gendered force. The Duchess's private marriage and pregnant body become objects of male investigation. This makes the revenge structure inseparable from patriarchy. Ferdinand does not revenge a political crime against himself; he punishes female autonomy as though it were a crime against dynastic honour. Contextually, this draws on early modern anxieties about widowhood, inheritance, female chastity and aristocratic bloodline.

Madness and psychological excess are also central to Webster's use of revenge tragedy. Ferdinand's language often becomes excessive before his mind openly collapses. His fantasy that the Duchess and Antonio should be burned so that their smoke cannot "ascend to heaven" extends revenge beyond death into damnation. This is revenge tragedy at its most grotesque: punishment is imagined not as justice but as total spiritual domination. Later, Ferdinand's "masque of madmen" transforms courtly entertainment into psychological torture. Webster uses the spectacle expected of revenge drama, but he directs it inward. The horror is not only the sight of suffering but the staging of mental destruction.

The hand-and-ring episode is a particularly powerful example of revenge-tragedy stagecraft. Ferdinand shows the Duchess what appears to be Antonio's hand, with her ring on it. The use of props, darkness and bodily fragments belongs to the sensational theatre of revenge tragedy, but Webster uses the device to create emotional and perceptual torture rather than straightforward horror. The hand is symbolic as well as visually shocking: it turns the marriage token into a false sign of death. The audience is made to watch how revenge manipulates reality before it destroys the body. Webster's revenge tragedy is therefore theatrical in a highly self-conscious way.

The Duchess's death scene both fulfils and challenges revenge-tragedy expectations. The executioners, disguises, memento mori language and strangling all create an atmosphere of ritualised violence. Bosola's instruction to the executioners to "put on your vizards" makes death a costumed performance, collapsing execution and theatre. Yet the Duchess's composure resists the genre's degradation of the victim. "I am Duchess of Malfi still" asserts identity against the machinery of revenge. Her command that the executioners "pull down heaven" upon her transforms strangling into spiritual ascent. In the context of the early modern good death tradition, Webster gives the victim a dignity that exceeds the revenge plot trying to consume her.

Bosola's moral turn after the Duchess's death creates a second revenge trajectory, but it is belated and contaminated. His statement that "Other sins only speak; murder shrieks out" suggests that murder has awakened conscience. He now becomes an avenger for the Duchess, Antonio, Julia and finally himself. However, Webster refuses to make this revenge clean. Bosola's guilt is too deep, and his actions too late, for simple moral restoration. His role as avenger is compromised because he was first the instrument of the earlier crime. This is a major way Webster revises revenge tragedy: the avenger is not morally pure but implicated in the wrong he punishes.

The final act intensifies this critique through accident and confusion. Antonio's death is especially important. He dies by mistake, with the brief cry, "Oh, I am gone! / Thou hast killed me." This is not satisfying revenge justice; it is waste. Bosola's later recognition that the mistake resembles something he has often seen "in a play" is a remarkable metatheatrical comment on revenge tragedy itself. Webster seems to expose the genre's machinery from within. The accumulation of bodies may look like revenge-tragedy closure, but the deaths do not produce proportionate justice. They reveal the absurdity and cost of violent plotting.

The deaths of Ferdinand and the Cardinal also fulfil revenge-tragedy expectation while refusing moral neatness. The Cardinal's question — whether death is real or "feigning" — fits a world where performance has so corrupted identity that even dying may be suspected as acting. Ferdinand's dying echo of the Duchess's diamond image, "Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust," creates a structural rhyme between victim and persecutor. Revenge tragedy often ends with recognition, but here recognition arrives too late and is shared by the guilty as well as the innocent. Knowledge does not save; it only clarifies catastrophe.

Delio's final attempt to make "noble use" of the "great ruin" gives the play a conventional closing gesture, but Webster leaves that closure uneasy. Revenge tragedy often ends with a restored order after bloodshed, yet *The Duchess of Malfi* offers only a child heir and a moral sentence that cannot erase the preceding waste. Bosola's fatalistic image of human beings as "the stars' tennis-balls" also resists a providential reading. If the stars strike humans where they please, then revenge is less divine justice than cosmic sport. Webster's ending therefore both uses and interrogates revenge-tragedy convention.

Ultimately, Webster makes use of revenge tragedy by adopting its most recognisable features — corrupt court, malcontent, secrecy, madness, spectacle, disguise and final catastrophe — while exposing their moral inadequacy. The genre gives the play its machinery, but Webster uses that machinery to reveal the failure of revenge to restore justice. The Duchess's dignity stands outside and above the revenge plot, while Bosola's revenge arrives too late and kills wrongly. The result is a tragedy in which revenge does not heal corruption; it becomes one more form of corruption's spread.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Imagery and Symbolism

Webster uses imagery and symbolism to make hidden corruption visible: poisoned fountains, crooked trees, animals, relics, diamonds, staged objects, masques and ruins create a symbolic structure in which the Duchess's integrity is set against a diseased and theatrical court.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Revenge Tragedy

Webster uses the machinery of revenge tragedy — corrupt court, malcontent, secrecy, madness, spectacle, disguise and final catastrophe — but critiques the genre by showing revenge as belated, contaminated and unable to restore justice.
