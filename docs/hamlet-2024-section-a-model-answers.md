# Model Answer 1 — Hamlet and Ophelia

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents the relationship between Hamlet and Ophelia in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents the relationship between Hamlet and Ophelia as a private emotional bond made increasingly impossible by the public structures of Elsinore: patriarchy, surveillance, dynastic anxiety and revenge. The tragedy of their relationship is not simply that Hamlet is cruel or that Ophelia is victimised, although both are true at points. Rather, Shakespeare dramatises a relationship in which intimacy is converted into evidence, sexuality into political risk, and language into performance. Ophelia’s obedience is not mere passivity, but a constrained form of survival in a court where father, brother and prince all script her behaviour. Hamlet’s treatment of her, meanwhile, moves between genuine emotional injury, misogynistic projection and strategic theatricality. By the end of the play, their relationship has become one of the clearest casualties of a corrupt court: love is not absent, but it is rendered structurally uninhabitable.

Shakespeare first establishes Hamlet and Ophelia’s relationship through absence and report, which immediately makes it unstable. The audience never sees their courtship directly. Instead, it is mediated through Laertes’ warning, Polonius’ suspicion and Ophelia’s careful recollection. This off-stage pre-history matters because the relationship is already interpreted before it is dramatised. Laertes frames Hamlet’s affection as aristocratic danger, telling Ophelia that Hamlet’s “will is not his own”, since his sexuality is bound to the state. The phrase reduces Hamlet from private lover to public prince: he cannot simply choose. In early modern aristocratic culture, marriage among the elite carried political and dynastic consequences, and Ophelia’s chastity is presented as a family asset to be guarded. Polonius is more brutal. His imperatives — “Affection? pooh!” and “Think yourself a baby” — infantilise Ophelia and strip her of interpretive authority over her own experience. Shakespeare therefore places the relationship inside a patriarchal grammar before Hamlet and Ophelia meet on stage together.

Yet Ophelia is not presented as intellectually empty. Her reply, “I do not know, my lord, what I should think”, is often read as submissive, but its syntax is more complex. The hesitation does not prove stupidity; it exposes a culture in which a young woman’s thinking must be authorised by a father. The modal “should” suggests social obligation rather than intellectual incapacity. Shakespeare makes Ophelia’s obedience dramatically legible as constrained agency: she knows how to survive in a world where female reputation is fragile and male authority is coercive. Her later “I shall obey, my lord” is devastating because it is both dutiful and self-effacing. It anticipates the way her relationship with Hamlet will be managed by others until it becomes impossible to distinguish her own voice from the roles imposed upon her.

Hamlet’s first major language about women also contaminates the relationship before Ophelia appears with him. In the first soliloquy, his grief at Gertrude’s remarriage expands into the misogynistic generalisation “Frailty, thy name is woman”. The abstract noun “Frailty” turns personal disillusionment into a universal judgement, while the apostrophe gives misogyny the force of definition. This is crucial for the Hamlet-Ophelia relationship: Ophelia becomes, in part, the recipient of a disgust generated elsewhere. Shakespeare presents Hamlet’s emotional world as damaged by bereavement, religious horror and sexual revulsion. A feminist reading might argue that Ophelia is punished for Gertrude’s perceived transgression; a more sympathetic reading might stress that Hamlet’s cruelty emerges from psychic collapse. The play refuses to make either interpretation sufficient. Hamlet is wounded, but his wounds do not absolve the violence of his language.

The “nunnery” scene is the central dramatic testing-ground of the relationship because it fuses love, surveillance and theatrical performance. Ophelia enters as an agent of male observation: Polonius and Claudius hide and use her as bait to diagnose Hamlet. This staging is essential AO2 evidence. The relationship is not allowed privacy. Shakespeare turns the stage itself into a mechanism of surveillance, so that Hamlet and Ophelia’s dialogue occurs under invisible pressure. Ophelia attempts a formal return of Hamlet’s “remembrances”, using the language of courtly exchange, but Hamlet destabilises the encounter with interrogation: “Are you honest?” and “Are you fair?” The repeated questions fracture ordinary romantic dialogue into a moral inquest. “Honest” carries the early modern sense of chastity as well as truthfulness, so Hamlet’s language exposes the gendered suspicion attached to female sexuality.

The command “Get thee to a nunnery” is especially ambiguous. Literally, “nunnery” may imply a convent, a space of chastity and withdrawal from corrupt sexuality; bawdily, it can suggest a brothel. Shakespeare exploits this doubleness to make Hamlet’s speech both protective and degrading. A critical route that sees Hamlet as trying to protect Ophelia from the corrupt court is possible, especially if the line is performed with desperation rather than contempt. However, the violence of the repeated imperative cannot be ignored. Hamlet denies Ophelia stable meaning: she cannot know whether she is being rejected, warned, insulted or tested. His broken contradictions — “I did love you once” followed by “I loved you not” — dramatise emotional incoherence as cruelty. The antithetical structure does not simply communicate uncertainty; it wounds. Ophelia is forced to receive Hamlet’s psychological conflict as public humiliation.

Ophelia’s response after the nunnery scene demonstrates her interpretive intelligence. “O, what a noble mind is here o’erthrown” is not merely sentimental lament. The balanced catalogue of “courtier’s, soldier’s, scholar’s” shows that she understands Hamlet’s identity in social, political and intellectual terms. The noun “mind” also locates the tragedy inside consciousness: Hamlet’s collapse is not only romantic but philosophical and princely. Ophelia recognises the destruction of an ideal Renaissance subject. Her final self-reference — “And I, of ladies most deject and wretched” — places her own suffering within his fall, but the line does not erase her. Instead, Shakespeare gives her an elegiac authority: she sees what the spying men fail to see. Polonius reduces Hamlet’s condition to love-melancholy; Ophelia understands that something more profound has been “o’erthrown”.

The play scene intensifies Hamlet’s public sexual aggression. When Hamlet asks, “Lady, shall I lie in your lap?”, Shakespeare turns performance into social exposure. The bawdy pun on “lie” collapses theatrical lying, sexual innuendo and misogynistic provocation. Ophelia’s brief replies — “No, my lord” and “I think nothing, my lord” — show her trapped inside public decorum. Hamlet’s obscene play on “nothing”, an early modern sexual pun, converts her silence and chastity into a target. Dramatically, this is not a private lovers’ quarrel; it happens in front of the court, during a metatheatrical trap designed to expose Claudius. Ophelia becomes collateral damage in Hamlet’s pursuit of truth. An AO5 debate is useful here: some may argue that Hamlet performs antic misogyny to maintain his disguise; others that the performance releases genuine hostility. Shakespeare’s method makes the distinction unstable. Performance in *Hamlet* does not conceal truth neatly; it often reveals what characters would otherwise repress.

Ophelia’s madness after Polonius’ death transforms her from obedient daughter into a disruptive theatrical presence. Her songs are fragmented, bawdy and allusive: “Tomorrow is Saint Valentine’s day” introduces sexual betrayal into courtly space, while “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance” turns flowers into symbolic accusation. Song becomes a dramatic method of indirect speech. In a patriarchal court where Ophelia’s rational speech has been managed, madness allows a dangerous form of truth-telling. It would be reductive to call this simple liberation, because her madness is also a breakdown produced by bereavement, abandonment and coercion. However, Shakespeare makes madness theatrically powerful. Her fragments expose what the court suppresses: death, desire, broken promises and female vulnerability. Critics often read Ophelia as the victim of patriarchal silencing; the play supports this, but it also gives her mad speech a symbolic authority that sane obedience denied her.

The circumstances of Ophelia’s death deepen the ambiguity of her relationship with Hamlet and with the society that destroys her. Gertrude’s account of Ophelia falling into the brook is lyrical, almost aestheticised, but the beauty of the description cannot erase the possibility of suicide. The “fantastic garlands” and “snatches of old lauds” suggest a tragic fusion of innocence, ritual and disordered consciousness. Early modern Christian doctrine treated suicide as spiritually dangerous, which explains the later dispute about her burial rites. Ophelia’s death therefore becomes both personal catastrophe and theological problem. Hamlet is absent from this process. His absence is dramatically important: the relationship has been so broken by the revenge plot that he does not witness the full consequence of the emotional world he helped destroy.

In the graveyard, Hamlet’s declaration “I loved Ophelia” is both moving and troubling. The past tense acknowledges a genuine emotional bond, but the competitive hyperbole — “Forty thousand brothers / Could not, with all their quantity of love, / Make up my sum” — turns grief into masculine rivalry with Laertes. The mathematical language of “quantity” and “sum” is emotionally excessive yet strangely performative. Hamlet can only declare love fully when Ophelia can no longer answer. Shakespeare thereby makes absence one of the relationship’s final dramatic methods. Ophelia’s silence in death mirrors, but intensifies, her earlier silencing in life. The audience is left to judge whether Hamlet’s claim is recovered truth, belated self-dramatisation or both.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents Hamlet and Ophelia’s relationship as genuine but structurally doomed. It is not adequate to describe it as only romance, only cruelty or only political exploitation. The relationship begins in reported intimacy, is disciplined by patriarchal authority, corrupted by surveillance, violated by Hamlet’s misogynistic and theatrical language, and finally resolved only in death and absence. Ophelia is a victim, but not an empty one: her obedience, insight, silence and madness all reveal the gendered violence of Elsinore. Hamlet is cruel, but not merely cruel: his treatment of Ophelia emerges from grief, disgust, strategic performance and moral disorientation. Shakespeare’s tragic achievement is to show how a corrupt court makes private love impossible. In *Hamlet*, intimacy cannot survive when every human bond is turned into evidence, performance or political risk.

The line of argument remains sharply focused on the relationship rather than drifting into a general Ophelia essay.

### AO5

Interpretations are weighed rather than name-dropped. The response repeatedly shows that Shakespeare keeps motives unstable, which is especially valuable for Level 5.

# Model Answer 2 — Doubt and Confusion

## Question

> Explore the extent to which Shakespeare’s Hamlet is a play full of doubt and confusion. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

To a large extent, *Hamlet* is a play full of doubt and confusion, but Shakespeare presents uncertainty as more than weakness or indecision. Doubt is the play’s ethical, theatrical and metaphysical condition. Hamlet doubts the Ghost, Claudius, Gertrude, Ophelia, himself, revenge, death and even the possibility of knowledge. Yet the play does not simply celebrate confusion. Shakespeare also shows characters trying to manufacture certainty through surveillance, performance, religious reasoning and violence. The tragedy is that these attempts either fail or arrive too late. By the final act, Hamlet reaches a kind of providential acceptance, but this is not the same as complete clarity. The ending produces action, not full understanding. Shakespeare therefore makes *Hamlet* a tragedy in which doubt is both destructive paralysis and moral intelligence.

The play opens in uncertainty. The first line, “Who’s there?”, is a question of identity, perception and authority. Before Hamlet appears, Shakespeare establishes a world in which sight is unreliable and political order feels unstable. The Ghost’s appearance generates confusion because it is both visually compelling and theologically ambiguous. In an early modern context shaped by Protestant suspicion of Purgatory and Catholic traditions of the returning dead, the Ghost cannot be read simply as a truthful messenger. It may be the spirit of Hamlet’s father, but it may also be demonic temptation. Shakespeare dramatises this uncertainty through the Ghost’s liminal staging: it appears at night, in armour, between life and death, speech and silence. The audience shares Hamlet’s epistemological problem: the Ghost seems to reveal truth, yet its status remains doubtful.

Hamlet’s response to the Ghost shows that doubt can be ethical rather than cowardly. He initially accepts the command to revenge, but later reflects, “The spirit that I have seen / May be a devil”. The modal verb “may” is crucial: Hamlet’s delay begins in theological caution. A revenge hero in a conventional revenge tragedy might proceed directly to bloodshed, but Shakespeare gives Hamlet a conscience formed by Christian anxieties about sin, damnation and spiritual deception. The Ghost’s demand for revenge conflicts with Christian teaching against private vengeance. This produces a tragic contradiction: to obey the father may endanger the soul; to refuse may betray filial duty. A critical interpretation that sees Hamlet as paralysed by excessive thought is persuasive, but incomplete. His doubt also marks a serious moral intelligence in a world where certainty may be spiritually dangerous.

The court itself is full of confusion because appearance and reality have become politically severed. Claudius’ opening speech performs stability through balanced public rhetoric, but its polished antitheses — “With mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage” — reveal moral disorder beneath ceremonial control. The oxymoronic structure compresses incompatible rituals into one political performance. Hamlet’s reply to Gertrude, “Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not ‘seems’”, establishes his obsession with the gap between external show and inward truth. The repetition of “seems” turns a courtly word into an ethical problem. However, Shakespeare complicates Hamlet’s position: the character who rejects seeming will soon adopt “antic disposition”. The play is full of doubt partly because no one can stand wholly outside performance.

Madness is one of Shakespeare’s most sustained methods for producing confusion. Hamlet’s antic behaviour is announced as strategy, but its boundaries are unstable. Polonius reads it as love-melancholy, Claudius suspects political danger, Gertrude sees grief, and Ophelia experiences emotional violence. Shakespeare uses dramatic irony because the audience knows Hamlet has chosen to perform madness, but the performance becomes so intense that it risks exceeding control. The broken logic, riddling insults and abrupt tonal shifts of Hamlet’s speech make the audience question whether performance reveals psychological truth. In early modern culture, melancholy was associated with intellectual refinement but also with disorder and spiritual danger. Hamlet’s madness therefore cannot be reduced to either fake or real. Shakespeare uses it to blur the line between theatrical role and inner collapse.

Polonius embodies a comic but dangerous form of interpretive confusion. He believes he can decode Hamlet through surveillance and aphoristic wisdom, but his readings are repeatedly reductive. His claim that “Your noble son is mad” and his confidence that rejected love explains Hamlet’s behaviour turn complex political and metaphysical disorder into a single cause. Shakespeare’s dramatic method here is satirical: Polonius’ prolixity and self-importance expose the inadequacy of bureaucratic interpretation. Yet his confusion has tragic consequences. By using Ophelia as bait and hiding behind the arras, he helps turn doubt into surveillance. Elsinore becomes a state in which uncertainty is not discussed openly but investigated covertly. This is politically significant: in a court anxious about legitimacy and succession, knowledge is treated as power.

The Mousetrap is Hamlet’s most explicit attempt to convert doubt into certainty. “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King” is a metatheatrical thesis for the whole drama. Hamlet uses theatre as an epistemological instrument: performance will reveal reality. The rhyming couplet gives the plan a sudden formal confidence, while the verb “catch” suggests both moral exposure and entrapment. Shakespeare here reflects Renaissance interest in theatre’s ability to mirror human truth, but he also complicates that confidence. Claudius’ reaction seems to confirm guilt, yet it still requires interpretation. The audience may feel a movement towards certainty, but the certainty is theatrical, not legal or theological. Hamlet has evidence of conscience, not a confession in court. Shakespeare therefore allows drama to expose truth while preserving the instability of how truth is known.

The “To be, or not to be” soliloquy broadens doubt from the political to the metaphysical. Its opening antithesis reduces existence itself to a grammatical alternative, but the infinitive “to be” is abstract, impersonal and strangely detached. Hamlet does not say “shall I live?”; he turns selfhood into philosophical structure. The soliloquy is full of rhetorical questions and balanced oppositions: suffering or action, endurance or resistance, life or death. Yet every alternative generates further uncertainty. The “undiscovered country” makes death unknowable, and “conscience does make cowards of us all” suggests that thought inhibits action. Contextually, the speech engages Christian fears of suicide and damnation, but it also reflects Renaissance scepticism: human reason can question everything except the thing most desired, final knowledge. The soliloquy makes doubt universal, not merely personal.

The prayer scene shows the destructive consequences of uncertain reasoning. Hamlet encounters Claudius apparently at prayer and refuses to kill him because he fears sending him to heaven: “Now might I do it pat”. The adverb “pat” suggests perfect opportunity, but the moment dissolves into theological calculation. Hamlet’s logic depends on assumptions about repentance, salvation and timing, all of which are uncertain. Dramatic irony intensifies the scene because Claudius admits that his “words fly up” but his “thoughts remain below”; he has not truly repented. Hamlet’s doubt therefore causes him to miss the moment when killing Claudius would not, by his own logic, save the king’s soul. Shakespeare makes uncertainty tragic not because Hamlet thinks, but because thought in a morally unstable universe produces misrecognition.

Ophelia’s madness offers another form of doubt and confusion, but unlike Hamlet’s it is not strategic. Her fragmented songs create interpretive instability: they seem to refer to Polonius’ death, Hamlet’s rejection, sexual betrayal and corrupted innocence, but they resist complete decoding. Shakespeare uses song as broken testimony. Ophelia’s language is confused, yet it may also be truthful precisely because it escapes courtly control. A feminist critical reading might argue that Ophelia’s madness exposes the costs of patriarchal silencing. She has been instructed, watched and used; madness becomes the only form in which the court hears the disorder it has created. Thus confusion is not evenly distributed in the play. Hamlet’s doubt is philosophical and theatrical; Ophelia’s confusion is embodied, gendered and socially produced.

Gertrude also contributes to the play’s uncertainty because Shakespeare keeps her moral knowledge ambiguous. Does she know about the murder? The play never gives a definitive answer. In the closet scene, Hamlet’s violent rhetoric forces her into recognition — “Thou turn’st mine eyes into my very soul” — but recognition here concerns sexual and moral corruption rather than explicit complicity in murder. The scene’s staging, with Polonius hidden behind the arras and the Ghost visible only to Hamlet, multiplies confusion. To Hamlet, the Ghost confirms purpose; to Gertrude, Hamlet may appear mad. Shakespeare makes the audience inhabit both possibilities. The closet scene is therefore a dramatic centre of uncertainty: maternal sexuality, political guilt, spiritual visitation and madness converge without being fully resolved.

By Act 5, the play appears to move from confusion towards acceptance. In the graveyard, Hamlet confronts mortality through physical evidence: Yorick’s skull. The earlier abstractions of “To be, or not to be” become material fact. The skull collapses social distinction: lawyer, courtier, jester and conqueror all return to dust. Yet this is not simple clarity. The graveyard scene also contains mistaken identity, delayed recognition and emotional excess at Ophelia’s grave. Hamlet understands mortality more deeply, but he still does not fully understand the damage surrounding him. His later statement that “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” suggests a turn towards providential thinking. In Christian terms, Hamlet begins to accept that human agency operates within a larger design.

However, the final acceptance — “The readiness is all” — should not be mistaken for the removal of doubt. The sentence is aphoristic and calm, but it accepts uncertainty rather than solving it. Hamlet does not know the outcome of the duel, the full plot against him, or the precise shape of providence. What changes is his relation to uncertainty. Earlier doubt produces delay, self-division and theatrical testing; later doubt becomes readiness. Some critics read this as Hamlet’s spiritual maturation, a movement from sceptical paralysis to providential trust. Others see it as fatalism: Hamlet submits to a violent plot he only partially understands. Shakespeare leaves both readings available. The ending delivers action, but it does not restore moral or political order without cost.

The final scene produces partial clarification through catastrophe. Claudius is exposed, Gertrude’s death reveals the poisoned cup, Laertes confesses the plot, and Hamlet finally kills the king. Yet this clarity is wasteful and belated. Nearly everyone who matters must die before truth becomes public. Fortinbras’ arrival imposes political order, but from outside Denmark, not from the moral repair of Elsinore itself. Hamlet’s final request that Horatio “report me and my cause aright” shows that even after the action ends, interpretation remains necessary. The play closes not with full certainty but with the need for narrative explanation.

Therefore, *Hamlet* is to a very large extent a play full of doubt and confusion, but Shakespeare’s presentation of uncertainty is double-edged. Doubt delays revenge, damages relationships and contributes to catastrophic waste. Yet doubt also prevents the play from becoming a crude revenge fantasy. It makes Hamlet ethically serious, theatrically self-conscious and metaphysically profound. Confusion is built into the Ghost, the court, madness, sexuality, conscience, death and political succession. The ending offers readiness and exposure, but not complete resolution. Shakespeare’s tragedy suggests that in a fallen world of performance, surveillance and spiritual uncertainty, certainty may only arrive when it is too late to save anyone.

## Examiner Commentary

### AO1

This is a high-Level-5 response because it directly addresses “extent to which” and sustains a nuanced argument: the play is full of doubt and confusion, but doubt is both destructive and ethically intelligent. The answer is conceptual rather than list-based, and it tracks the whole play through a coherent line of development from Ghostly uncertainty to final readiness.

### AO2

AO2 is consistently foregrounded through analysis of Shakespeare’s methods: opening interrogative, soliloquy, rhetorical questioning, antithesis, oxymoron, dramatic irony, metatheatre, delayed tragic structure, surveillance staging, madness, song, graveyard symbolism and final aphorism. The answer treats *Hamlet* as drama, not as a novel, and repeatedly links language to staging and form.

### AO3

Context is used precisely: Protestant/Catholic uncertainty around the Ghost and afterlife, Christian conscience and damnation, revenge tragedy conventions, Renaissance scepticism, early modern melancholy, patriarchy, monarchy and court surveillance. The contextual material explains why doubt matters in the play’s world.

### AO5

The answer integrates debate throughout. It weighs Hamlet as paralysed thinker against Hamlet as morally responsible sceptic; the Ghost as truth or temptation; madness as performance or breakdown; the ending as providential resolution or fatalistic waste. Interpretations are embedded in the argument rather than presented as detachable critic references.

- *Hamlet* as a tragedy of uncertainty: useful whole-play frame.

- Hamlet as morally responsible because he doubts: stronger, more nuanced argument.
- The Ghost as truth, temptation or theatrical catalyst: excellent AO5 route.
- Doubt as weakness: valid if balanced with ethical caution.
- Doubt as intelligence: strong route for Level 5.
- The ending as providential resolution: useful for “divinity” and “readiness”.
- The ending as political and existential waste: useful for the deaths and Fortinbras’ arrival.
