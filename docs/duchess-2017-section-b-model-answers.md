# Model Answer 1 — A Corrupted Court

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of a corrupted court in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents the court in *The Duchess of Malfi* as a corrupted social organism: its moral disease begins at the top, spreads through patronage and surveillance, and finally destroys even those who try to exploit it. The court is not merely a backdrop for individual villainy. It is a structure that rewards hypocrisy, converts service into dependency, and turns honour into a language of control. Through images of poison, predation, disease and theatrical performance, Webster exposes a world in which political authority has lost ethical purpose. The Duchess's tragedy is therefore not simply caused by her brothers' cruelty, but by a court culture already so diseased that genuine integrity appears almost impossible to sustain.

Webster establishes the court's corruption immediately through Antonio's famous fountain metaphor. A prince's court, he says, "Is like a common fountain" from which "pure silver drops" should flow, but if a "cursed example poison't near the head," then "Death and diseases through the whole land spread." The image is structurally important because it makes corruption systemic before the plot has fully begun. The court should purify the state, yet Webster's conditional syntax imagines that the source itself has been poisoned. The phrase "near the head" also works politically, suggesting that corruption descends from those in authority. In a Jacobean context, where the court was understood as the visible centre of political favour, hierarchy and national order, this image would carry sharp force. Webster presents courtly corruption not as isolated moral failure but as contamination within the body politic.

Antonio's description of Ferdinand and the Cardinal develops this diagnosis through grotesque natural imagery. The brothers are "like plum trees that grow crooked over standing pools," rich and "o'erladen with fruit," but feeding only "crows, pies, and caterpillars." The simile is rich in contradiction: the trees are fruitful, but their fruit nourishes scavengers and vermin. Webster therefore presents aristocratic wealth as materially abundant but morally sterile. The adjective "crooked" suggests deformation at the root, while "standing pools" evoke stagnation rather than movement. Courtly status produces no civic benefit; it feeds parasitic creatures. This kind of imagery belongs to a wider early modern habit of linking moral disorder with physical and natural corruption. The court is unnatural because it has lost the reciprocal duty that should bind rank to service.

Bosola's entrance intensifies Webster's presentation of court corruption by showing how patronage deforms the servant as well as the master. Bosola bitterly observes that if he were one of their "flattering panders," he would hang on their ears "like a horse-leech" until full, then drop off. The simile reduces court service to blood-sucking dependence, exposing a world where advancement requires parasitism. Yet Bosola's insight does not save him: he accepts Ferdinand's commission and becomes the very instrument of corruption he despises. Webster's dramatic irony is severe. Bosola can diagnose the court accurately but cannot escape its economic logic. In the Jacobean patronage system, service to powerful aristocrats could offer advancement, but it also risked moral compromise. Bosola embodies that danger: corruption is not only wickedness but dependency.

The Cardinal's role reveals the court's institutional corruption, especially where political and religious power intersect. Antonio compares his use of law to "a foul black cobweb to a spider," in which he makes both "dwelling" and "prison" to entangle those who feed him. The metaphor compresses domestic security and predatory entrapment into one image. Law should protect, yet here it becomes the means by which power feeds on others. The Cardinal's identity as a churchman deepens the hypocrisy. In a Protestant English theatrical culture often suspicious of Catholic authority and Italianate corruption, a corrupt Cardinal would carry immediate ideological charge. However, Webster's critique is not merely sectarian. The Cardinal's function is to show that institutions of justice and religion can become theatrical costumes for appetite.

Ferdinand represents a more fevered and psychological version of court corruption. His command that Bosola be employed "for your intelligence" makes surveillance foundational to court life. The noun "intelligence" means information, but it also hints at a culture of hidden observation and strategic performance. The court becomes a space in which nobody is allowed a private self. This is crucial to the Duchess's tragedy: her marriage is not discovered by justice, but hunted by espionage. Webster links this surveillance to aristocratic control over female sexuality. Ferdinand's later obsession with the Duchess does not protect the court from scandal; it produces the very moral ugliness it claims to punish. Court honour is exposed as a mask for voyeurism, misogyny and ownership.

Webster's presentation of corruption is especially powerful because the Duchess's private integrity is surrounded by theatrical cruelty. Ferdinand's plan to send a "masque of madmen" turns courtly entertainment into psychological torture. The masque was associated with elite performance, ceremony and aristocratic display; Webster perverts it into an instrument of punishment. Ferdinand's claim that he will make it his "masterpiece" casts cruelty as art, making him a grotesque dramatist who stages suffering for his imprisoned sister. This is a court where aesthetics, spectacle and power have become morally inverted. Rather than producing harmony, court performance manufactures fear.

The Duchess's death scene exposes the corrupted court most clearly because it forces a contrast between her dignity and the language of those who kill her. Bosola's chilling comment that "Strangling is a very quiet death" reduces murder to a technical matter of sound. The euphemistic understatement is horrifying because it shows how normalised violence has become. Yet after her death, Bosola recognises, "There's a great spirit gone!" This line marks the court's belated moral perception. The instrument of corruption sees, too late, the value of what the court has destroyed. Webster therefore uses Bosola to dramatise a form of tragic recognition, but it is not redemptive enough to undo the damage. The court can recognise virtue only after annihilating it.

By the final act, corruption consumes the corrupt. Ferdinand's lycanthropy literalises the bestial violence that has shaped his behaviour throughout. Pescara's report of him carrying a man's leg and howling behind Saint Mark's Church transforms aristocratic power into grotesque animality. The setting near a church intensifies the moral dislocation: sacred space cannot contain the eruption of violence. The Cardinal's cold secrecy also collapses, and the machinery of silence that protected him becomes useless. Webster's tragic structure therefore makes corruption self-consuming. The court has destroyed the Duchess, but in doing so it exposes its own disease.

Ultimately, Webster presents the court as a poisoned system in which power, law, religion, patronage and spectacle are all perverted. The Duchess's virtue is not sentimental innocence; it is moral steadiness within a world designed to make steadiness impossible. Through the fountain, cobweb, leech, masque and lycanthropy images, Webster makes corruption visible as contamination, predation, performance and bodily disorder. The play's bleakest insight is that a corrupt court does not merely contain evil people; it manufactures the conditions in which evil becomes normal, profitable and theatrical.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it treats the court as a system, not just as a collection of villains. AO1 is controlled by a conceptual thesis and cumulative paragraph structure. AO2 is developed through close work on metaphor, simile, dramatic irony, staging, spectacle and structural progression. AO3 is integrated through Jacobean ideas of court patronage, body politic imagery, religious hypocrisy, anti-Catholic theatrical convention and aristocratic honour culture.

# Model Answer 2 — Uncertainty

## Question

Explore how Webster creates uncertainty in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster creates uncertainty in *The Duchess of Malfi* by making the audience inhabit a world where truth is unstable, appearances are staged, and private identity is constantly threatened by surveillance. Uncertainty operates at several levels: characters are uncertain about motives, bodies, sanity, social meaning and spiritual justice. Yet Webster's most unsettling technique is that he often gives the audience more knowledge than the characters while still refusing moral or psychological certainty. We know, for example, that the Duchess's marriage is real and loving, but the court reads it as shameful. We witness Ferdinand's cruelty, but his motives remain disturbingly ambiguous. Webster therefore turns uncertainty into a tragic atmosphere: it is not simply confusion in the plot, but a condition of life in a corrupt and theatrical court.

At the start of the play, Webster creates uncertainty through the contrast between public appearance and private corruption. Antonio's account of the court as a fountain that should produce "pure silver drops" but may be poisoned "near the head" establishes a world where external order conceals internal disease. The metaphor depends on uncertainty: the fountain appears to flow, but its source may already be contaminated. This opening prepares the audience to distrust courtly surfaces. In a Jacobean context, where court life was associated with performance, favour-seeking and political concealment, Webster's imagery would speak to anxieties about how truth could be recognised in a world governed by rank and display.

The Duchess's secret marriage creates dramatic uncertainty because it separates moral truth from public knowledge. On stage, the audience sees the marriage ritual and hears the Duchess's assertion that she will "put off all vain ceremony." The phrase suggests sincerity, as though she removes the false costume of rank. Yet because the marriage remains hidden, its meaning becomes unstable within the wider court. Is the Duchess exercising rightful autonomy, or violating the codes of aristocratic widowhood? Webster encourages a sophisticated audience to recognise the marriage as emotionally authentic, but he also situates it in a society where secrecy will inevitably generate suspicion. Early modern expectations about chastity, widowhood, inheritance and social hierarchy make the Duchess's private truth publicly dangerous. Uncertainty is therefore produced by the collision between individual intention and social interpretation.

Surveillance is one of Webster's major dramatic methods for creating uncertainty. Ferdinand orders that Bosola be used "for your intelligence," immediately turning the court into a space of hidden observation. The punning force of "intelligence" suggests both information and strategic cunning. Bosola's later boast that his "masterpiece of cunning" was to make the Duchess "reveal herself" makes uncertainty actively manufactured: he creates situations in which the Duchess's body and speech can be misread. The audience is placed in an uneasy position because Bosola is both perceptive and corrupt. We often trust his analysis of the court, yet we watch him betray the play's most morally coherent character. Webster thus makes even insight uncertain; the person who sees most clearly may act most destructively.

Webster also creates uncertainty through the unstable theatricality of the Duchess's imprisonment. Ferdinand's dead hand trick is a striking example. In the darkness, he presents "a hand / To which you have vowed much love," with her ring upon it, making the Duchess believe Antonio is dead. The uncertainty here is sensory and theatrical: sight, touch and recognition are manipulated by stagecraft. Ferdinand does not merely lie; he constructs a false reality using props, darkness and timing. This makes him resemble a perverted dramatist, controlling what the Duchess and audience perceive. Jacobean revenge tragedy often uses spectacle to confront audiences with violence, but Webster's spectacle is psychologically unstable rather than simply bloody. The horror lies in not knowing what is real until after the emotional damage has been done.

The "masque of madmen" deepens this uncertainty by blurring entertainment, punishment and madness. A masque would normally suggest elite courtly art, harmony and controlled display, but Webster converts it into a chaotic assault on the Duchess's mind. Ferdinand's claim that he will make it his "masterpiece" is disturbing because it aestheticises torture. The audience is uncertain how to respond: the scene is theatrical, almost spectacular, yet its purpose is cruelty. It also raises the question of sanity. The madmen are staged as performers of disorder, while Ferdinand, who organises them, increasingly appears more disordered than they are. Webster destabilises the boundary between madness as spectacle and madness as moral condition.

The Duchess's own language resists uncertainty even while acknowledging a disordered world. Her declaration, "I am not mad yet, to my cause of sorrow," is repeated against apocalyptic imagery of "molten brass" and "flaming sulphur." The repetition of "I am not mad" makes sanity an act of will. Webster creates tension between the extremity of her surroundings and the clarity of her self-possession. The audience is invited to recognise that madness in this play may not belong to the person under pressure, but to the system applying that pressure. Contextually, early modern drama often associated disorder in the body or mind with moral and cosmic disturbance; Webster exploits that association but reverses its direction. The Duchess's sanity exposes the court's madness.

Ferdinand is the play's most concentrated source of psychological uncertainty. His line after the Duchess's murder — "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young" — is fractured into three short clauses, each refusing explanation. The imperative "Cover her face" suggests avoidance, shame or horror; "Mine eyes dazzle" may imply grief, guilt, desire, shock or recognition; "She died young" sounds almost tender but arrives too late. Webster's syntax makes certainty impossible. This is not a speech that clarifies motive; it reveals the impossibility of reducing Ferdinand's response to one emotion. His obsession with his sister has always been troublingly excessive, and this moment leaves the audience suspended between incestuous desire, family guilt, moral awakening and psychological collapse.

The final act widens uncertainty into a tragic structure. Ferdinand's lycanthropy, described as a "pestilent disease," gives his corruption a medical name, but naming does not explain it. The image of him howling and carrying a man's leg makes inner bestiality outwardly visible, yet it does not provide moral order. Antonio's accidental death intensifies the bleakness. He dies by mistake, with little ceremony, killed by the man who intended to help him. This creates uncertainty about providence and justice. If revenge tragedy traditionally moves towards moral reckoning, Webster's ending is more troubling: justice arrives through error, misunderstanding and waste.

Ultimately, Webster creates uncertainty by refusing to let appearances, institutions or even language provide secure truth. The court looks ordered but is poisoned; marriage is virtuous but read as scandal; spectacle appears artistic but becomes torture; madness is diagnosed but morally displaced; revenge appears corrective but produces accidents. Through darkness, props, surveillance, fractured syntax, theatrical spectacle and ambiguous psychology, Webster makes uncertainty the form of the tragedy itself. The audience is left with one relative certainty: in a corrupt court, truth does not disappear, but it becomes dangerously difficult to see in time.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer targets high Level 5 because it defines uncertainty broadly but precisely: not only plot confusion, but instability of perception, meaning, sanity, morality and justice. AO1 is strong because each paragraph develops the central thesis rather than listing scenes. AO2 is integrated through analysis of metaphor, props, darkness, staging, masque, repetition and fractured syntax. AO3 is embedded through court culture, Jacobean theatricality, gender expectations, revenge tragedy convention and early modern ideas about madness and disorder.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Corrupted Court

Webster presents the court as a poisoned organism in which corruption begins at the top and spreads through patronage, surveillance, religious hypocrisy and theatrical violence. The tragedy exposes not only individual evil but a whole culture that rewards predation and punishes integrity.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Uncertainty

Webster creates uncertainty by making truth unstable within a theatrical and corrupt court: characters misread bodies, motives, sanity and moral value, while the audience is forced to watch how surveillance, darkness, props and spectacle distort reality.
