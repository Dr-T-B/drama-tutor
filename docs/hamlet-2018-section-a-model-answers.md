# Model Answer 1 — Uncertainty

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents uncertainty in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents uncertainty in *Hamlet* not merely as Hamlet’s personal indecision, but as the tragic condition of Elsinore itself. The play imagines a court in which political performance, spiritual ambiguity and psychological self-questioning make stable knowledge almost impossible. As a revenge tragedy, *Hamlet* appears to offer the clear pattern of crime, discovery and retribution; yet Shakespeare repeatedly complicates that pattern by making Hamlet question the Ghost’s authority, Claudius’s guilt, Gertrude’s innocence, Ophelia’s sincerity, and even the moral value of revenge itself. In this sense, uncertainty becomes both theme and dramatic method: the audience, like Hamlet, is forced to interpret signs, overhear evasions, test appearances, and watch a tragedy in which truth is always mediated through performance.

This uncertainty is established from the opening scene, where Shakespeare begins not with the prince but with guards asking, “Who’s there?” The question is theatrically simple but conceptually important, since identity and knowledge are unstable from the first line. The darkness, the changing watch, and the anxious references to the Ghost create an atmosphere in which Denmark seems politically and spiritually insecure before Hamlet enters. Marcellus’s later statement that “Something is rotten in the state of Denmark” gives this uncertainty a diseased moral vocabulary. The adjective “rotten” suggests corruption beneath the surface, but the indefinite “something” is equally important: the characters sense disorder before they can diagnose it. Shakespeare’s staging therefore makes uncertainty a condition of the dramatic world. The audience sees a state where the signs of crisis precede explanation, reflecting the anxieties of a court culture preoccupied with succession, surveillance and political legitimacy.

The Ghost intensifies this uncertainty because it seems to offer revelation while also creating doubt. When Hamlet first confronts it, his antithetical phrasing — “Be thou a spirit of health or goblin damned” — dramatises competing theological possibilities. The Ghost may be a truthful spirit from purgatory, a morally authoritative father, or a devil exploiting Hamlet’s melancholy. Shakespeare’s language forces these alternatives into the same line, so the syntax embodies uncertainty rather than resolving it. This ambiguity would have had particular force in a culture shaped by Protestant and Catholic disagreements about ghosts and the afterlife. A Catholic framework might make sense of the Ghost’s purgatorial suffering, while a Protestant framework could treat such apparitions as demonic illusions. Shakespeare does not settle the issue. Instead, he makes religious uncertainty the foundation of the revenge plot, so Hamlet’s obligation to act is inseparable from his inability to know.

Hamlet’s response to the Ghost reveals a form of uncertainty that is ethical as much as psychological. The Ghost demands revenge, but Hamlet cannot simply become a conventional revenger because the command produces conflict between filial duty, Christian conscience and political danger. His cry that “The time is out of joint” gives his situation a structural dimension: Denmark itself has lost moral alignment, and Hamlet feels “cursed” to repair it. The metaphor of dislocation suggests a world in which action is necessary but restoration may be impossible. A simpler revenge-tragedy hero might translate knowledge into violence, but Shakespeare gives Hamlet a mind trained to question evidence and consequences. Critical debate often turns on whether this makes Hamlet morally intelligent or tragically evasive. The play sustains both readings: his doubt prevents reckless murder, yet his delay also allows corruption to spread.

Shakespeare’s soliloquies are central to this presentation because they dramatise thought as unstable, recursive and self-dividing. In “O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I,” Hamlet’s blank verse moves through self-accusation, theatrical comparison and renewed suspicion. He condemns himself as “pigeon-livered” and lacking “gall,” but almost immediately turns from emotional self-loathing to epistemological caution: “The spirit that I have seen / May be a devil.” The movement of the soliloquy matters. Shakespeare does not present Hamlet as simply cowardly; rather, the speech shows consciousness testing itself. The contrast between the actor who can “force his soul” into performance and Hamlet who cannot force certainty into action also makes theatre a central metaphor for uncertainty. Hamlet recognises that emotion may be performed, but this insight destabilises all appearances, including his own.

The gap between seeming and being is one of the play’s most persistent sources of uncertainty. Claudius’s court is built on performance: public ceremony masks private crime, and political order conceals fratricide. Claudius’s opening speech moves fluently between grief, celebration and statecraft, balancing “mirth in funeral” and “dirge in marriage.” The rhetorical antithesis is politically skilful, but it also sounds morally contaminated because opposites are unnaturally fused. Claudius appears certain in public because he can control language; yet Shakespeare later exposes his spiritual division in the prayer scene. His admission that “O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven” confirms his guilt for the audience, but the very privacy of this revelation means Hamlet does not possess it with equal certainty. Shakespeare therefore uses dramatic irony to create an uneven distribution of knowledge: the audience may know more than Hamlet, but Hamlet must act within the uncertainty of the stage world.

Hamlet’s “antic disposition” further complicates the relation between appearance and truth. His decision to “put an antic disposition on” seems strategic, but the disguise becomes unstable because neither the court nor the audience can always determine where performance ends and genuine disturbance begins. Polonius reads Hamlet’s behaviour as love-melancholy, Claudius suspects a deeper threat, Ophelia experiences it as cruelty, and the audience must hold multiple interpretations at once. The prose Hamlet uses in several court exchanges gives his language a quick, evasive, punning quality; he turns conversation into verbal traps. Yet the brilliance of the performance does not make it transparent. Shakespeare makes Hamlet’s feigned madness produce real uncertainty, exposing a court so dependent on surveillance that it can only interpret human behaviour as evidence.

The Mousetrap is the play’s most explicit attempt to convert uncertainty into knowledge. Hamlet’s claim that “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King” gives theatre an investigative function: performance becomes a test of hidden truth. Structurally, the play-within-the-play delays revenge but also provides a method for testing the Ghost’s story. This metatheatrical device is crucial because Shakespeare makes theatre both a medium of deception and a possible means of revelation. On one reading, The Mousetrap proves theatre’s moral power: Claudius’s reaction exposes guilt when direct accusation cannot. On another, Hamlet’s staging is itself manipulative, turning art into entrapment. The audience’s own act of watching is implicated, since the scene asks whether spectators can read truth from performance. Shakespeare thus makes uncertainty not an obstacle outside drama but one of drama’s central powers.

Even after The Mousetrap, however, certainty does not produce straightforward action. The prayer scene shows Hamlet apparently possessing the opportunity he has sought, yet he refuses to kill Claudius because he fears sending him to heaven: “Now might I do it pat, now he is praying.” The clipped monosyllables briefly suggest readiness, but the speech turns into theological calculation. Hamlet’s uncertainty here is not about Claudius’s guilt but about the invisible consequences of revenge. He tries to control salvation and damnation, imagining a more “horrid” moment for killing. This is morally disturbing, since Hamlet’s desire for perfect revenge begins to resemble spiritual presumption. In Christian terms, he attempts to know what only God can know. Shakespeare therefore darkens uncertainty: Hamlet’s conscience may restrain sin, but his speculative reasoning also corrupts his moral imagination.

Gertrude’s role adds another form of uncertainty because Shakespeare refuses to make her inwardness fully available. Hamlet treats her sexuality as evidence of moral corruption, declaring “Frailty, thy name is woman,” but the play never conclusively proves that she knew of the murder. In the closet scene, Hamlet attempts to force certainty upon her by staging a moral comparison between his father and Claudius. His language is violently visual, presenting one brother as a hyperbolic ideal and the other as diseased corruption. Yet Gertrude’s responses remain emotionally intense but ethically opaque. Some interpretations see her as self-deceived, others as morally weak, others as a pragmatic survivor in a patriarchal court. Shakespeare’s dramatic method withholds soliloquy from Gertrude, meaning the audience cannot access her private truth in the way it can access Hamlet’s. Her uncertainty is therefore produced by silence as much as speech.

Ophelia’s madness also contributes to the play’s wider concern with uncertain meaning. Her songs appear fragmented, but they are not meaningless. They combine references to death, sexual betrayal and corrupted innocence, creating a form of coded speech that unsettles the court. The uncertainty lies in whether her madness is simply psychological collapse or whether it releases truths that ordinary court language has suppressed. When she distributes flowers — “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance” — the symbolic language invites interpretation but resists exact decoding. The audience must ask who receives which flower, what each symbol implies, and whether Ophelia understands the political force of her speech. Shakespeare uses lyric fragmentation to make madness theatrically legible and unstable at once. In contrast to Hamlet’s performed madness, Ophelia’s appears more genuinely broken, yet it may speak more directly about the court’s hidden violence.

The gravedigger scene shifts uncertainty from politics and conscience to death, identity and the body. The prose dialogue with the gravediggers reduces mortality to legal, comic and physical terms, especially in the debate over whether Ophelia deserves Christian burial. Hamlet’s encounter with Yorick’s skull strips identity of courtly performance: “Where be your gibes now? your gambols? your songs?” The repeated questions confront the disappearance of personality into matter. Earlier, Hamlet’s uncertainty focused on whether and how to act; here, Shakespeare enlarges it into existential uncertainty about what remains of human distinction after death. The graveyard also exposes the limits of social hierarchy, since king, jester and beggar all become physical remains. Renaissance humanist confidence in “what a piece of work is a man” is tested against the levelling fact of decay.

In the final act, Shakespeare does not remove uncertainty so much as change Hamlet’s relation to it. Hamlet’s claim that “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” and later that “The readiness is all” suggests a movement from compulsive testing towards providential acceptance. This does not mean that Hamlet has solved the play’s mysteries. He still enters the duel without full knowledge of Claudius’s plot. What changes is his willingness to act within incomplete knowledge. The word “readiness” is crucial: it implies spiritual preparedness rather than absolute certainty. Some critics read this as Hamlet’s moral maturity, a recognition that human beings cannot control providence. Others see it as resignation, a surrender of agency after catastrophic delay. Shakespeare’s tragic structure sustains both possibilities. Hamlet’s acceptance enables action, but it arrives only when the court’s corruption has already become fatal.

The ending confirms that uncertainty has political consequences. The poisoned cup, the unbated foil and the rapid deaths produce a stage picture in which hidden plots finally become visible through catastrophe. Claudius’s guilt is publicly exposed, yet the truth emerges too late to save Gertrude, Laertes or Hamlet. Fortinbras arrives to impose order, but the future of Denmark remains uneasy, since the state passes to an external military figure. Horatio’s survival matters because he is instructed to “tell my story”; narrative becomes the only means of preserving meaning after uncertainty has destroyed the court. Even here, Shakespeare emphasises mediation: Hamlet’s truth must be reconstructed after death.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents uncertainty as the central tragic pressure of *Hamlet*. It is spiritual, because the Ghost may be saved soul or demon; moral, because revenge may be duty or sin; psychological, because Hamlet’s mind interrogates itself; theatrical, because performance both conceals and reveals truth; and political, because Elsinore is governed by secrecy and surveillance. The tragedy does not simply punish Hamlet for indecision. It shows a world in which certainty is ethically dangerous when false, but paralysis is destructive when doubt becomes endless. By making the audience share Hamlet’s interpretive labour, Shakespeare transforms revenge tragedy into a tragedy of knowledge itself.

# Model Answer 2 — Ophelia

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare presents Ophelia in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents Ophelia as one of the play’s most vulnerable figures, but he does not make her dramatically insignificant. Her tragedy lies in the fact that she is repeatedly spoken for, directed, interpreted and used by men, yet her breakdown releases a fragmented language that exposes the emotional and sexual violence of Elsinore. Ophelia is therefore both victim and revealer. Through her obedience, silence, lyric songs, symbolic flowers and disputed death, Shakespeare uses Ophelia to examine the pressures placed on female chastity, daughterly duty and speech in a corrupt patriarchal court. Her presentation is inseparable from Hamlet’s larger concern with surveillance, performance and the difficulty of interpreting human behaviour.

At first, Ophelia is presented through relational identities: daughter, sister, beloved and court subject. Shakespeare introduces her in conversation with Laertes, whose warnings about Hamlet immediately place her sexuality under male supervision. Laertes tells her that Hamlet’s “will is not his own,” presenting the prince’s desire as politically constrained by monarchy and dynastic obligation. This advice may be protective, but its language assumes that Ophelia’s honour is fragile and must be guarded. The imagery of flowers and cankers in Laertes’ speech constructs female chastity as something beautiful but easily corrupted. In early modern patriarchal culture, a woman’s sexual reputation could determine her social value, and Shakespeare makes Ophelia’s body and choices a site of family anxiety before the audience has seen her act independently.

Ophelia’s response to Laertes is important because it prevents her from being reduced to simple passivity. She replies with wit and moral intelligence, warning him not to behave like “a puffed and reckless libertine” while preaching virtue to her. The phrase exposes a double standard: Laertes claims authority over her chastity, but she recognises that male moral instruction may be hypocritical. Shakespeare gives Ophelia a controlled blank-verse voice here, capable of resistance within the limits of decorum. She cannot reject patriarchal authority outright, but she can identify its contradictions. This early moment allows a more nuanced reading of Ophelia as emotionally vulnerable but not intellectually empty.

Polonius then intensifies the pattern of control. His treatment of Ophelia is more openly instrumental than Laertes’ advice. He dismisses Hamlet’s vows as “springes to catch woodcocks,” reducing love language to traps and Ophelia to naïve prey. The hunting metaphor reveals Polonius’s suspicious view of courtship, but it also denies Ophelia’s capacity to interpret her own experience. His imperatives — “I would not, in plain terms, from this time forth, / Have you so slander any moment leisure” — impose paternal command over her speech, time and access to Hamlet. Her answer, “I shall obey, my lord,” is short and formally submissive. Shakespeare’s dramatic method here is economical: Ophelia’s curtailed line registers the contraction of her agency.

Ophelia’s obedience becomes tragic because Polonius uses her as an instrument of surveillance. In the nunnery scene, she is positioned as bait while Polonius and Claudius hide and observe Hamlet. This staging is crucial. Ophelia is not merely having a private conversation with Hamlet; she is made part of a political experiment. Her return of Hamlet’s “remembrances” is therefore emotionally charged and theatrically compromised. She must perform rejection under male instruction, while Hamlet may suspect that he is being watched. Shakespeare turns Ophelia into both participant and victim in the court’s surveillance system, showing how Elsinore corrupts intimate relations by converting love into evidence.

The nunnery scene presents Ophelia at the centre of interpretive uncertainty. Hamlet’s command, “Get thee to a nunnery,” may mean a convent, a brothel, or both, and its ambiguity makes his language sexually and morally violent. His questions — “Are you honest?” and “Are you fair?” — reduce Ophelia to chastity and appearance, reflecting a misogynistic logic already present in his earlier generalisation, “Frailty, thy name is woman.” Yet the scene also invites debate about Hamlet’s motives. He may be cruel because he feels betrayed, because he is performing madness, because he wants to protect Ophelia from the corrupt court, or because his disgust with Gertrude has contaminated his view of women. Shakespeare does not allow a single explanation to erase the damage done to Ophelia. Whatever Hamlet intends, she is forced to absorb the violence of his fractured language.

Ophelia’s response after Hamlet exits is one of the play’s most significant accounts of his collapse, but it also reveals her own. “O, what a noble mind is here o’erthrown” is often read as a lament for Hamlet, yet it simultaneously shows Ophelia measuring the destruction of the man she loved. The catalogue that follows — “The courtier’s, soldier’s, scholar’s, eye, tongue, sword” — presents Hamlet as an ideal Renaissance figure whose faculties have been disordered. The balanced triads and formal lament show that Ophelia still has access to a coherent poetic language, but that language is directed towards loss. Dramatically, she becomes a witness to the collapse of masculine identity, even as the audience recognises that her own identity is being damaged by the same courtly pressures.

Shakespeare also presents Ophelia as a foil to Hamlet, especially in relation to grief and madness. Hamlet’s melancholy is intellectualised, verbal and self-dramatising; Ophelia’s later madness is lyric, fragmented and embodied. Hamlet chooses an “antic disposition,” whereas Ophelia appears to be overwhelmed by actual psychic rupture after her father’s death and Hamlet’s rejection. Yet the contrast should not be simplified into active male intellect versus passive female breakdown. Ophelia’s madness produces a different kind of truth. Where sane court language disguises motive, her songs expose what the court would rather suppress: death, betrayal, sex, and the vulnerability of women.

The mad scenes transform Ophelia’s dramatic function. Her speech no longer follows the controlled obedience expected by father and court. Instead, she sings. Song gives her language a public, ritual and unsettling quality. Lines such as “He is dead and gone, lady” turn grief into refrain, while the bawdy Valentine song — “Before you tumbled me, / You promised me to wed” — introduces sexual betrayal into the royal court. The shift from blank verse to lyric fragments is central AO2 evidence: Shakespeare changes form to show a mind that has broken from social control, but also a voice that can now speak forbidden material. Critics often debate whether Ophelia’s madness is pure breakdown or coded protest. The strongest reading holds both together: her language is damaged, but the damage gives theatrical access to truths that polite obedience concealed.

The flowers deepen this ambiguity. “There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance” sounds like symbolic order, but the distribution of flowers remains uncertain and theatrically charged. Rosemary, pansies, fennel, columbines, rue and daisies carry associations of memory, thought, flattery, infidelity, repentance and innocence. Shakespeare makes the scene interpretive: the audience must read Ophelia’s gestures as well as her words. If the flowers are coherent accusations, then Ophelia becomes a truth-teller whose madness exposes corruption. If they are fragments of a disordered mind, then the scene is an image of psychological ruin. Either way, the court is destabilised. Her madness refuses the silence imposed on her earlier, and its symbolism makes Elsinore’s moral sickness visible.

Gertrude’s report of Ophelia’s death continues Shakespeare’s pattern of representing Ophelia through mediated speech. Ophelia does not narrate her own death; Gertrude describes it in one of the play’s most lyrical passages. The “fantastic garlands” and the “weeping brook” turn death into beautiful natural imagery, while the description that her clothes “pulled the poor wretch from her melodious lay / To muddy death” combines song, beauty and physical drowning. The aesthetic delicacy of the speech creates pathos, but it also raises disturbing questions. Was Ophelia’s death accident, suicide, or a deliberately unresolved dramatic ambiguity? Gertrude’s language may soften the horror, making Ophelia’s death appear passive and picturesque. A feminist reading might argue that even in death Ophelia is turned into an image by another character’s speech.

The burial scene makes Ophelia’s body the site of religious and social judgement. The gravediggers’ debate about whether she should receive Christian burial reflects early modern anxieties about suicide, sin and salvation. The “maimed rites” show that her death has not been fully resolved by the Church, and Laertes’ anger reveals the emotional inadequacy of institutional judgement. Shakespeare uses the graveyard to expose the cruelty of a world that first fails Ophelia and then questions her entitlement to ritual dignity. Her funeral also provokes Hamlet’s theatrical declaration of love, but even this moment centres male rivalry. Hamlet and Laertes compete over grief while Ophelia remains silent in the grave. This staging is devastating: her death becomes another occasion for men to perform feeling.

Ophelia’s relationship with Gertrude also matters, even though the two women are not developed as a unified female alliance. Ophelia functions partly as a foil to Gertrude: the younger woman is associated with chastity, obedience and victimhood, while Gertrude is associated in Hamlet’s imagination with sexuality, remarriage and moral compromise. Yet Shakespeare complicates this opposition. Both women are interpreted through male suspicion; both are denied soliloquies; both are made opaque by the play’s structure. Gertrude’s tenderness in describing Ophelia’s death may suggest genuine pity, but it arrives too late to protect her. The play therefore presents women’s vulnerability not as individual weakness but as a structural condition within Elsinore.

Ophelia is also central to the play’s critique of surveillance. Polonius uses Reynaldo to spy on Laertes and Ophelia to spy on Hamlet; Claudius uses Polonius, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern; Hamlet uses The Mousetrap. In this environment, Ophelia’s emotional life becomes material for political investigation. Her tragedy shows how a corrupt court destroys the possibility of trust. When love is turned into a test, speech becomes performance, and sincerity becomes unreadable. Shakespeare’s presentation of Ophelia therefore links gender to the play’s wider political structure: patriarchal control and state surveillance reinforce each other.

Critical interpretations of Ophelia have often shifted between seeing her as marginal victim and seeing her as a figure who exposes the play’s deepest violence. A traditional reading may emphasise her innocence and fragility, making her tragedy a consequence of Hamlet’s cruelty and Polonius’s death. A more feminist reading stresses how Ophelia’s silence and madness reveal the limits imposed on women’s speech. Elaine Showalter’s influential approach to Ophelia as a figure through whom cultures imagine female madness is useful here, but the play itself already dramatises that problem: Ophelia is watched, interpreted and aestheticised by others. Shakespeare makes her fragmentation theatrically powerful because it reveals what coherent male discourse hides.

By the end of the play, Ophelia’s significance is disproportionate to her stage time. She does not control events in the way Hamlet, Claudius or the Ghost appear to, but her suffering exposes the human cost of their actions. Through Ophelia, Shakespeare presents a world in which obedience can become self-erasure, love can be weaponised by surveillance, and female speech can only become fully disruptive when it is classified as madness. Her tragedy is not simply that she goes mad and dies; it is that the court gives her no sustainable language in which to live. Shakespeare presents Ophelia as both broken by Elsinore and essential to understanding it.
