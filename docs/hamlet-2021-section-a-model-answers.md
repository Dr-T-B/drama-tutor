# Model Answer 1 — Acting and the Theatre

## Question

> Explore how Shakespeare makes use of acting and the theatre in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare makes acting and theatre central to *Hamlet*, not merely through the literal arrival of the Players, but through the whole dramatic world of Elsinore, where identity is repeatedly staged, watched, rehearsed and exposed. The play presents performance as both a corrupt habit of political life and a possible instrument of moral discovery. Claudius survives by acting the role of legitimate king; Polonius turns human relationships into staged experiments; Rosencrantz and Guildenstern become scripted instruments of power; Ophelia is forced into a theatricalised encounter with Hamlet; and Hamlet himself adopts an “antic disposition” that both protects and entraps him. Shakespeare therefore uses theatre reflexively: drama becomes the means by which hidden guilt may be revealed, but the play also questions whether performance can ever produce moral certainty in a court built upon “seeming”.

From the opening movement of the play, Shakespeare establishes Elsinore as a world in which public appearance and inward truth have split apart. Hamlet’s early rebuke to Gertrude, “Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not ‘seems’,” introduces the play’s central suspicion of performance. The antithesis between “seems” and “is” makes Hamlet’s grief appear morally authentic because it resists the superficial ceremonies of the court. Yet the line is also theatrically unstable: Hamlet is speaking on stage, wearing the visible “inky cloak” of mourning, so the audience is immediately made aware that sincerity itself must be performed in drama. Shakespeare’s theatre therefore does not simply oppose truth and acting; it shows that truth is always mediated through gesture, costume, speech and public interpretation. In an early modern court culture governed by ceremony, hierarchy and display, Hamlet’s hostility to “seeming” is also a political objection. Claudius’ new monarchy depends upon performance: a funeral has been rapidly converted into a marriage, and public language is used to disguise moral disorder.

Claudius is one of the play’s most successful actors because he performs kingship with rhetorical control. In his first public speech he balances grief and celebration through carefully managed antitheses: “With mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage.” The chiasmic compression of opposites creates the impression of political equilibrium, but the unnatural pairing of “funeral” and “marriage” also exposes a court forcing incompatible meanings into a single public script. Shakespeare makes Claudius’ performance politically effective because he understands that monarchy in a court society is theatrical: the king must be seen to command language, emotion and ceremony. However, the later prayer scene fractures this performance. Claudius’ admission that his “words fly up” while his “thoughts remain below” reveals a terrifying division between outward religious acting and inward corruption. The vertical imagery exposes the inadequacy of performative repentance when conscience remains earthbound. Shakespeare therefore presents Claudius as a political performer whose theatrical success cannot finally abolish guilt.

Hamlet’s response to this world is to become an actor himself. His decision “to put an antic disposition on” is explicitly theatrical: he invents a role, anticipates an audience and controls interpretation by making others misread him. The phrase “put on” suggests costume, disguise and deliberate assumption, and the adjective “antic” implies a grotesque or clownish theatrical persona rather than genuine madness. In revenge tragedy, disguise often functions as a practical tactic for investigation, and Shakespeare uses Hamlet’s assumed madness to connect the genre’s conventions with a deeper Renaissance anxiety about selfhood. If identity can be “put on”, then the self may be less stable than Hamlet wishes to believe. This is why the “antic disposition” becomes morally ambiguous. It gives Hamlet freedom to test Claudius and mock the court, but it also licenses cruelty, evasiveness and delay. One interpretation sees Hamlet as a truth-seeker using theatre against a theatrical tyrant; another sees him as an actor who becomes trapped in the role he has chosen.

Shakespeare develops this ambiguity through the surveillance scenes, where the court repeatedly converts private feeling into staged spectacle. Polonius treats Hamlet’s behaviour as a performance to be decoded, declaring, “I’ll loose my daughter to him,” as though Ophelia were a prop or lure in a courtly experiment. The verb “loose” dehumanises Ophelia and presents her as an object released into a staged encounter while male observers conceal themselves. The nunnery scene is consequently structured as a play-within-a-play before the Players even appear: Ophelia is positioned, Hamlet is watched, and Claudius and Polonius become a hidden audience. Dramatic irony intensifies the cruelty because the theatre audience knows the encounter is being manipulated even as Hamlet may suspect it. Shakespeare’s staging therefore makes surveillance itself a form of theatre. Elsinore is not merely corrupt because people deceive one another; it is corrupt because power turns human beings into actors, directors and spectators within coercive performances.

The arrival of the Players sharpens this metatheatrical pattern by placing professional theatre inside Shakespeare’s tragedy. Hamlet’s response to the Player’s speech is crucial because it reveals his envy of theatrical intensity. The Player can weep for Hecuba, a fictional figure, while Hamlet condemns himself as “a rogue and peasant slave” for failing to act upon a real father’s murder. The rhetorical violence of the soliloquy, its exclamations, insults and self-lacerating questions, shows Hamlet measuring his ethical failure against the actor’s artificial emotion. Shakespeare does not simply praise acting here. The scene exposes both the power and the danger of theatrical feeling: the actor’s grief is false in origin but true in effect, whereas Hamlet’s grief is true in origin but paralysed in action. This reversal destabilises any simple hierarchy between reality and performance. The theatre can generate real emotional force from fiction, while reality itself can become inert when trapped in thought.

The Mousetrap is the play’s central experiment in theatre as moral investigation. Hamlet’s claim that “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King” gives theatre an almost judicial function. The rhyming couplet is memorable and decisive, and the metaphor “catch” presents drama as a trap designed to make hidden guilt visible. In an early modern context, where theatre was often attacked as morally suspect or deceptive, Shakespeare provocatively presents drama as capable of revealing truth more effectively than official institutions. Claudius cannot be legally prosecuted, the Ghost may be a devil, and court language is unreliable; therefore Hamlet turns to staged fiction as evidence. The embedded performance of *The Murder of Gonzago* becomes a test of conscience.

Yet Shakespeare also complicates this confidence. The Mousetrap is not pure proof in any modern evidential sense. It produces a reaction, but that reaction still requires interpretation. Hamlet’s excitement may be justified, but his triumph also reveals his dependence on theatrical signs. Claudius’ interruption — “Give me some light” — is both a literal stage direction and a symbolic exposure of darkness. The demand for “light” suggests that theatre has illuminated guilt, but it also reminds the audience that visibility is not the same as certainty. Hamlet’s subsequent behaviour becomes more performative and unstable, especially in the closet scene where his language turns violently theatrical. Shakespeare therefore makes The Mousetrap a brilliant but dangerous instrument. It exposes Claudius, but it also intensifies Hamlet’s reliance on performance as a way of acting upon the world.

Hamlet’s advice to the Players provides Shakespeare’s clearest reflection on dramatic art. His instruction to “Speak the speech, I pray you, as I pronounced it to you” and to “Suit the action to the word, the word to the action” promotes a disciplined, naturalistic ideal of performance. The balanced syntax of “action” and “word” suggests harmony between outward sign and inward meaning, precisely the harmony missing from Elsinore. Hamlet condemns overacting because excessive theatricality falsifies human experience, making performance grotesque rather than revelatory. However, this advice is also ironic. Hamlet can articulate the theory of truthful performance, but he cannot consistently live by it. His own behaviour alternates between philosophical authenticity, strategic role-play, verbal cruelty and impulsive violence. Shakespeare uses this irony to suggest that theatre can aspire to truth, but human beings are far less easily regulated than actors following stage directions.

The distinction between public and private performance also shapes Shakespeare’s presentation of Gertrude. At court, Gertrude performs the role of queen, wife and mother within Claudius’ political script; in the closet scene, those roles are violently interrogated by Hamlet. The scene is theatrically charged because it occurs in a private chamber but is still structured by hidden spectatorship: Polonius is behind the arras, and the theatre audience observes the attempted stripping away of Gertrude’s public role. Hamlet’s use of the two portraits — old Hamlet as Hyperion, Claudius as a “mildewed ear” — creates a miniature visual theatre through which he tries to stage moral recognition for his mother. Yet the scene also exposes the danger of forcing others into one’s own interpretive drama. Hamlet acts as director, prosecutor and moral lecturer, but his killing of Polonius demonstrates how quickly theatrical exposure can become tragic violence.

Even minor figures are absorbed into the play’s theatrical machinery. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are presented less as autonomous friends than as actors performing roles assigned by Claudius. Hamlet’s accusation that they would “play upon” him like a pipe turns musical performance into a metaphor for political manipulation. The image suggests that the court treats persons as instruments to be fingered, sounded and controlled. Hamlet’s counter-performance is witty and hostile: he refuses to be played while continuing to play madness himself. Shakespeare thus creates a tragic irony in which nearly everyone in Elsinore accuses others of performance while performing in return.

By the final act, the play’s theatricality has darkened into fatal structure. The fencing match is another staged event, apparently a public sport but secretly scripted by Claudius and Laertes as murder. The court watches as audience, the duelists perform honour, and the poisoned cup and blade convert ceremony into lethal theatre. Revenge tragedy conventionally moves towards spectacular death, and Shakespeare fulfils this expectation while exposing the cost of a world where appearances have been weaponised. At the end, Fortinbras’ command that Hamlet be borne “like a soldier to the stage” turns Hamlet’s body into the final object of public display. Even after death, identity is staged and interpreted.

Overall, Shakespeare makes acting and theatre the governing metaphor and mechanism of *Hamlet*. Performance corrupts Elsinore because political power depends upon scripted appearances, surveillance and false roles. Yet theatre also offers Hamlet a means of inquiry: fiction can reveal what official speech conceals. The play’s deepest complexity lies in its refusal to separate theatre neatly from truth. Shakespeare presents drama as morally powerful because it exposes conscience, but also morally dangerous because performance may entrap the performer and distort the spectator’s judgement. In this sense, *Hamlet* is not merely a play containing theatre; it is a tragedy about the necessity and unreliability of theatrical interpretation itself.

# Model Answer 2 — Polonius

## Question

> Explore Shakespeare’s presentation of Polonius in Hamlet. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents Polonius as far more than a comic old counsellor. Although he is often theatrically funny, especially in his verbal excess and self-importance, his comedy is inseparable from his danger. Polonius embodies a court culture in which language has become formulaic, paternal authority has become control, and political service has become surveillance. As counsellor, father and courtier, he attempts to manage others through maxims, spying and staged encounters, but his habitual misreading of human behaviour makes him both absurd and destructive. Shakespeare therefore uses Polonius as a tragicomic figure whose meddling helps expose the corrupt mechanisms of Elsinore.

Polonius’ first major appearance presents him as a father whose language is full of moral certainty but also theatrical self-display. His advice to Laertes is structured as a sequence of sententiae: “Give thy thoughts no tongue,” “Neither a borrower nor a lender be,” and “This above all: to thine own self be true.” These maxims sound authoritative because they are balanced, memorable and proverb-like. Shakespeare gives Polonius the language of traditional patriarchal wisdom, reflecting an early modern world in which fathers were expected to guide children and protect family reputation. Yet the sheer abundance of advice also makes him comic. The phrase “This above all” implies climactic moral seriousness, but it arrives after a long series of conventional social rules, making sincerity feel like another formula. Shakespeare therefore creates a double effect: Polonius speaks the language of prudence, but his verbal performance exposes a mind dependent on cliché.

This dependence on formula is central to Shakespeare’s presentation of Polonius as a courtier. His famous claim that “brevity is the soul of wit” is immediately comic because it occurs inside a speech that is anything but brief. The aphorism becomes self-indicting: Polonius can define rhetorical excellence but cannot practise it. Dramatically, this produces a comic interruption in the tense court scene, but it also has more serious implications. Elsinore is a court where language is constantly used to conceal, manage and manipulate. Claudius uses rhetoric to legitimise crime; Hamlet uses wit to evade interpretation; Polonius uses sententious language to perform wisdom. His language therefore makes him an embodiment of a court where words have become detached from truth.

Shakespeare intensifies this critique through Polonius’ treatment of Ophelia. As a father, he appears concerned with her protection, but his concern is inseparable from patriarchal control. When he warns her about Hamlet’s vows, he tells her that she speaks “like a green girl,” reducing her judgement to childish naivety. The adjective “green” implies immaturity and inexperience, while the imperative force of his commands denies her interpretive agency. His instruction that she should “tender” herself “more dearly” turns female reputation into a kind of economic asset. This reflects early modern assumptions about chastity, obedience and family honour: a daughter’s sexuality could be treated as part of the family’s social value. However, Shakespeare does not simply endorse this patriarchal logic. By showing Ophelia’s obedience and later collapse, the play exposes the human cost of a system in which a young woman is controlled by father, brother and prince.

Polonius’ relationship with Ophelia also reveals his habit of turning people into instruments. When he later says, “I’ll loose my daughter to him,” the verb “loose” is chillingly practical. Ophelia becomes a lure released into Hamlet’s path, while Polonius and Claudius hide as observers. The staging makes Polonius not only a father but a stage-manager of surveillance. He arranges bodies, controls entrances, scripts encounters and watches from concealment. In this sense, Shakespeare presents him as an agent of the same corrupt court machinery that Claudius exploits. The scene also invites alternative readings. Polonius may believe he is protecting his daughter and diagnosing Hamlet’s madness; yet his methods reveal a willingness to sacrifice Ophelia’s emotional safety to court politics. His paternal concern is therefore compromised by his appetite for control.

This appetite is already evident in his instructions to Reynaldo. Polonius tells him to investigate Laertes by using indirect slanders: “By indirections find directions out.” The balanced phrasing sounds clever, but it normalises deception as a method of knowledge. Shakespeare’s use of antithesis — “indirections” and “directions” — captures the moral crookedness of Polonius’ intelligence-gathering. He believes truth can be reached through manipulation, rumour and staged falsehood. Contextually, this connects Polonius with courtly counsel and the surveillance culture of absolutist monarchy, where political knowledge often depends upon informants and hidden observation. Dramatically, it also mirrors the wider structure of *Hamlet*, a play in which almost every major character watches, tests or interprets someone else. Polonius is not an isolated fool; he is a local expression of Elsinore’s governing habits.

Shakespeare’s presentation of Polonius’ misreading of Hamlet is comic, but the comedy is edged with danger. Polonius repeatedly assumes that Hamlet’s behaviour can be explained by rejected love for Ophelia, declaring that he has found “the very cause of Hamlet’s lunacy.” The phrase is comic in its overconfidence, especially because the audience knows that Hamlet’s disturbance is connected to the Ghost, Claudius’ murder and his own disgust with the court. Dramatic irony makes Polonius appear foolish because he mistakes a partial symptom for a complete explanation. However, the danger lies in the fact that his misreading has power. Because he is a counsellor close to Claudius, his flawed interpretation shapes court policy and leads to further surveillance. Shakespeare therefore presents Polonius as an example of how foolishness becomes dangerous when joined to institutional authority.

Hamlet’s treatment of Polonius reinforces this dual presentation. Hamlet mocks him as a “fishmonger” and later compares him to “Jephthah,” exposing both Polonius’ verbal limitations and his willingness to use his daughter. Hamlet’s wit makes Polonius look ridiculous, especially because Polonius often fails to understand the full force of the insults. Yet Shakespeare also ensures that Hamlet’s mockery is not morally simple. Polonius may be absurd, but Hamlet’s verbal cruelty contributes to the play’s atmosphere of hostility and emotional damage. The audience may laugh at Polonius’ obtuseness while recognising that he is caught in a world where language itself has become aggressive performance.

Polonius’ comic function is therefore structurally important. He slows scenes down with digression, interrupts serious exchanges and repeatedly mistakes surfaces for truth. His prose often contrasts with Hamlet’s more agile, punning intelligence or with Claudius’ controlled public verse. Yet Shakespeare does not allow the audience to treat him as harmless comic relief. His comic habits drive tragic consequences. He interferes in Ophelia’s relationship with Hamlet, promotes surveillance, confirms Claudius’ suspicions and hides behind the arras in Gertrude’s chamber. The tragic-comic instability of his character is one of Shakespeare’s most sophisticated effects: the same qualities that make Polonius amusing — curiosity, verbosity, overconfidence, officiousness — make him fatal within a revenge tragedy.

His death behind the arras is the culmination of his role as eavesdropper and stage-manager. When Polonius cries out from concealment, Hamlet kills him without seeing him, then calls him a “wretched, rash, intruding fool.” The adjectives are severe but accurate. “Intruding” defines Polonius’ whole dramatic function: he enters relationships, conversations and private spaces in order to control them. The staging is symbolically precise. Polonius dies hidden behind a curtain because concealment has become his habitual mode of action. Shakespeare turns his method into his fate. In revenge tragedy, hidden bodies and mistaken killings are conventional, but here the convention also becomes moral pattern: a man who lives by surveillance dies as a concealed spectator.

The consequences of Polonius’ death prevent the audience from dismissing him as merely ridiculous. His absence contributes directly to Ophelia’s madness and Laertes’ revenge. Ophelia’s breakdown is shaped by the removal of the father whose authority had dominated her, however restrictively. Her songs and fragmented speech reveal a world in which patriarchal control has failed to protect the daughter it claimed to guard. Laertes’ return, by contrast, shows another version of filial response: immediate, public and violent. Polonius therefore becomes a structural catalyst. His death helps generate the revenge plot that mirrors and pressures Hamlet’s own delay. In this sense, Shakespeare uses a seemingly secondary character to accelerate the tragedy’s final movement.

An important interpretive question is whether Polonius should be judged primarily as a protective father, a comic fool or a dangerous agent of corruption. Shakespeare keeps all three possibilities in play. Polonius’ advice to Laertes is not wholly foolish; some of it is socially prudent. His anxiety about Ophelia is not entirely baseless in a patriarchal society where female reputation could be severely damaged. His desire to understand Hamlet’s madness may even appear responsible within his role as counsellor. Yet his methods consistently corrupt whatever protective intention he may have. He spies rather than trusts, instructs rather than listens, and treats others as evidence in theories he has already decided. Shakespeare’s presentation is therefore not one-dimensional. Polonius is absurd because he overestimates his wisdom, dangerous because his authority gives that overconfidence consequences, and pathetic because he never understands the forces he is helping to release.

By presenting Polonius in this way, Shakespeare also critiques the culture of counsel at Elsinore. A good counsellor should help a ruler distinguish truth from flattery and justice from appetite. Polonius, however, supplies Claudius with surveillance rather than moral correction. He is useful to corrupt power because he turns suspicion into procedure. This makes him an important foil to the ideal of honest counsel. His language of duty and service masks a deeper failure of judgement. In a monarchy already founded on fratricide and concealment, Polonius’ courtly habits strengthen the very corruption they should restrain.

Ultimately, Shakespeare presents Polonius as a minor figure with major structural and moral significance. His rhetorical excess makes him comic; his patriarchal control damages Ophelia; his surveillance aligns him with Elsinore’s corruption; his misreadings intensify the court’s errors; and his death becomes a turning point in the tragic action. To reduce him to comic relief is therefore to miss Shakespeare’s dramatic method. Polonius is funny because he is foolish, but he is dangerous because his foolishness operates inside systems of family, court and state authority. Through him, Shakespeare shows how a corrupt world does not depend only on villains like Claudius; it is also sustained by officious servants, formulaic language and the ordinary habit of spying on the human soul.
