# Model Answer 1 — Family Relationships

## Question

Explore Webster's presentation of family relationships in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster presents family relationships in *The Duchess of Malfi* as the place where love, inheritance, sexual control and political authority become violently entangled. The family is not a private refuge from the corrupt court; it is one of the main instruments through which that corruption operates. The Duchess's brothers claim to protect dynastic honour, but Webster exposes this claim as a theatrical mask for possessiveness, misogyny and aristocratic anxiety about bloodline. By contrast, the Duchess's chosen family with Antonio is presented as emotionally authentic, yet structurally vulnerable because it exists outside the authorised hierarchies of rank, lineage and patriarchal supervision. The tragedy therefore turns on a profound contradiction: the biological family destroys, while the chosen family briefly humanises.

Webster first presents the Aragonian family as a corrupted aristocratic unit in which fraternity has become indistinguishable from surveillance. In Act 1, Ferdinand and the Cardinal warn the Duchess against remarriage, but the language of their warning immediately reveals that their concern is not moral purity but ownership. Ferdinand's obscene innuendo that women are like the part which, "like the lamprey, / Hath ne'er a bone in't" reduces his sister to a sexualised body rather than a sovereign woman. The simile is deliberately grotesque: the "lamprey" image makes female sexuality seem slippery, boneless and parasitic, exposing Ferdinand's own diseased imagination more than the Duchess's desire. Dramatically, Webster places this language inside a family conversation, making the audience witness how brotherly advice mutates into eroticised policing. In a Jacobean context, elite widows could possess property and some social autonomy, but remarriage still carried questions of inheritance, chastity and family reputation. Webster exploits that context by showing that the brothers' patriarchal authority depends on treating the Duchess's body as a dynastic possession.

The Duchess's secret marriage to Antonio challenges this family structure because it creates a private bond based on reciprocal affection rather than aristocratic strategy. Her statement, "The misery of us that are born great — / We are forced to woo because none dare woo us," presents rank as imprisonment rather than privilege. The paradox of being "forced" to initiate courtship suggests that her agency is itself produced by social constraint: she must act because the hierarchy around her prevents ordinary human intimacy. Webster's dramatic method is crucial here. The proposal scene reverses conventional gender and class expectations, with the Duchess directing the action, offering the ring, and rhetorically lowering herself from public title to "a young widow / That claims you for her husband." The verb "claims" is assertive, even legalistic, yet the phrase "young widow" also strips away ceremonial grandeur. Contextually, this would be provocative for a Jacobean audience alert to anxieties about female rule, widows' remarriage and social mobility. Webster does not present the marriage simply as reckless rebellion; rather, he frames it as an attempt to create an ethical family within a world where official family structures have become tyrannical.

However, Webster also makes clear that the Duchess and Antonio's family is politically fragile because it cannot be openly recognised. Their marriage must become secret, and secrecy exposes the family to surveillance. Bosola's role as spy turns domestic life into evidence. When he describes his "masterpiece of cunning" as making the Duchess "reveal herself," Webster uses the language of artistry to make espionage seem like a perverted dramatic craft. The female body becomes a text to be interpreted by male observers. Family here is not merely emotional; it is legible, watched and punishable. The pregnancy plot intensifies the conflict between private fertility and public reputation: the Duchess's maternal body threatens to make the hidden marriage visible. In a society concerned with legitimate succession, the birth of children is not just a private event but a political fact. Webster therefore makes family relationships unstable because domestic intimacy cannot remain separate from inheritance, status and surveillance.

Ferdinand's response to the Duchess's marriage reveals the most disturbing version of family feeling in the play: incestuous possessiveness disguised as honour. When he confronts her, he declares, "You have shook hands with Reputation / And made him invisible." The personification of "Reputation" as a male figure is revealing. Ferdinand imagines honour as something the Duchess has betrayed by entering another relationship, as though her body were accountable to a masculine social contract. The phrase "shook hands" also converts reputation into a public transaction, implying that female sexuality is managed through visible signs of social approval. Dramatically, Ferdinand's sudden appearance in the Duchess's bedchamber transforms the sibling relationship into a scene of invasion. He is not merely a brother defending family name; he becomes a voyeuristic intruder. Webster thereby critiques a patriarchal family system in which male relatives claim the right to regulate female desire even when that regulation becomes psychologically and sexually grotesque.

The Cardinal's family role is colder but no less destructive. Whereas Ferdinand's violence is feverish and imaginatively excessive, the Cardinal embodies institutionalised family corruption. Antonio's early description of the Cardinal's law as "a foul black cobweb to a spider" presents authority as predatory entrapment. The image links legal, religious and familial power: the Cardinal is not only a brother but also a churchman and political operator. His treatment of Julia further reveals the hypocrisy of patriarchal reputation. He condemns the Duchess's sexuality while conducting his own illicit affair, making the family's moral code asymmetrical: male transgression is managed; female transgression is punished. For a Jacobean audience familiar with anti-Catholic stage conventions and suspicion of Italianate court corruption, the Cardinal would embody both religious and familial perversion. Webster uses him to show that family honour is often a language used by powerful men to conceal their own appetite.

The Duchess's relationship with her children offers a sharply contrasting model of family, based not on possession but care. In the death scene, her request that Cariola give her little boy "some syrup for his cold" and make the girl "say her prayers ere she sleep" is devastating because it brings ordinary maternal tenderness into the machinery of revenge tragedy. The domestic specificity of "syrup" resists the brothers' attempt to turn her into a symbol of sexual shame. Even at the point of death, she thinks not of dynastic abstraction but of practical care. Webster's tonal shift is significant: after spectacles of wax figures, madmen and strangling, the plain maternal register re-humanises the Duchess. Contextually, early modern conduct literature often idealised women through chastity and obedience, but Webster locates the Duchess's virtue in constancy, courage and care rather than submission. Her family feeling is ethical because it protects life; her brothers' family feeling is corrupt because it polices and destroys life.

The tragedy's final movement shows family relationships collapsing into belated recognition and self-punishment. Ferdinand's line over the Duchess's body — "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young" — is fractured into short clauses, suggesting a mind unable to process the consequence of its own familial violence. The imperative "Cover her face" attempts to remove the sight of the sister he has destroyed, while "Mine eyes dazzle" implies guilt, grief, desire or horror without resolving them. Webster's refusal to clarify Ferdinand's feeling is what makes the moment powerful. It exposes the emotional disorder beneath patriarchal authority. The family has not preserved honour; it has produced madness, murder and spiritual disintegration.

Ultimately, Webster presents family relationships as a tragic test of social values. The Duchess's chosen family with Antonio is imperfect, secretive and politically vulnerable, but it is rooted in mutual recognition. The Aragonian biological family is publicly powerful yet morally diseased, because it treats women, marriage and children as extensions of status. Through grotesque imagery, invasive staging, domestic tenderness and the brothers' psychological collapse, Webster suggests that the real corruption of the play lies not simply in court politics but in the patriarchal family itself. The tragedy is therefore not that the Duchess abandons family duty, but that her family cannot imagine love except as ownership.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer aims for high Level 5 because it sustains a conceptual argument throughout: family is treated as a political and patriarchal structure, not merely a set of relationships. AO1 is developed through a clear thesis, controlled paragraph sequencing and precise evaluative vocabulary. AO2 is integrated through close analysis of imagery, syntax, staging, scene structure and tonal contrast. AO3 is embedded rather than bolted on, with contextual links to widows' remarriage, inheritance, patriarchy, Jacobean gender ideology, anti-Catholic stage convention and aristocratic reputation.

# Model Answer 2 — Reputation

## Question

Explore how Webster makes use of reputation in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

## Model Answer

Webster makes reputation one of the central mechanisms of tragedy in *The Duchess of Malfi*. Reputation is not presented as a stable reflection of moral worth; instead, it is a social fiction controlled by rank, gender and surveillance. The Duchess's brothers claim that reputation protects family honour, but Webster exposes it as a coercive system that disciplines female sexuality while excusing male corruption. The Duchess's tragedy is therefore not caused by the loss of true virtue, but by a society in which public reputation matters more than inward integrity. By the end of the play, Webster has reversed the hierarchy: the Duchess dies with moral identity intact, while the reputations of Ferdinand, the Cardinal and the court collapse into madness, exposure and death.

The play's opening establishes reputation as a public and political issue before it becomes a domestic one. Antonio's image of the court as "a common fountain" from which "pure silver drops" should flow suggests that reputation begins at the top of society. The metaphor implies that a ruler's moral condition circulates through the body politic; if the fountain is poisoned near "the head," then "Death and diseases through the whole land spread." Webster therefore frames reputation structurally, not merely personally. A corrupt court produces corrupt reputations because public honour is manufactured by those who hold power. In the Jacobean context, where court favour, patronage and visible status shaped social advancement, Webster's image would have particular force. Reputation is shown as a political ecosystem: when authority is diseased, public judgement becomes diseased too.

The Duchess's reputation becomes dangerous because she refuses to let public image define private truth. Her proposal to Antonio contains the bold assertion, "I do here put off all vain ceremony," which rejects the external signs of rank that normally protect aristocratic reputation. The phrase "put off" has a dramatic immediacy, as though she removes a costume on stage. This is not just romantic sincerity; it is a symbolic stripping away of the theatrical apparatus of status. Yet Webster makes the act double-edged. To an audience shaped by early modern assumptions about hierarchy and gender, an elite widow secretly marrying beneath her rank would threaten social order, inheritance and dynastic continuity. The Duchess's private virtue therefore becomes publicly unreadable. Webster's tragedy lies in that gap: she acts with emotional honesty, but her society interprets unregulated female desire as reputational disgrace.

Ferdinand's obsession with reputation reveals the patriarchal logic behind that interpretation. His accusation, "You have shook hands with Reputation / And made him invisible," personifies reputation as a masculine social figure from which the Duchess has supposedly separated herself. The line is revealing because it does not accuse her of lovelessness, cruelty or moral vice; it accuses her of making reputation unseen. Ferdinand's anxiety is visual and social. What cannot be seen, certified and controlled becomes intolerable. The verb phrase "shook hands" suggests a public transaction, implying that reputation exists as an agreement among men rather than as an ethical reality. Webster's staging intensifies the point: Ferdinand invades the Duchess's private space, turning reputation into surveillance. His concern for public honour is exposed as a desire to control his sister's body and narrative.

Webster also uses reputation to expose gendered hypocrisy. The Cardinal, a senior religious figure, preserves a public reputation for authority while privately engaging in sexual exploitation and murder. His corruption is captured in Antonio's earlier image of law as "a foul black cobweb to a spider," a metaphor that presents institutional respectability as predatory concealment. A cobweb looks delicate but functions as a trap; similarly, the Cardinal's reputation depends on structures that entangle others while protecting him. The Duchess, by contrast, is punished not because she lacks virtue but because her virtue does not conform to the public script of obedient widowhood. Her declaration that she is "flesh and blood" and not an "alabaster" figure at her husband's tomb rejects the reputational ideal of the frozen, sexless widow. Webster thereby attacks a culture in which women are praised when they become monuments and condemned when they remain human.

Bosola's function in the play shows how reputation is produced through observation, rumour and interpretation. As intelligencer, he turns the Duchess's private life into evidence for hostile male judgement. His claim that his "masterpiece of cunning" is to make her "reveal herself" presents surveillance as a kind of dark artistry. The word "masterpiece" is chilling because it aestheticises betrayal; Bosola constructs reputation as a performance in which the watched person is forced into visibility. The apricot scene and discovery of pregnancy make the Duchess's body publicly readable. In a culture deeply concerned with chastity, legitimacy and female modesty, pregnancy outside an acknowledged marriage becomes reputationally explosive. Webster does not endorse that judgement; he stages its cruelty. The Duchess's body tells the truth of marriage, but because the marriage is secret, the court reads that truth as shame.

The Duchess repeatedly resists the reduction of reputation to social opinion. Her line, "Whether I am doomed to live or die, / I can do both like a prince," appropriates masculine political language to assert self-definition. The phrase "like a prince" is crucial: she does not defend herself by appealing to conventional feminine modesty, but by claiming sovereign dignity. Later, "I am Duchess of Malfi still" becomes the play's most concise statement of reputational resistance. The five simple words separate identity from public slander. Reputation may be damaged, but being remains intact. Dramatically, this line gains force because it is spoken when external signs of power have been stripped away. Imprisoned, tortured and facing death, she has fewer social supports than ever, yet her verbal self-possession is absolute. Webster makes true nobility internal, ethical and performative in speech, not dependent on courtly approval.

The brothers' destruction of the Duchess finally destroys their own reputations. Ferdinand's attempt to police female honour produces the spectacle of his own dishonour: madness, lycanthropy and moral collapse. His command, "Cover her face. Mine eyes dazzle. She died young," exposes the failure of his reputational project. The broken syntax suggests a psyche overwhelmed by the sight of the woman he has tried to shame and control. The verb "dazzle" is especially ambiguous, implying that the Duchess's moral presence continues to shine even after death. The Cardinal's reputation also disintegrates in the final act as secrecy turns into exposure. The structures that once protected him become useless; his public authority cannot save him from the consequences of private corruption.

Webster's use of reputation is therefore fundamentally critical. Reputation in the play is not the same as virtue. It is a social technology used by patriarchal and aristocratic power to regulate bodies, preserve hierarchy and disguise corruption. The Duchess loses public reputation but retains moral identity; her brothers defend reputation but lose all ethical substance. Through imagery of poisoned fountains, cobwebs, theatrical self-stripping, surveillance and final bodily spectacle, Webster shows that a society obsessed with honour may be least capable of recognising honour when it appears. The Duchess's tragedy is that she lives in a world where reputation is louder than truth, but her final victory is that Webster makes truth theatrically unforgettable.

## Examiner Commentary

This answer aims for high Level 5 because it keeps the key term "reputation" under analytical pressure throughout. It does not simply list examples of good and bad reputation; it argues that reputation is a social technology shaped by patriarchy, rank and surveillance. AO1 is shown through a sustained conceptual line of argument. AO2 is developed through detailed analysis of metaphor, personification, staging, syntax and structural reversal. AO3 is integrated through references to Jacobean hierarchy, widowhood, female chastity, court patronage, religious authority and dynastic anxiety.

# Revision Notes for the Student

## Best thesis for Question 19 — Family Relationships

Webster presents family relationships as corrupted by patriarchy and rank: the biological family claims to protect honour but becomes an engine of surveillance and violence, while the Duchess's chosen family with Antonio briefly offers emotional authenticity but cannot survive a society governed by bloodline, reputation and male control.

## Best thesis for Question 20 — Reputation

Webster uses reputation to expose the distance between public honour and moral truth: the Duchess is reputationally condemned despite her integrity, while the brothers' obsession with honour reveals their corruption, hypocrisy and psychological disorder.
