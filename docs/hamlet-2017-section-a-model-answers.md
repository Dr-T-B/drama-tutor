# Model Answer 1 — Suffering

## Question

“Explore how Shakespeare presents suffering in *Hamlet*.”

## Model Answer

Shakespeare presents suffering in *Hamlet* as both intensely private and politically infectious. The play does not simply show characters experiencing grief; it dramatises suffering as a force that distorts language, action, identity and the state itself. Hamlet’s suffering begins in bereavement, but Shakespeare steadily widens it into a tragic condition in which private pain becomes inseparable from moral disgust, religious uncertainty and political corruption. A straightforward revenge-tragedy reading might see Hamlet’s suffering as the necessary emotional engine of revenge, but Shakespeare complicates this by making suffering philosophically articulate, theatrically unstable and ethically paralysing. In this sense, Hamlet is not merely a revenger who suffers; he is a tragic consciousness for whom suffering becomes the means by which the world is interpreted.

At the start of the play, Shakespeare presents suffering through a sharp contrast between public ceremony and private grief. Claudius attempts to control mourning through political rhetoric, describing the court’s response to Old Hamlet’s death as a balance of “mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage”. The antithetical structure exposes the artificiality of the court’s emotional language, where grief is managed as a matter of statecraft. Hamlet’s first soliloquy breaks violently from this controlled public register: “O that this too too solid flesh would melt.” The repeated intensifier “too too” gives suffering a bodily pressure, as if grief has become physically unbearable. Yet the verb “melt” also suggests a desire for dissolution rather than revenge. Shakespeare’s use of soliloquy is crucial here because it gives the audience access to suffering before Hamlet has any revenge duty. His pain is not initially heroic or purposeful; it is inward, disgusted and suicidal. For an early modern audience, Hamlet’s desire for self-destruction would be spiritually dangerous, since suicide was understood as a sin against God. The phrase “the Everlasting had not fixed / His canon ’gainst self-slaughter” therefore fuses psychological suffering with religious prohibition. Hamlet suffers because he wants release, but his religious imagination forbids it.

Shakespeare also presents suffering as a response to moral contamination. Hamlet’s anguish is not only caused by his father’s death but by his mother’s “o’erhasty marriage”, which he interprets as a collapse of sexual, familial and political order. His exclamation “Frailty, thy name is woman” converts personal pain into misogynistic generalisation. The abstract noun “Frailty” shows how suffering can distort judgement: Gertrude becomes, in Hamlet’s mind, the emblem of female weakness. A feminist reading might argue that Shakespeare exposes the violence of patriarchal interpretation here, since Gertrude’s interior life is largely withheld while Hamlet’s suffering dominates the dramatic frame. However, another interpretation is that Hamlet’s language reveals the scale of his emotional injury: he experiences his mother’s remarriage as a betrayal of memory itself. In either case, Shakespeare makes suffering linguistically unstable. Hamlet’s grief does not remain pure; it mutates into disgust, accusation and theatrical excess.

The Ghost intensifies suffering by transforming grief into obligation. When Hamlet hears that his father was murdered, his pain becomes bound to revenge: “The time is out of joint: O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right.” The metaphor of dislocation suggests that suffering is structural, not merely emotional. Denmark itself has become misaligned. Shakespeare draws on revenge-tragedy conventions — the Ghost, the murder, the demand for vengeance — but he also revises them. Hamlet’s suffering is not only that he must revenge; it is that he must act in a world where moral truth is uncertain. Protestant suspicion of ghosts and Catholic ideas of purgatorial return would have made the Ghost theatrically and theologically ambiguous. Hamlet therefore suffers from epistemological anxiety: if the Ghost is truthful, revenge is duty; if demonic, revenge may damn him. Shakespeare’s tragic form depends on this uncertainty, as suffering becomes inseparable from interpretation.

Hamlet’s suffering is most famously philosophical in “To be, or not to be”, where Shakespeare moves from personal grief to universal human endurance. The balanced infinitives reduce existence to a stark antithesis, while the repeated abstractions — “suffer”, “fortune”, “conscience”, “resolution” — make the soliloquy less a confession than a meditation. The “slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” present suffering as assault, but the alternative, “to take arms against a sea of troubles”, is paradoxical and futile: one cannot fight a sea. Shakespeare’s imagery therefore dramatises the impossibility of mastering pain through action. Critical debate often turns on whether Hamlet’s thoughtfulness is noble moral intelligence or destructive paralysis. The soliloquy supports both readings. His conscience makes him recognisably human, even heroic in intellectual honesty; yet it also “does make cowards of us all”, suggesting that reflective suffering drains action of force. In Renaissance humanist terms, Hamlet’s capacity for reason elevates him, but in tragic terms it also disables him.

Shakespeare contrasts Hamlet’s articulate suffering with Ophelia’s more fragmented and socially silenced suffering. Ophelia is used by Polonius and Claudius as an instrument of surveillance, instructed to perform obedience while the men observe Hamlet. Her suffering is therefore produced by patriarchal control and court politics. After Polonius’s death, her madness breaks into songs, flowers and sexual fragments: “He is dead and gone, lady”. Unlike Hamlet’s soliloquies, Ophelia’s broken lyricism is not granted philosophical authority by the court. Yet dramatically, her suffering exposes what the political world tries to repress. The flowers she distributes become symbolic judgements, turning madness into a displaced language of truth. Some critics read Ophelia as merely passive victim; a stronger interpretation is that Shakespeare gives her madness a subversive theatrical force. Her suffering reveals the cost of the male revenge plot and the emotional casualties hidden beneath Hamlet’s intellectual drama.

Claudius too suffers, but Shakespeare presents his suffering as guilty conscience rather than innocent pain. In the prayer scene, Claudius admits “O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven.” The sensory image of corruption recalls Marcellus’s “Something is rotten in the state of Denmark”, linking personal sin to national decay. Yet Claudius’s suffering is morally compromised because it does not produce repentance: “My words fly up, my thoughts remain below.” The antithesis between “words” and “thoughts” exposes a division between performance and inward truth. Shakespeare’s staging is rich in dramatic irony: Hamlet enters while Claudius appears to pray, but the audience later knows that the prayer is spiritually hollow. Claudius’s suffering therefore becomes another form of theatrical performance, unlike Hamlet’s soliloquies, which at least struggle towards self-knowledge.

By the final act, Shakespeare transforms suffering from paralysis into tragic acceptance. Hamlet’s declaration that “There’s a divinity that shapes our ends” suggests a movement from tortured self-consciousness towards providential readiness. This is not simple consolation: the graveyard scene still confronts the material reality of death through Yorick’s skull, reducing human ambition to bone and dust. However, Hamlet’s final statement that “the readiness is all” gives suffering a new form. He no longer tries to master the future through thought; he accepts human limitation. Some may read this as spiritual maturity, while others may see it as exhausted fatalism. Shakespeare deliberately leaves the distinction unresolved. In the final scene, suffering becomes communal catastrophe: Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius and Hamlet all die, and Horatio is left to narrate the tragedy. “Good night, sweet prince” converts suffering into elegy, restoring dignity only after destruction.

Overall, Shakespeare presents suffering in *Hamlet* as the central medium of tragedy. It is psychological, spiritual, political and theatrical. Hamlet’s suffering gives him depth, but it also distorts his perception and delays action. Ophelia’s suffering exposes the human cost of patriarchal and political manipulation. Claudius’s suffering reveals the torment of guilt without repentance. Through soliloquy, imagery, irony, staging and tragic structure, Shakespeare turns suffering into a way of testing what it means to think, act and remain morally conscious in a corrupted world.

# Model Answer 2 — The Play Within the Play

## Question

“Explore how Shakespeare makes use of the play within the play in *Hamlet*.”

## Model Answer

Shakespeare makes use of the play within the play as the dramatic centre of *Hamlet* because it turns theatre itself into a means of investigation, exposure and moral pressure. *The Mousetrap* is not simply an entertaining device or a plot mechanism to prove Claudius’s guilt; it embodies the play’s wider concern with performance, surveillance and unstable truth. In a court where almost every character is acting a part, Hamlet uses theatre to test whether performance can reveal what political language conceals. A simple reading might see the play within the play as Hamlet’s clever trap for Claudius, but Shakespeare makes it far more complex: it is also a metatheatrical reflection on the power and danger of drama itself.

Before the travelling players arrive, Shakespeare has already made Elsinore a theatrical world. Claudius performs kingship through controlled public rhetoric, Gertrude performs courtly composure, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern perform friendship while serving as spies, and Hamlet chooses to “put an antic disposition on”. The play within the play therefore develops an existing pattern of role-playing. Hamlet’s madness is itself a kind of theatre, but one that creates uncertainty rather than truth. By contrast, his plan for the players aims to convert performance into evidence: “The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King.” The rhyming couplet gives the line a decisive theatrical closure, but the phrase “catch the conscience” is morally and dramatically ambiguous. Hamlet seeks inward truth through outward reaction, assuming that staged fiction can expose hidden guilt. Shakespeare thus places extraordinary pressure on theatre’s ability to reveal conscience.

The arrival of the players allows Shakespeare to contrast authentic theatrical emotion with Hamlet’s frustrated inaction. When the First Player weeps for Hecuba, Hamlet is shocked that an actor can summon tears for a fictional figure while he, with real cause, remains delayed: “O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I.” The soliloquy is structured as self-accusation, moving through rhetorical questions, violent imperatives and disgusted comparison. The contrast between “Hecuba” and Hamlet’s murdered father exposes Hamlet’s anxiety that performance may be more emotionally potent than reality. Yet this moment also complicates any reading of Hamlet as simply inactive. His response to the player generates a new strategy: he will use drama not as escape but as a diagnostic instrument. Shakespeare therefore makes the play within the play arise directly from Hamlet’s self-consciousness and his distrust of immediate revenge.

Theologically and ethically, *The Mousetrap* is necessary because the Ghost’s evidence is uncertain. Hamlet fears that “The spirit that I have seen / May be a devil”, a concern that would resonate in an early modern culture divided by Protestant and Catholic assumptions about ghosts, purgatory and demonic temptation. The play within the play becomes Hamlet’s attempt to move from supernatural testimony to observable proof. In revenge-tragedy terms, this is a striking delay: rather than rushing to bloody action, Hamlet stages an experiment. Some critics view this as evidence of paralysis; others see it as moral intelligence. Shakespeare makes the audience feel both. The delay frustrates revenge convention, but it also prevents Hamlet from becoming a crude revenger who kills without ethical verification.

During the performance itself, Shakespeare uses staging, doubling and dramatic irony to make the court watch itself. The dumb show silently enacts the murder before the spoken play begins, creating a visual mirror of Claudius’s crime. Its silence is important: truth first appears without explanatory language, suggesting that gesture and spectacle can communicate what court speech conceals. Yet Claudius does not react decisively to the dumb show, which creates interpretive uncertainty. Does he fail to recognise it, conceal his reaction, or dismiss it as harmless fiction? Shakespeare’s use of the dumb show prevents the audience from treating theatrical evidence as simple. The later spoken murder, however, intensifies the pressure when Lucianus pours poison into the sleeper’s ear. This image echoes the Ghost’s account of murder by ear-poisoning and also symbolically links political corruption to corrupted hearing: Denmark is a state where words infect.

Hamlet’s behaviour during the play is itself performative. His obscene verbal sparring with Ophelia — “Do you think I meant country matters?” — shows him manipulating the audience within the playhouse of Elsinore. He becomes both spectator and commentator, director and actor. His commentary is designed to provoke, and his public cruelty to Ophelia reveals the moral risk of his theatrical strategy. While Hamlet uses theatre to expose Claudius, he also performs misogynistic aggression and emotional displacement. A feminist interpretation might argue that Ophelia becomes collateral damage in Hamlet’s metatheatrical revenge plot, forced to sit at the intersection of male surveillance, sexual humiliation and political danger. Shakespeare therefore prevents the play within the play from being a purely triumphant moment of intellectual control.

Claudius’s reaction gives the scene its immediate dramatic power: “Give me some light. Away!” The imperative and abrupt exit appear to confirm guilt. Symbolically, the demand for “light” is ironic because Claudius seeks literal illumination at the moment his moral darkness has been exposed. Yet the scene remains theatrically complex. Claudius is not convicted in a legal sense; he is read by Hamlet and the audience through behaviour. Shakespeare makes spectatorship itself an interpretive act. The audience watches Hamlet watching Claudius watching a play that mirrors his own crime. This layered structure is intensely metatheatrical: the theatre audience outside the play is invited to consider how drama produces knowledge, suspicion and judgement.

The consequences of the play within the play are structurally decisive. It confirms Hamlet’s belief in Claudius’s guilt, but it does not lead immediately to revenge. Instead, it leads to the prayer scene, where Hamlet again delays: “Now might I do it pat.” The monosyllabic directness suggests opportunity, but Hamlet’s theological reasoning prevents action. This shows that the play within the play solves one problem but intensifies another. It answers the question of guilt, but not the question of how revenge can be morally executed. Shakespeare’s structure therefore uses *The Mousetrap* as a pivot rather than a resolution. After it, the tragedy accelerates: Polonius is killed, Ophelia is destroyed, Laertes returns, and Claudius becomes more openly murderous. Theatre exposes corruption, but exposure does not purify the state; it pushes corruption into more violent forms.

The play within the play also reflects Shakespeare’s wider defence of theatre. Hamlet’s advice to the players — to “hold, as ’twere, the mirror up to nature” — suggests that drama can reveal moral truth by reflecting human behaviour. This is a recognisably Renaissance claim for theatre’s ethical and mimetic power. However, Shakespeare is not naïve about performance. Acting can reveal, but it can also disguise; it can awaken conscience, but it can also humiliate and manipulate. The same theatrical intelligence that allows Hamlet to expose Claudius also allows him to evade direct action and wound Ophelia. Thus, Shakespeare makes the play within the play both a triumph of dramatic art and a troubling symptom of Hamlet’s dependence on performance.

Overall, Shakespeare uses the play within the play as the metatheatrical core of *Hamlet*. It tests the Ghost, exposes Claudius, extends the theme of performance, and turns spectatorship into moral judgement. At the same time, it deepens the tragedy because theatrical proof does not produce ethical certainty or decisive action. Through *The Mousetrap*, Shakespeare shows that drama can reveal hidden guilt, but it cannot repair a corrupted world. The play within the play catches the conscience of the King, but it also reveals the dangerous theatricality of everyone in Elsinore, including Hamlet himself.

Shakespeare uses the play within the play as the metatheatrical centre of *Hamlet*, turning theatre into a device for exposing hidden guilt while also revealing the dangers of performance, surveillance and interpretive uncertainty.
