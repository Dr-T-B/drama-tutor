### What the Examiner Expects — In a Single Integrated Paragraph

The examiner is looking for a sustained, conceptually driven argument \[AO1\] that treats the text as a dramatic work — not a novel — and analyses how Shakespeare or Webster constructs meaning through specific dramatic and linguistic methods \[AO2\], while embedding relevant contextual factors as interpretive tools rather than background filler \[AO3\], and (in Section A only) engaging with named critical perspectives as genuine interpretive debate rather than decorative name-dropping \[AO5\]. A Level 5 response reads as a single, confident critical voice making an argument, not a student ticking boxes; every sentence pushes the thesis forward, every quotation is subjected to close verbal analysis, and every contextual or critical reference earns its place by shaping interpretation.

## 1b. Level Descriptor Translations

Edexcel mark schemes use five levels. Here, each level is translated into plain language so you can diagnose exactly where your writing sits — and what to do to move up.

### Level 1 (Marks: 0–6 for Section A / 0–5 for Section B)

**What the writing actually does:** Retells the story or identifies features without explaining them. Quotations may be present but are not analysed. Context, if mentioned, sits in a separate sentence disconnected from argument.

**Self-assessment diagnostic:** *"If I read my essay back, it mostly describes what happens in the play rather than arguing what it means."*

**Why students get stuck here:** They have not yet understood that the exam rewards argument about the text, not description of it. They write about events rather than dramatic effects.

### Level 2 (Marks: 7–12 for Section A / 6–10 for Section B)

**What the writing actually does:** Makes relevant points supported by some textual evidence. There is awareness of methods and context, but analysis tends to be general ("Shakespeare uses imagery to show...") rather than precise. Points are listed rather than connected into an argument.

**Self-assessment diagnostic:** *"My paragraphs make valid points, but each one feels like a fresh start rather than part of a building argument."*

**Why students get stuck here:** They identify features correctly but do not push through to effect and significance. Each paragraph is a standalone observation rather than a step in a thesis.

### Level 3 (Marks: 13–18 for Section A / 11–15 for Section B)

**What the writing actually does:** Develops a clear line of argument with relevant textual support. Methods are named and their effects explored, though analysis sometimes stays at the level of general effect ("creates tension") rather than precise thematic significance. Context is present but may feel bolted on rather than woven in. Critical readings (Section A) are referenced but not fully integrated.

**Self-assessment diagnostic:** *"I have an argument and I analyse quotations, but my AO2 often stops at 'this creates a sense of...' without connecting to a bigger idea, and my context sits in its own sentence."*

**Why students get stuck here:** They analyse competently but do not conceptualise. The leap from "what does this method do?" to "what does this tell us about the play's ideological project?" is the barrier.

### Level 4 (Marks: 19–24 for Section A / 16–20 for Section B)

**What the writing actually does:** Sustains a clear, analytical argument with well-chosen evidence. AO2 analysis identifies specific words and techniques and connects them to thematic significance. Context is embedded as part of the interpretive framework, not listed as background. Critical readings (Section A) are used to shape interpretation, not just cited.

**Self-assessment diagnostic:** *"My argument is clear and my analysis is specific, but I sometimes explain what a critic says rather than using their idea to drive my own reading."*

**Why students get stuck here:** They are analytical but not yet fully evaluative. They respond to the question rather than reshaping it conceptually. Critical perspectives are understood but not wielded as interpretive tools.

### Level 5 (Marks: 25–35 for Section A / 21–25 for Section B)

**What the writing actually does:** Constructs a conceptually sophisticated argument that treats the question as an invitation to think, not a template to fill. Every sentence advances the thesis. AO2 analysis moves from word-level precision to structural and ideological significance. Context is not a paragraph — it is the lens through which the entire argument operates. Critical perspectives (Section A) are engaged with evaluatively: the student positions themselves in relation to the debate, not just beside it.

**Self-assessment diagnostic:** *"My essay reads as though I am making a case about what the play does and why it matters, not answering a question. My reader would struggle to separate the 'AO2 bit' from the 'AO3 bit' because they are fused."*

**Why students get stuck here:** Very few students get stuck *at* Level 5 — they get stuck *below* it because they have not yet learned to write conceptually. The shift is from "what does Shakespeare/Webster do here?" to "what is Shakespeare/Webster staging, interrogating, or exposing through the dramatic architecture of this play?"

## 1d. Common Errors That Keep Answers Below Level 5

### Error 1: Storytelling Instead of Arguing

**What it is:** The essay narrates events from the play rather than constructing an argument about what the play does and means.

**Why it limits the mark:** Examiners at Level 5 are looking for conceptual argument \[AO1\]. Plot retelling, however accurate, demonstrates comprehension, not analysis. It typically sits at Level 1–2.

**The fix:** Before writing each paragraph, state your point as a claim about meaning, not an event. Test: does your topic sentence work without knowing the story? If it only makes sense as part of a plot summary, it is not an argument.

### Error 2: Feature-Spotting Without Effect

**What it is:** The student names a technique ("Shakespeare uses a metaphor") but does not explain what it achieves or why it matters.

**Why it limits the mark:** AO2 rewards analysis of how meaning is *shaped*, not identification of techniques. Naming a method without exploring its effect is Level 2 work.

**The fix:** Apply the AO2 formula every time: **Method → Word/Phrase → Effect → Thematic Significance.** Never name a method without completing all four stages.

### Error 3: Context as a Separate Paragraph

**What it is:** The student writes a block of historical information ("In the Jacobean period, women had limited rights…") disconnected from textual analysis.

**Why it limits the mark:** AO3 is assessed as *embedded* interpretation, not background knowledge. A free-standing context paragraph contributes nothing to the analytical argument and signals a surface understanding of what AO3 requires.

**The fix:** Contextual knowledge must appear *inside* the analytical sentence, shaping the reading of a specific moment. The context should explain *why* a dramatic choice carries particular weight for its audience.

### Error 4: Critic Name-Drop Without Integration

**What it is:** The student writes "Showalter argues that Ophelia's madness is significant" without explaining what Showalter's argument actually is or how it changes the reading of a specific passage.

**Why it limits the mark:** AO5 rewards genuine interpretive debate, not a bibliography. A name without an argument is decoration, not analysis.

**The fix:** Every named critic must come with (a) their specific position in one sentence, and (b) a sentence showing how that position reshapes the reading of a particular moment in the text. If you cannot do both, do not mention the critic.

### Error 5: Treating AO5 as a Bolt-On

**What it is:** The student adds a final sentence to each paragraph — "However, a feminist critic would argue…" — as a formulaic afterthought, disconnected from the preceding analysis.

**Why it limits the mark:** This produces a mechanical feel that examiners associate with Level 3. AO5 should be woven through the argument, not stapled to the end of each paragraph.

**The fix:** Use one of three integration strategies: (1) *frame* — open the paragraph with the critical debate, then analyse the text through it; (2) *weave* — let the critical lens shape the word-level analysis mid-paragraph; (3) *conclude* — use the critic to elevate the paragraph's final claim to a conceptual level. Vary the strategy across paragraphs.

### Error 6: Comparing the Two Texts in Section B

**What it is:** The student brings *Hamlet* into a *Duchess of Malfi* answer, assuming both plays must be compared.

**Why it limits the mark:** Section B assesses AO1, AO2, and AO3 only. AO4 (connection across literary texts) is not assessed in Component 1. Comparison wastes time and demonstrates misunderstanding of the paper.

**The fix:** Treat Section B as a single-text essay. Every reference, every quotation, every argument must come from *The Duchess of Malfi* alone.

### Error 7: The "This Shows That" Filler Phrase

**What it is:** Analytical sentences that lean on empty connective phrases: "This shows that...", "The writer uses this to...", "This technique creates a sense of...".

**Why it limits the mark:** These phrases signal that the student is *reporting* an observation rather than *making* an argument. They pad word count without adding analytical substance and are a hallmark of Level 2–3 writing.

**The fix:** Delete the filler and state the analytical claim directly. Instead of "Shakespeare uses a metaphor to show Claudius is corrupt", write: "The metaphor of the 'serpent' (1.5) aligns Claudius with the Edenic tempter, framing regicide as an act of primordial evil \[AO2\] that resonates with the play's persistent staging of Elsinore as a fallen world \[AO3\]."

### Error 8: Ignoring Dramatic Form

**What it is:** The student analyses the text as though it were a novel, discussing "what Shakespeare writes" rather than "what Shakespeare stages". Soliloquies, asides, entrances, exits, stage positioning, and audience experience are ignored.

**Why it limits the mark:** Both texts are plays. AO2 in drama assesses analysis of dramatic methods — not just linguistic ones. Ignoring the performed dimension of the text limits the range of AO2 and signals that the student has not grasped the genre.

**The fix:** Treat the text as a blueprint for performance. Ask: what does the audience see, hear, and experience? How does staging create meaning that the words alone do not? Analyse structural positioning (where a scene sits in the act), theatrical spectacle, and the relationship between actor and audience.

### Error 9: Quoting Without Analysing

**What it is:** The student drops in a long quotation and moves on, assuming the quote speaks for itself.

**Why it limits the mark:** Quotations are evidence, not arguments. An unanalysed quote is a missed opportunity for AO2 and typically signals Level 2 work.

**The fix:** Keep quotations short — a phrase or clause is usually enough. Embed the quotation within your own sentence. Immediately after the quotation, zoom in on a specific word or phrase and apply the full AO2 chain: Method → Word → Effect → Thematic Significance.

### Error 10: Running Out of Time on Section B

**What it is:** The student spends too long on Section A (35 marks) and writes an incomplete or rushed Section B answer (25 marks).

**Why it limits the mark:** A complete, competent Section B answer at Level 4 is worth up to 20 marks. A rushed, incomplete answer may score Level 2 (6–10 marks). Losing 10+ marks on Section B can reduce an overall A to a C.

**The fix:** Stick to the timing plan: 5 minutes planning + 70 minutes writing for Section A; 5 minutes planning + 50 minutes writing for Section B; 5 minutes buffer. Practise writing Section B essays within 50 minutes until the discipline is automatic.

### Error 11: Unfocused Thesis

**What it is:** The introduction restates the question or makes a broad, unfalsifiable claim ("Shakespeare presents madness in many ways throughout the play") rather than offering a specific, debatable argument.

**Why it limits the mark:** A weak thesis means the essay has no argumentative spine. Each paragraph drifts rather than building toward a conclusion, and the examiner cannot identify a controlling idea. This caps the response at Level 3.

**The fix:** A thesis should be a claim that someone could disagree with. Test it: if the opposite of your thesis sounds absurd ("Shakespeare does not present madness"), your thesis is too vague. A strong thesis argues *what* the play does with its theme and *why* it matters.

## 1e. The Integrated AO Paragraph Model

The following is a single analytical paragraph on *Hamlet*, demonstrating all four assessed AOs (AO1, AO2, AO3, AO5) operating simultaneously within one analytical unit. Each sentence is annotated.

**Question:** *Explore the significance of delay in* Hamlet. *You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.*

**Controlling theme:** HAM\_T01 — Revenge, Delay and Action

> **\[1\]** Shakespeare stages Hamlet's delay not as a psychological deficiency but as a structurally embedded crisis in which the ethical imperatives of revenge and conscience are rendered irreconcilable. **\[AO1 — thesis sentence establishing the conceptual argument: delay is structural, not personal.\]**
>
> **\[2\]** The Ghost's command to "Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder" (1.5) locks Hamlet into the revenge tragedy genre's central obligation, yet the qualifier "Taint not thy mind" simultaneously prohibits the moral contamination that revenge inevitably produces. **\[AO2 — close analysis of specific language: imperative verb "Revenge" vs. prohibitive "Taint not"; AO3 — embedded context: revenge tragedy genre and its conventions; AO1 — advancing the thesis by identifying the paradox.\]**
>
> **\[3\]** This double bind activates what Greenblatt identifies as a post-Reformation anxiety surrounding the Ghost's theological status: if the Ghost emerges from a Catholic Purgatory that Protestant England had officially abolished, then its commandment carries the authority of a doctrine the audience has been instructed to reject, and Hamlet's hesitation becomes not cowardice but a rational response to an impossible epistemological demand. **\[AO5 — Greenblatt's argument integrated as interpretive lens; AO3 — Reformation theology embedded within the analytical sentence, not listed separately; AO1 — developing the thesis: delay as rational, not pathological.\]**
>
> **\[4\]** The positioning of the "To be, or not to be" soliloquy at 3.1 — *after* the Mousetrap plan has been set — deepens the structural irony: Hamlet philosophises about the merits of action at the precise moment when he has already committed to a course of it, so that the speech functions not as deliberation but as deferral, an intellectual performance that substitutes for the physical act it contemplates. **\[AO2 — structural analysis: dramatic positioning of the soliloquy within the play's architecture; AO1 — advancing argument: the soliloquy as performance of delay; AO2 — antithesis: "deliberation" vs. "deferral".\]**
>
> **\[5\]** Against Bradley's sympathetic reading of Hamlet as a man "too good" for the task of revenge, de Grazia offers a materialist counter-argument: Hamlet's paralysis is not moral sensitivity but the structural consequence of dispossession, a prince stripped of succession and therefore of the political agency that would make revenge meaningful rather than merely violent. **\[AO5 — two competing critical positions (Bradley and de Grazia) evaluated against each other; AO1 — the student positions themselves within the debate, using the critics to complicate rather than close the argument.\]**
>
> **\[6\]** Shakespeare thus constructs delay as the play's governing condition — not a flaw in the protagonist but the dramatic engine that exposes the impossibility of ethical action within a court defined by surveillance, performance, and moral contamination. **\[AO1 — concluding sentence returns to the thesis at a higher level of conceptual abstraction; AO3 — embedded contextual resonance: the surveillance state of Elsinore.\]**

### What Makes This Level 5

-   **AO1 operates throughout:** the paragraph is a single, building argument, not a list of observations. Each sentence advances the thesis.

-   **AO2 works at two levels:** word-level (the imperative/prohibitive tension in the Ghost's command; antithesis of "deliberation" and "deferral") and structural (positioning of the soliloquy after the Mousetrap plan).

-   **AO3 is embedded, not bolted on:** Reformation theology and revenge tragedy conventions appear *within* analytical sentences, shaping interpretation rather than sitting in background blocks.

-   **AO5 is evaluative, not decorative:** two critics (Greenblatt, Bradley, de Grazia) are not merely cited but used to open genuine interpretive debate. The student positions themselves within the critical conversation.

-   **The paragraph reads as a critical voice:** it does not feel like a student ticking boxes. The AOs are fused into a single, confident analytical register.

### Practical Implications for the Student

**In Section A**, plan which critics you will use *before* you start writing. Map each of your four paragraphs to a critical lens or interpretive debate. This prevents the "bolt-on" problem where AO5 appears only in final sentences.

**In Section B**, the absence of a mandatory AO5 requirement means you have more space for AO2 depth. Use this space to analyse dramatic methods in close detail — staging, spectacle, structural positioning, prose rhythm — rather than trying to pad the essay with critical references you are not required to provide.

\[EXAM WARNING: Do not assume Section B is "easier" because it has fewer AOs. Section B rewards *density* of analysis. A 25-mark essay with sustained AO2 and embedded AO3 scores higher than a 35-mark essay that ticks all four AOs superficially.\]
