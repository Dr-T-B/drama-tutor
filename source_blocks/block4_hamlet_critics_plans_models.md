## 4a. AO5 Critical Profiles

Each profile provides the critic's name, school of thought, core position on *Hamlet*, key text(s), integration technique, a worked example showing how to embed the critic in an analytical sentence, and common misuses to avoid.

### 1. A.C. BRADLEY (1851–1935)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Character criticism / Romantic-humanist tradition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Hamlet is a man of exceptional moral sensitivity and intellectual brilliance whose "habitual feeling is one of disgust at life and everything in it." His delay stems not from cowardice or indecision but from a melancholic temperament that renders him incapable of sustained, purposive action. Hamlet is "too good" for the task revenge demands — his moral idealism is his tragic flaw.                                                             |
| Key text                  | *Shakespearean Tragedy* (1904)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| What Bradley unlocks      | A sympathetic, psychologically rich reading of Hamlet as a fundamentally good man destroyed by circumstances that demand a kind of action his nature cannot accommodate. Useful for exploring delay (HAM\_T01), conscience (HAM\_T09), and Hamlet's philosophical crisis (HAM\_T11).                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Integration technique     | Use Bradley to establish the sympathetic baseline, then complicate it with a materialist or feminist counter-reading. Bradley works best as a position to engage with rather than simply adopt.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Worked example            | Bradley's reading of Hamlet as morally paralysed illuminates the prayer scene (3.3), where the refusal to kill Claudius at prayer reveals a protagonist whose scruple — sending the king's soul to heaven would constitute imperfect revenge — demonstrates that Hamlet's moral sensitivity extends even to his enemy's spiritual welfare; yet this very scruple, as de Grazia argues, may mask a more material calculation about political timing \[AO5\]. |
| Common misuse             | Summarising Bradley as "Hamlet is too sensitive" without specifying *what* this sensitivity does to a *particular* moment in the text. Bradley is an argument, not a label.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |

### 2. ERNEST JONES (1879–1958)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Psychoanalytic / Freudian criticism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Hamlet cannot kill Claudius because Claudius has fulfilled Hamlet's own repressed Oedipal desire — to kill the father and possess the mother. To destroy Claudius would be to destroy the externalised realisation of his unconscious wish. Hamlet's delay is therefore not moral but psychosexual: he is paralysed by a guilt he cannot consciously acknowledge.                                                                                                                                                   |
| Key text                  | *Hamlet and Oedipus* (1949)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| What Jones unlocks        | A reading that explains the closet scene's extraordinary intensity (3.4), the disproportionate rage directed at Gertrude's sexuality, and the otherwise puzzling relationship between the revenge plot and the maternal preoccupation. Useful for HAM\_T01, HAM\_T08, HAM\_T10.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Integration technique     | Jones is most effective when used to open questions rather than close them. Frame the Oedipal reading as an interpretive possibility that illuminates specific textual moments, then test its limits against a historicist or materialist alternative.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| Worked example            | Jones's Oedipal reading provides a compelling framework for the closet scene (3.4), where Hamlet's verbal assault on Gertrude's sexuality — "In the rank sweat of an enseamèd bed" — exceeds any political or moral justification and suggests a psychic investment in the maternal body that the play's conscious discourse cannot account for; however, Adelman's revision of this framework locates the anxiety not in individual psychology but in the cultural construction of maternal contamination \[AO5\]. |
| Common misuse             | Treating the Oedipal reading as proven fact rather than an interpretive lens. The examiner wants you to *use* the theory to generate a reading, not to diagnose Hamlet as though he were a real patient.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |

### 3. T.S. ELIOT (1888–1965)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Formalist / New Critical                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | *Hamlet* is "most certainly an artistic failure." Hamlet's emotion exceeds its "objective correlative" — the play provides no dramatic situation adequate to the intensity of Hamlet's disgust. The protagonist's feeling is "in excess of the facts as they appear," and Shakespeare was unable to find an adequate dramatic vehicle for the emotion he wished to express.                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Key text                  | "Hamlet and His Problems" (1919)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| What Eliot unlocks        | A formal critique that foregrounds the gap between Hamlet's emotional intensity and the dramatic situations that ostensibly produce it. Useful for questioning whether the play's philosophical ambition exceeds its dramatic structure (HAM\_T11), and for discussing the relationship between language and dramatic action (HAM\_T01).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Integration technique     | Eliot is best used as a provocation — his judgement is deliberately extreme and most effective when contested. Frame Eliot's critique, then argue that what he identifies as a flaw is in fact the play's defining achievement.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Worked example            | Eliot's complaint that Hamlet's emotion lacks an adequate "objective correlative" finds its strongest evidence in "What a piece of work is a man" (2.2), where the philosophical scope — the entire humanist tradition, the nature of existence — vastly exceeds the dramatic occasion (a conversation with two spies); yet this disproportion is arguably the point: Shakespeare constructs a protagonist whose consciousness operates on a plane that the revenge genre cannot contain, making the formal "failure" Eliot identifies the play's most radical achievement \[AO5\]. |
| Common misuse             | Simply agreeing or disagreeing with Eliot. The examiner wants you to *use* the concept of the objective correlative as an analytical tool, not to settle the question of whether *Hamlet* is a failure.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |

### 4. ELAINE SHOWALTER (b. 1941)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Feminist criticism / cultural history                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Ophelia has been systematically appropriated by male critical and theatrical traditions — sentimentalised, eroticised, or pathologised — and each era reinvents her in its own image. Her madness should be read not as personal breakdown but as the cultural expression of silenced female experience: her songs and fragmented speech articulate desires (sexual, political, grieving) that the patriarchal order of Elsinore has denied her a legitimate language to voice.              |
| Key text                  | "Representing Ophelia: Women, Madness, and the Responsibilities of Feminist Criticism" (1985)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| What Showalter unlocks    | A historicist feminist reading that reveals the cultural construction of "Ophelia" across different periods and challenges students to recognise that their own reading of her is itself culturally conditioned. Useful for HAM\_T02, HAM\_T08, and any question about Ophelia's dramatic function.                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Integration technique     | Use Showalter to expose the gendered assumptions embedded in other readings (e.g., Bradley's sentimentalisation of Ophelia) and to reframe the mad scene (4.5) as an act of subversive expression rather than passive collapse.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| Worked example            | Showalter's argument that Ophelia's mad songs constitute "the displacement of her unspeakable feelings onto the medium of song" reframes the flower scene (4.5) as an act of coded resistance rather than pathological fragmentation \[AO5\]: the ballad form connects Ophelia to a popular, oral, and predominantly female tradition that operates outside the court's male-authored discourse, making madness paradoxically the only available mode of female self-expression in Elsinore. |
| Common misuse             | Reducing Showalter to "Ophelia is a feminist victim." Showalter's argument is about cultural *construction* — how successive eras have built Ophelia in their own image — not simply about victimhood.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |

### 5. JONATHAN DOLLIMORE (b. 1948)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Cultural materialism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Power in Elsinore is not an aberration caused by one man's crime but the normal operation of a political system that sustains itself through surveillance, performance, and the strategic management of moral discourse. Claudius's regime is not uniquely corrupt — it merely makes visible the violence that all political order depends upon but normally conceals. Moral language (conscience, virtue, duty) is deployed by the powerful to legitimise their authority, not to constrain it. |
| Key text                  | *Radical Tragedy: Religion, Ideology and Power in the Drama of Shakespeare and His Contemporaries* (1984; 3rd edn 2004)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| What Dollimore unlocks    | A reading that shifts attention from individual psychology to systemic analysis — corruption is structural rather than personal, and the play exposes how power uses moral discourse ideologically. Useful for HAM\_T03, HAM\_T04, HAM\_T07, and any question about the political dimension of the play.                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Integration technique     | Use Dollimore to challenge readings that locate the play's problems in individual characters (Bradley's Hamlet, Jones's Hamlet) by insisting that the causes are systemic. Dollimore pairs well with de Grazia's materialist approach.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Worked example            | Dollimore's reading of Claudius's regime as the normal operation of power — rather than an aberration from it — reshapes the "Something is rotten" diagnosis (1.4): the rottenness is not introduced by Claudius's crime but pre-exists it, so that the play exposes the violence that sustains all political order rather than critiquing one particular ruler's moral failure \[AO5\].                                                                                                         |
| Common misuse             | Using Dollimore as a synonym for "power is bad." His argument is more specific: power constitutes itself through the strategic deployment of moral discourse, so that what appears to be ethical judgement is actually ideological management.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |

### 6. CATHERINE BELSEY (1940–2021)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Post-structuralist / cultural materialism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Renaissance drama stages the crisis of the emergent modern subject — the idea of a coherent, self-knowing individual. Hamlet's quest for authenticity ("that within which passeth show," 1.2) is structurally impossible because the play repeatedly demonstrates that identity is *constructed* through performance rather than *discovered* beneath it. The "self" Hamlet seeks to be true to does not exist as a stable entity prior to its theatrical expression.                   |
| Key text                  | *The Subject of Tragedy: Identity and Difference in Renaissance Drama* (1985)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| What Belsey unlocks       | A reading that connects the play's metatheatrical concerns to a philosophical argument about selfhood and identity. Useful for HAM\_T06, HAM\_T02, HAM\_T11, and any question about performance, appearance, or identity.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Integration technique     | Use Belsey to elevate analysis of theatricality from a thematic observation ("the play is about appearance vs. reality") to a philosophical argument about the construction of the subject. She pairs well with the metatheatrical analysis of the Mousetrap and the antic disposition.                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Worked example            | Belsey's argument that Renaissance selfhood is constructed through performance rather than discovered beneath it reframes the "antic disposition" (1.5 onwards): Hamlet does not "pretend" to be mad by concealing a stable inner self — rather, the sustained act of performing madness destabilises the boundary between mask and face, so that the question the court asks ("is he mad?") becomes unanswerable because there is no essential Hamlet beneath the performance \[AO5\]. |
| Common misuse             | Paraphrasing Belsey as "identity is just a performance." Her argument is historically specific: the *Renaissance* subject is in crisis because the concept of a coherent self is *emerging* during this period, and drama stages the instability of that emergence.                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |

### 7. STEPHEN GREENBLATT (b. 1943)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | New historicism / cultural poetics                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | *Hamlet* is haunted by the cultural memory of Catholic Purgatory, which the Protestant Reformation had officially abolished but which persisted in the popular imagination. The Ghost emerges from this theological no-man's-land, and its command to "Remember me" is simultaneously a dramatic device and a trace of a suppressed religious practice (prayer for the dead) that Protestant culture could neither fully embrace nor entirely abandon. The play stages the "half-life of purgatory" in the Protestant imagination. |
| Key text                  | *Hamlet in Purgatory* (2001)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| What Greenblatt unlocks   | A reading that embeds the play's supernatural and theological elements in the specific historical circumstances of the English Reformation. Useful for HAM\_T09, HAM\_T05, HAM\_T01, and any question about the Ghost, the afterlife, or religious conscience.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Integration technique     | Use Greenblatt to transform contextual knowledge from background information into an interpretive lens — the Reformation is not merely "when the play was written" but the cultural crisis that generates the play's central conflicts.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Worked example            | Greenblatt's argument that the Ghost occupies the "half-life of purgatory" in the Protestant imagination transforms the prayer scene (3.3): Hamlet's theological reasoning — killing Claudius at prayer would send him to heaven — depends on a Catholic framework of sin, repentance, and damnation that Protestant doctrine had rendered uncertain, so that Hamlet's scruple operates within a belief system whose authority his own culture has officially revoked \[AO5\].                                                     |
| Common misuse             | Reducing Greenblatt to "the play is about Purgatory." His argument is about the cultural *afterlife* of abolished beliefs — how practices, anxieties, and emotional structures survive the institutions that produced them.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |

### 8. MARGRETA DE GRAZIA (b. 1945)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Materialist / historicist criticism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | The critical tradition has misread Hamlet by privileging his interiority — treating the play as a drama of consciousness. In fact, Hamlet's crisis is rooted in *material dispossession*: stripped of the succession by Claudius's election, he lacks the political agency that would make revenge a meaningful act of restoration rather than a private act of violence. "Hamlet's problem" is economic and political before it is psychological. The delay is not produced by excessive thought but by the structural impossibility of acting from a position of dispossession. |
| Key text                  | *"Hamlet" Without Hamlet* (2007)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| What de Grazia unlocks    | A reading that radically reframes the play by displacing psychology in favour of material circumstances. Useful for HAM\_T01, HAM\_T10, HAM\_T04, and any question that risks becoming purely psychological.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Integration technique     | Use de Grazia to challenge the dominance of psychological readings (Bradley, Jones). She is most effective as a counterpoint: "Where Bradley reads delay as melancholy, de Grazia reframes it as the structural consequence of dispossession..."                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Worked example            | De Grazia's materialist reading reshapes the "To be, or not to be" soliloquy (3.1) from an existential meditation into a political calculation: "the undiscovered country" gestures not toward metaphysical dread but toward the territory of kingship from which Hamlet has been exiled, and "the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune" becomes a description of material injustice rather than abstract suffering \[AO5\].                                                                                                                                                   |
| Common misuse             | Treating de Grazia as merely "another interpretation." Her argument is a methodological challenge — she argues that the entire tradition of reading Hamlet psychologically is an anachronistic projection of post-Romantic assumptions onto a pre-Romantic text.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |

### 9. JANET ADELMAN (1941–2010)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Psychoanalytic-feminist criticism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | The play's crisis of corruption originates in the maternal body. Gertrude's sexuality — specifically, the remarriage — is experienced by Hamlet as the source of a contamination that spreads outward to encompass the state. The disease imagery, the "unweeded garden," and the closet scene's verbal assault on Gertrude's body all locate corruption in female sexuality and maternal desire. The play constructs a "fantasy of maternal malevolence" in which the mother's body is the origin of pollution. |
| Key text                  | *Suffocating Mothers: Fantasies of Maternal Origin in Shakespeare's Plays,* Hamlet *to* The Tempest (1992)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| What Adelman unlocks      | A reading that connects the play's disease imagery to a specifically gendered anxiety about the maternal body. Useful for HAM\_T07, HAM\_T08, HAM\_T10, and any question about corruption, gender, or the closet scene.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Integration technique     | Use Adelman to show how the play's apparently "political" themes (corruption, decay, disease) are rooted in a gendered anxiety about female sexuality. She works well in combination with Showalter and as a complication of Dollimore's systemic analysis.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Worked example            | Adelman's reading of the "unweeded garden" (1.2) as a figure for the contaminated maternal body reveals the gendered origin of the play's corruption theme: the word "rank" — meaning both overgrown and foul-smelling — maps sexual disgust onto natural imagery, so that Hamlet's revulsion at his mother's remarriage generates the disease metaphors that the play will subsequently apply to the state, making female desire the root cause of political disorder \[AO5\].                                  |
| Common misuse             | Treating Adelman as identical to Jones. Adelman's argument is about the *cultural construction* of maternal contamination, not about individual Oedipal psychology — she reads the play's anxiety as ideological, not clinical.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |

### 10. MICHAEL NEILL (b. 1942)

| **Field**                 | **Content**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| School                    | Cultural history / performance studies                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Core position on *Hamlet* | Death in Renaissance tragedy is inherently theatrical — a spectacle staged for an audience. The play's deaths are performances of mortality designed to provoke the audience into contemplating the relationship between violence, power, and representation. The graveyard scene and the final catastrophe expose the degree to which death in *Hamlet* is always a public event whose meaning is constructed by those who narrate it (Horatio's promise to "speak to th'yet unknowing world," 5.2). |
| Key text                  | *Issues of Death: Mortality and Identity in English Renaissance Tragedy* (1997)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| What Neill unlocks        | A reading that connects the play's engagement with death to its metatheatrical self-consciousness — death in *Hamlet* is simultaneously a dramatic event and a comment on the theatre's capacity to represent mortality. Useful for HAM\_T05, HAM\_T12, HAM\_T06, and any question about the graveyard scene, the catastrophe, or the play's ending.                                                                                                                                                  |
| Integration technique     | Use Neill to elevate analysis of the play's ending from plot summary to a reflection on narrative, representation, and the cultural work of tragedy. He pairs well with discussions of Horatio's survival and the Fortinbras ending.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Worked example            | Neill's argument that death in Renaissance tragedy is always a staged spectacle transforms the final scene (5.2): the accumulation of bodies — Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius, Hamlet — is not merely the fulfilment of the revenge plot but a theatrical display designed to force the audience into confronting the relationship between the violence they have paid to watch and the political violence it represents \[AO5\].                                                                        |
| Common misuse             | Reducing Neill to "death is important in the play." His argument is specifically about how death functions as *representation* — a cultural practice of making mortality visible, public, and meaningful through theatrical staging.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |

## 4b. Section A Essay Plans

Each plan provides a question, thesis, 4-paragraph structure with mapped quotes (by Quote ID), an AO integration grid showing where each AO is served, and a timing plan. All plans follow the Section A stem: *"Explore \[topic\] in* Hamlet. *You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading."*

### ESSAY PLAN 1

**Question:** *Explore the significance of delay in* Hamlet. *You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.*

**Thesis \[AO1\]:** Shakespeare constructs delay not as Hamlet's personal failing but as the structural condition of a tragedy in which the ethical, theological, and political frameworks required to authorise action have been rendered mutually irreconcilable.

**Primary themes:** HAM\_T01, HAM\_T09, HAM\_T11

**Introduction (3–4 sentences):** State thesis. Establish that delay is the play's governing dramatic principle, not a character flaw. Signal the critical debate: Bradley's psychological reading vs. de Grazia's materialist reframing.

**Paragraph 1 — The Revenge Imperative and Its Contradictions**

*Argument:* The Ghost's command establishes a double bind that makes delay structurally inevitable.

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                    | **AO served**                                                  |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q001    | "Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder" | AO2 (imperative), AO3 (revenge code vs. Christian prohibition) |
| HAM\_Q008    | "Taint not thy mind"                         | AO2 (antithetical imperative), AO1 (paradox advanced)          |

*AO5 move:* Open with Greenblatt's argument that the Ghost's command presupposes a Catholic Purgatory whose authority the Reformation has undermined — delay is the rational response to an epistemologically unstable commandment.

**Paragraph 2 — The Prayer Scene: Theological Paralysis**

*Argument:* The prayer scene crystallises delay as a theological mechanism: Hamlet's reason for not killing is simultaneously rational (within Catholic sacramental logic) and based on a false premise (the prayer has failed).

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                        | **AO served**                                                               |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q004    | "Now might I do it pat... That would be scanned" | AO2 (caesura, soliloquy), AO3 (sacramental theology)                        |
| HAM\_Q056    | "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below"      | AO2 (spatial antithesis), AO5 (Greenblatt — collapse of penitential system) |

*AO5 move:* Jones's psychoanalytic reading suggests the theological reasoning is a rationalisation masking Oedipal resistance. Counter with de Grazia: the refusal to kill may reflect a political calculation about the conditions under which revenge would constitute meaningful restoration.

**Paragraph 3 — Language as Deferral**

*Argument:* Hamlet's soliloquies do not merely describe delay — they *enact* it. The intellectual energy that defines the protagonist is itself the mechanism of his paralysis.

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                | **AO served**                                                                                           |
|--------------|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q003    | "To be, or not to be"                    | AO2 (antithesis, structural positioning after Mousetrap plan), AO3 (post-Reformation afterlife)         |
| HAM\_Q005    | "How all occasions do inform against me" | AO2 (personification, structural positioning as final soliloquy), AO1 (self-diagnosis as form of delay) |

*AO5 move:* Bradley reads the soliloquies as evidence of temperamental paralysis — a man too reflective to act. Eliot's "objective correlative" argument complicates this by suggesting the emotion exceeds the dramatic framework. Counter both with a structural reading: the soliloquies are *formally* positioned to delay the action, making delay a property of the play's architecture, not just the protagonist's psychology.

**Paragraph 4 — Resolution: Readiness Replaces Resolution**

*Argument:* Hamlet's transformation in Act 5 does not resolve delay through action but transcends it through surrender to providence — "the readiness is all" replaces the need for decisive control.

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                 | **AO served**                                                    |
|--------------|-------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q006    | "The readiness is all"                    | AO2 (monosyllabic diction, aphorism), AO3 (Calvinist providence) |
| HAM\_Q036    | "There's a divinity that shapes our ends" | AO2 (craft metaphor), AO3 (providential theology)                |

*AO5 move:* Bradley reads this as tragic wisdom. De Grazia's materialist reading contests this by arguing that "readiness" is not philosophical achievement but resignation to political impotence. The essay's conclusion should position itself within this debate: is Hamlet's acceptance a spiritual transformation or a surrender?

**Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** Return to thesis at a higher level. Delay is the play's *dramatic engine*, not a problem to be solved: it exposes the impossibility of ethical action within a world defined by theological contradiction, political corruption, and the paralysing brilliance of a mind that understands more than it can act upon.

**AO Integration Grid:**

| **AO** | **Where it appears**                                                                                                                                     |
|--------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| AO1    | Thesis; every topic sentence; concluding synthesis                                                                                                       |
| AO2    | Every paragraph: imperative/antithesis (P1), caesura/spatial antithesis (P2), structural positioning/antithesis (P3), monosyllabic diction/metaphor (P4) |
| AO3    | Embedded in P1 (Reformation theology), P2 (sacramental theology), P3 (post-Reformation afterlife), P4 (Calvinist providence)                             |

**Timing:** 5 min planning → 70 min writing (≈17 min per paragraph + intro/conclusion) → review in Section B buffer.

### ESSAY PLAN 2

**Question:** *Explore how Shakespeare presents madness in* Hamlet. *You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors and ideas from your critical reading.*

**Thesis \[AO1\]:** Shakespeare uses madness to destabilise the boundary between performed and authentic selfhood, staging the distinction between strategic sanity and genuine psychological collapse as fundamentally undecidable in a court built on surveillance and perpetual performance.

**Primary themes:** HAM\_T02, HAM\_T06, HAM\_T08

**Paragraph 1 — The Antic Disposition: Performed Madness as Counter-Surveillance**

*Argument:* Hamlet's announced performance of madness (1.5) converts the dramatic question from "is he mad?" to "where does performance end?"

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                     | **AO served**                                                        |
|--------------|-------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q009    | "Put an antic disposition on" | AO2 (dramatic irony/foreshadowing), AO3 (madness as licensed speech) |
| HAM\_Q010    | "Mad north-north-west"        | AO2 (riddling prose, compass imagery), AO3 (humoral theory)          |

*AO5 move:* Belsey argues that performed madness destabilises the concept of a coherent self — the act of performing madness undermines the stable identity it is supposed to conceal.

**Paragraph 2 — The Nunnery Scene: Madness Directed at the Female Body**

*Argument:* The nunnery scene blurs the line between strategic performance and genuine misogynistic rage, directing the antic disposition's violence onto Ophelia.

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                 | **AO served**                                                     |
|--------------|-------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| HAM\_Q011    | "Get thee to a nunnery"                   | AO2 (double entendre), AO3 (female chastity, patriarchal control) |
| HAM\_Q059    | "One face... you make yourselves another" | AO2 (antithesis), AO3 (anti-cosmetic literature)                  |

*AO5 move:* Showalter reads the nunnery scene as the patriarchal appropriation of female identity — Hamlet denies Ophelia autonomous selfhood. Counter with Belsey: the attack on cosmetics extends the play's broader anxiety about all identity as performance.

**Paragraph 3 — Ophelia's Madness: The Return of the Repressed**

*Argument:* Ophelia's madness is not merely pathological but constitutes a counter-language that articulates what courtly decorum has suppressed.

| **Quote ID** | **Quote**                                | **AO served**                                                                |
|--------------|------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
