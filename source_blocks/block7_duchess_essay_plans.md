## 7a. FIVE ESSAY PLANS

### ESSAY PLAN 1: Surveillance and Control

**Question:** Explore the significance of surveillance and control in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

**Controlling theme:** MAL\_T03 (Surveillance, Control and Entrapment) **Secondary themes:** MAL\_T01 (Power, Corruption and Courtly Degeneration), MAL\_T06 (Marriage, Sexuality and Bodily Autonomy)

**Conceptual thesis:** Webster presents surveillance not merely as a political tool but as a structural condition of courtly existence, in which the Duchess's private desires become public property precisely because patriarchal authority demands the total visibility of the female body.

#### Paragraph 1 — Ferdinand's Commissioning of Bosola as Intelligencer

**Topic sentence:** Webster establishes surveillance as the foundational mechanism of fraternal control in the opening act, when Ferdinand installs Bosola as his "intelligencer" within the Duchess's household, converting a domestic space into a theatre of observation. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q009 — Ferdinand to Bosola: "Be yourself; / Keep your old garb of melancholy; 'twill express / You envy those that stand above your reach, / Yet strive not to come near 'em" (1.1)

**AO2 focus:** Imperative mood ("Be yourself", "Keep") — Ferdinand's commands construct Bosola as an instrument; the irony of "Be yourself" when Bosola is being asked to perform a role reveals that surveillance corrupts the watcher as well as the watched. Dramatic irony: the audience knows Bosola's function before the Duchess does. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** In Jacobean court culture, intelligence networks were a routine instrument of political control — Sir Robert Cecil's spy apparatus under James I made surveillance a normalised feature of aristocratic life, not an aberration. Webster reflects this systemic reality. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 2 — The Bedchamber Scene (3.2) and the Invasion of Private Space

**Topic sentence:** The bedchamber scene stages surveillance as a violation of the most intimate domestic space, as Ferdinand enters the Duchess's private chamber uninvited and unseen — an intrusion that collapses the boundary between public authority and private selfhood. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q021 — Ferdinand: "Virtue, where art thou hid? What hideous thing / Is it that doth eclipse thee?" (3.2)

**AO2 focus:** Interrogative rhetoric and light/dark imagery — Ferdinand's questions are not genuine enquiries but rhetorical assertions of moral judgement. "Eclipse" figures the Duchess's sexuality as a darkening of virtue, an astronomical metaphor that elevates Ferdinand's disgust to a cosmic register. The staging of Ferdinand appearing in darkness with a poniard makes control physically threatening. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Early modern anxieties about widows' sexuality rendered the Duchess's private choices a matter of public — specifically male familial — concern. A widow's body was legally freer than a wife's, which made its regulation a site of heightened patriarchal anxiety. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 3 — Bosola's Apricot Test (2.1) — Surveillance as Bodily Intrusion

**Topic sentence:** Webster dramatises the invasiveness of surveillance through Bosola's apricot stratagem, in which the act of watching becomes inseparable from the act of controlling the Duchess's body itself. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q015 — Bosola: "A whirlwind strike off these bawd-farthingales, / For, but in a modesty of honour, / They serve but as a cover for the belly" (2.1)

**AO2 focus:** The apricots function as a dramatic device — a prop that physically tests the Duchess's body by inducing symptoms that reveal her pregnancy. Bosola's aside to the audience ("bawd-farthingales") deploys animal imagery and bawdy language to reduce the Duchess from a sovereign figure to a body to be read, inspected, and decoded. Prose rhythm in Bosola's commentary signals his detachment from courtly decorum. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The Jacobean stage's fascination with the legibility of the body — the idea that concealed sin would inevitably manifest physically — underpins this scene. Webster draws on humoral medicine's assumption that the body could not sustain deception indefinitely. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 4 — The Duchess's Defiance and the Limits of Surveillance

**Topic sentence:** Ultimately, Webster suggests that surveillance, however total, fails to contain the Duchess's selfhood, as her declaration of identity in Act 4 constitutes a refusal to be defined by the gaze that has pursued her throughout the play. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q037 — Duchess: "I am Duchess of Malfi still" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The monosyllabic simplicity of "still" — placed at the end of the line, stressed by its position — carries enormous weight. It is a declarative sentence, assertive rather than interrogative or imperative, and its brevity contrasts with the elaborate rhetoric of her tormentors. The word "still" simultaneously means "even now" and "always", compressing temporal resistance into a single syllable. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** In a culture where identity was conferred by rank and could be stripped by male relatives, the Duchess's self-naming is a radical assertion — she claims an identity that her brothers have spent the entire play attempting to erase. \[AO3\]

### ESSAY PLAN 2: Female Agency and Transgression ⏱️ TIMED PLAN (50 MINUTES)

**Question:** Explore the ways Webster presents female agency and transgression in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

**Controlling theme:** MAL\_T02 (Gender, Patriarchy and Female Agency) **Secondary themes:** MAL\_T06 (Marriage, Sexuality and Bodily Autonomy), MAL\_T05 (Class, Rank and Social Transgression)

**Conceptual thesis:** Webster constructs the Duchess's agency as genuinely transgressive — she acts, chooses, and desires — yet the play simultaneously reveals that female agency within a patriarchal system can only be exercised at the cost of annihilation, making her freedom both heroic and structurally impossible.

#### Paragraph 1 — The Wooing Scene: The Duchess as Active Desiring Subject (1.1)

**Topic sentence:** Webster's wooing scene inverts the conventions of courtly romance by positioning the Duchess as the initiator of desire, making her the active subject in a transaction that early modern culture reserved exclusively for men. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q005 — Duchess: "The misery of us that are born great: / We are forced to woo because none dare woo us" (1.1)

**⏱️ 12 minutes. Open with thesis, move quickly into quote, spend bulk of time on AO2 chain.**

**AO2 focus:** The paradox of "forced to woo" — agency is presented as compulsion, and freedom as a form of constraint. "Born great" positions rank as both privilege and prison. The Duchess controls the theatrical staging of her own proposal, turning Antonio from suitor into recipient, reversing the conventional dramatic grammar of courtship. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Jacobean widows possessed greater legal autonomy than wives or unmarried women — they could own property, manage estates, and technically choose their own partners. Yet this legal freedom existed within a culture that viewed independent female desire with deep suspicion, making the Duchess's agency legally permissible but socially transgressive. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 2 — Marriage Across Rank: Desire as Class Transgression (1.1–2.1)

**Topic sentence:** The Duchess's transgression is doubled because her desire is not merely sexual but socially disruptive — by marrying her steward, she collapses the class hierarchy that her brothers' authority depends upon. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q007 — Ferdinand: "Foolish men, / That e'er will trust their honour in a bark / Made of so slight weak bulrush as is woman" (2.5)

**⏱️ 12 minutes. Focus on how Ferdinand's language reveals the intersection of gender and class anxiety.**

**AO2 focus:** The metaphor of "bark" (ship) and "bulrush" reduces the Duchess to fragile material, an object incapable of bearing the weight of aristocratic honour. The enjambment across "bark / Made of" creates a moment of suspension that mimics the precariousness Ferdinand fears. His generalisation to "woman" reveals that his rage is not about this particular marriage but about female autonomy as a category. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Marriages across rank threatened the Jacobean social hierarchy at its foundation — bloodline, inheritance, and title. Ferdinand's fury reflects not personal eccentricity but a culturally legible anxiety about aristocratic contamination through misalliance. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 3 — The Containment of Agency: Imprisonment and the Wax Figures (4.1)

**Topic sentence:** Webster stages the systematic dismantling of the Duchess's agency through her imprisonment, in which her brothers' instruments of psychological torture — the wax figures of Antonio and her children — attempt to reduce her from agent to spectator of her own destruction. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q033 — Duchess: "I account this world a tedious theatre, / For I do play a part in't 'gainst my will" (4.1)

**⏱️ 12 minutes. The metatheatrical quote is rich for AO2 — spend time on the theatre metaphor.**

**AO2 focus:** The metatheatrical metaphor — "tedious theatre" — is double-edged: it acknowledges the Duchess's loss of control (she plays a "part" assigned to her) while simultaneously revealing her capacity for self-conscious reflection, which is itself a form of intellectual agency. "Tedious" is a critical judgement, not a passive acceptance — she evaluates the spectacle imposed upon her and finds it wanting. The contrast between "I do play" (active verb) and "'gainst my will" (passive condition) compresses the paradox of constrained agency into a single line. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The wax figures draw on the Jacobean theatrical fascination with spectacle, illusion, and memento mori — audiences accustomed to public execution and anatomical display would recognise the scene as a deliberate cruelty that weaponises visual culture against its victim. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 4 — "I am Duchess of Malfi still": What Survives Annihilation

**Topic sentence:** The Duchess's final self-assertion — "I am Duchess of Malfi still" — constitutes Webster's most radical statement about female agency: that identity, once claimed, cannot be fully destroyed, even when the body that carried it is annihilated. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q037 — Duchess: "I am Duchess of Malfi still" (4.2)

**⏱️ 10 minutes. This is the conceptual capstone. Prioritise the argument over exhaustive AO2.**

**AO2 focus:** The declarative simplicity — subject, verb, complement, adverb — resists the ornate, Latinate rhetoric of the brothers. "Still" carries temporal weight (even now, after all of this) and existential weight (always, essentially). The line is spoken in the face of death, giving it the force of a final testament. Its brevity is its power — it refuses to engage with the elaborate justifications of patriarchal authority on their own terms. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** In a culture that defined women's identity through their relationships to men — daughter, wife, widow — the Duchess's self-naming is a defiant refusal of relational definition. She claims a title that is hers by right but that her brothers have attempted to revoke by force. \[AO3\]

### ESSAY PLAN 3: The Significance of Bosola

**Question:** Explore the significance of Bosola in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

**Controlling theme:** MAL\_T11 (Integrity, Identity and Moral Resistance) **Secondary themes:** MAL\_T01 (Power, Corruption and Courtly Degeneration), MAL\_T12 (Fate, Providence and Nihilism)

**Conceptual thesis:** Bosola functions as Webster's most complex dramatic instrument — simultaneously agent and victim of the corrupt court — whose trajectory from cynical intelligencer to guilt-stricken avenger stages the impossibility of moral integrity within a system that rewards only complicity.

#### Paragraph 1 — Bosola as Malcontent: The Corrupted Intellectual (1.1)

**Topic sentence:** Webster introduces Bosola through the malcontent tradition, establishing him as a figure whose intelligence has been degraded by service to corrupt power — a man who sees clearly but cannot act cleanly. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q003 — Bosola: "I fell into the galleys in your service, where, for two years together, I wore two towels instead of a shirt" (1.1)

**AO2 focus:** The physical degradation — "two towels instead of a shirt" — grounds Bosola's resentment in bodily suffering, not abstract complaint. Prose rhythm (Bosola frequently speaks in prose rather than verse) signals his exclusion from the courtly register of power. Direct address creates intimacy with the audience, positioning him as a commentator figure. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The malcontent was a recognised stage type in Jacobean drama — a disaffected intellectual whose cynicism reflected genuine grievances about patronage, preferment, and the corruption of merit-based advancement in a system governed by favour and bloodline. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 2 — The Spy Who Sees Too Much: Bosola's Moral Complicity (2.1–3.5)

**Topic sentence:** As Ferdinand's intelligencer, Bosola occupies a morally untenable position — his surveillance of the Duchess generates knowledge that he recognises as ethically compromised, yet he continues to serve because the court offers no alternative economy of survival. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q016 — Bosola: "What rests but I reveal / All to my lord?" (2.3)

**AO2 focus:** The interrogative "What rests" — the question is rhetorical, signalling that Bosola perceives no alternative, yet the very act of asking reveals his awareness that alternatives should exist. "Rests" implies exhaustion of options, not willing choice. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Jacobean patronage culture created precisely this bind: advancement depended on powerful sponsors, and the price of patronage was moral subordination. Bosola's dilemma is structurally produced, not a personal failing. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 3 — The Executioner Who Repents: Bosola in Act 4

**Topic sentence:** Webster transforms Bosola from observer to active agent of destruction in Act 4, but crucially stages his participation in the Duchess's murder as the moment that triggers his moral collapse — the act of killing produces the conscience he has spent the play suppressing. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q038 — Bosola (after the Duchess's death): "Off, my painted honour! / While with vain hopes our faculties we tire, / We seem to sweat in ice and freeze in fire" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The antithetical pairings ("sweat in ice", "freeze in fire") create a rhetorical structure of paradox that mirrors Bosola's psychological state — he is trapped between contradictory moral impulses. "Painted honour" exposes his reward as a fraud, using visual imagery (paint as cosmetic, superficial) to figure his self-disgust. The shift from prose to verse in Bosola's later speeches signals his entry into tragic seriousness. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The revenge tragedy convention demanded that the instrument of tyranny become the agent of retribution — Bosola's turn fulfils a generic expectation, but Webster complicates it by making the turn motivated by guilt rather than honour. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 4 — "In a mist": Bosola's Death and the Failure of Redemption (5.5)

**Topic sentence:** Bosola's death — achieved through accidental violence in near-darkness — denies him the clarity of redemptive purpose, staging Webster's bleakest insight: that moral awakening in a corrupt world comes too late and achieves nothing. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q048 — Bosola: "In a mist: I know not how; / Such a mistake as I have often seen / In a play" (5.5)

**AO2 focus:** The metatheatrical self-awareness ("as I have often seen / In a play") collapses the boundary between Bosola's fictional world and the audience's reality. "In a mist" uses a spatial metaphor for moral and epistemological confusion. The enjambment forces a pause after "how", enacting the uncertainty it describes. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Webster's nihilistic ending — where the avenger dies confused and ineffectual — subverts the revenge tragedy's promise that violence restores order. This reflects a broader Jacobean scepticism about providential justice. \[AO3\]

### ESSAY PLAN 4: Madness

**Question:** Explore the ways Webster uses madness in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

**Controlling theme:** MAL\_T04 (Madness, Cruelty and Psychological Violence) **Secondary themes:** MAL\_T08 (Revenge, Violence and Jacobean Tragic Excess), MAL\_T10 (The Body, Disease and Physical Imagery)

**Conceptual thesis:** Webster deploys madness as both a weapon of patriarchal cruelty and a mirror of courtly corruption — the madmen's pageant inflicted upon the Duchess exposes the irrationality not of its victim but of the power structures that produce it, while Ferdinand's lycanthropy reveals that the true madness belongs to the oppressor.

#### Paragraph 1 — The Madmen's Masque (4.2): Spectacle as Psychological Weapon

**Topic sentence:** Webster stages the masque of madmen as a deliberate instrument of psychological torture, in which the conventions of courtly entertainment are perverted into a mechanism of cruelty — the Duchess is forced to become an audience for her own destruction. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q035 — Duchess: "Nothing but noise and folly / Can keep me in my right wits, whereas reason / And silence make me stark mad" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The paradox — noise preserves sanity, silence produces madness — inverts expected logic, suggesting that the Duchess's rational world has been so thoroughly distorted that conventional categories no longer hold. The masque form itself is a dramatic method: Webster takes a genre associated with courtly celebration and aristocratic order and fills it with chaotic, grotesque content. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The Jacobean masque was a form associated with royal power and ideological order (Ben Jonson's court masques). Webster's anti-masque — madmen instead of allegorical figures — directly inverts this association, staging disorder where the audience expects harmony. Early modern attitudes to madness, shaped by humoral theory and the spectacle of Bedlam, made the display of the insane a recognisable (if disturbing) form of entertainment. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 2 — Ferdinand's Lycanthropy (5.2): The Oppressor's Madness

**Topic sentence:** Ferdinand's lycanthropy — his delusion that he is transforming into a wolf — relocates madness from the victim to the oppressor, revealing that the pathology of the play lies not in the Duchess's desire but in the possessive violence of patriarchal authority. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q044 — Ferdinand: "One met the duke 'bout midnight in a lane / Behind Saint Mark's church, with the leg of a man / Upon his shoulder" (5.2, reported by a Doctor)

**AO2 focus:** The reported speech distances the audience from Ferdinand — he is no longer a speaking subject but an observed spectacle, inverting his earlier role as the observer of the Duchess. The Gothic imagery (midnight, churchyard, dismembered body) draws on revenge tragedy's excess to make Ferdinand's madness physically grotesque. Animal imagery — the wolf — connects to the play's sustained bestiary, where human behaviour is repeatedly figured through predatory animals. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Lycanthropy was a recognised medical condition in early modern Europe, discussed in treatises on melancholy (including Robert Burton's *Anatomy of Melancholy*, published 1621). Webster's use of it connects Ferdinand's madness to the period's understanding of excessive passion as physiological disease, not merely moral failure. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 3 — The Duchess's Resistance to Madness (4.2)

**Topic sentence:** The Duchess's refusal to succumb to madness under sustained psychological assault constitutes one of the play's most significant assertions of rational agency — she remains a thinking, judging subject when every element of her world has been designed to destroy that capacity. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q037 — Duchess: "I am Duchess of Malfi still" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The declarative sentence form — simple, assertive, unadorned — contrasts with the chaotic rhetoric of the madmen's speeches and Ferdinand's increasingly disordered language. The Duchess's linguistic clarity *is* her sanity. Webster uses the simplicity of the line to stage reason as a form of resistance against elaborate cruelty. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** In a culture that associated female emotional expression with hysteria and irrationality, the Duchess's composure in the face of torture is doubly significant — she resists both the specific cruelty inflicted upon her and the gendered expectation that she will collapse into feminine weakness. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 4 — Madness as Structural Commentary: The Court as Bedlam

**Topic sentence:** Cumulatively, Webster uses madness not as a localised dramatic event but as a structural metaphor for the entire court — by the play's final act, the boundary between the sane and the insane has collapsed entirely, suggesting that the real madness is the system of power itself. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q048 — Bosola: "In a mist: I know not how" (5.5)

**AO2 focus:** The spatial metaphor "in a mist" implies total epistemological breakdown — characters can no longer distinguish motive, consequence, or moral position. The final act's confused, accidental killings — Bosola kills Antonio by mistake, Ferdinand kills in darkness — stage madness not as spectacular disorder but as banal chaos. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Jacobean revenge tragedy conventionally ended with the restoration of order through the elimination of the corrupt. Webster subverts this: his ending suggests no restoration, only exhaustion, reflecting a sceptical worldview in which providential justice is absent. \[AO3\]

### ESSAY PLAN 5: Mortality and Death

**Question:** Explore the significance of mortality and death in *The Duchess of Malfi*. You must relate your discussion to relevant contextual factors.

**Controlling theme:** MAL\_T09 (Death, Suffering and Spiritual Resistance) **Secondary themes:** MAL\_T10 (The Body, Disease and Physical Imagery), MAL\_T12 (Fate, Providence and Nihilism)

**Conceptual thesis:** Webster stages death not as a single dramatic climax but as a pervasive condition — mortality saturates the play's language, imagery, and dramatic structure from the opening scene — so that the Duchess's actual death becomes less a catastrophe than a confirmation of what the entire play has been rehearsing.

#### Paragraph 1 — Death in the Language: Corporeal Imagery from the Opening

**Topic sentence:** From the play's first act, Webster saturates dialogue with the vocabulary of bodily decay — death is not a future event but a present condition embedded in the play's linguistic texture. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q002 — Bosola: "A soldier that has lodged in a ditch / Is like to smell some o'th' earth as long as he lives" (1.1, near)

**AO2 focus:** The simile connects military service to permanent contamination — the body retains the residue of death. Olfactory imagery ("smell") makes decay sensory and inescapable. Bosola's prose register grounds mortality in physical experience rather than philosophical abstraction. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** Memento mori — the cultural injunction to "remember you must die" — was a pervasive feature of early modern visual and literary culture. Webster does not treat death as extraordinary but as the baseline condition of existence, reflecting a period in which plague, infant mortality, and public execution made death constantly visible. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 2 — The Wax Figures and the Theatrics of Death (4.1)

**Topic sentence:** The wax figures of Antonio and the children — presented to the Duchess as corpses — stage death as a theatrical effect, weaponising the Jacobean fascination with spectacle to inflict psychological suffering before physical annihilation. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q032 — Bosola (disguised as tomb-maker): "Thou art a box of worm-seed, at best but a salvatory of green mummy" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The catalogue of corporeal decay — "worm-seed", "green mummy" — reduces the human body to its post-mortem materials. The direct address ("Thou art") makes the Duchess the object of a memento mori lecture, but Webster complicates this by having Bosola deliver it in disguise — the message of mortality is itself a performance, not a truth. Sententiae (sententious couplets) give Bosola's speech the texture of moral authority while his disguise undermines it. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The anatomy theatre, wax modelling, and public dissection were features of early modern intellectual culture — the boundary between scientific enquiry and spectacle was porous. The wax figures literalise the culture's desire to see, know, and master the dead body. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 3 — The Duchess's Death: "I am i'th' way to study a long silence" (4.2)

**Topic sentence:** The Duchess's death scene is remarkable for its quietness — Webster refuses the spectacular violence typical of revenge tragedy and instead stages dying as a form of dignified withdrawal, granting the Duchess authorial control over her final moments. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q039 — Duchess: "Pull, and pull strongly, for your able strength / Must pull down heaven upon me; / Yet stay, heaven gates are not so highly arched / As princes' palaces; they that enter there / Must go upon their knees" (4.2)

**AO2 focus:** The imperative ("Pull") gives the Duchess command even of her own executioners. The shift from "pull down heaven" to "go upon their knees" modulates from defiance to humility within a single speech, creating an emotional arc compressed into five lines. Religious diction ("heaven gates") reframes death as spiritual passage rather than annihilation. The image of kneeling inverts worldly hierarchy — in death, princes must humble themselves. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The art of dying well — the ars moriendi tradition — was central to early modern Christian culture. The Duchess's composure conforms to this ideal: she meets death with spiritual readiness, faith, and self-possession, positioning her as morally superior to her tormentors. \[AO3\]

#### Paragraph 4 — Act 5: Death Without Meaning

**Topic sentence:** The final act's chaotic, accidental killings — Ferdinand stabbing in darkness, Bosola killing Antonio by mistake — strip death of the spiritual dignity the Duchess had conferred upon it, suggesting that her composure was exceptional and that death in Webster's world is ordinarily senseless. \[AO1\]

**Best quote:** MAL\_Q049 — Bosola: "We are merely the stars' tennis balls, struck and bandied / Which way please them" (5.4)

**AO2 focus:** The metaphor reduces human agency to passivity — "tennis balls" are objects acted upon, not actors. The personification of the stars ("please them") attributes agency to an indifferent cosmos. The sporting metaphor domesticates cosmic determinism, making fate feel casual rather than grand — death is not tragic but trivial. \[AO2\]

**AO3 trigger:** The tension between providential theology (God governs all events) and emergent scepticism (the universe is indifferent) is one of the defining intellectual conflicts of the Jacobean period. Webster's ending aligns with the darker, more nihilistic strand of Jacobean thought. \[AO3\]

## 7b. MODEL PARAGRAPHS

### FROM PLAN 1 (Surveillance and Control) — Two A/A\* Model Paragraphs

#### MODEL PARAGRAPH 1A: The Bedchamber Scene — Surveillance as Violation (A/A\*)

> **\[1\]** Webster stages the bedchamber scene (3.2) as the play's most acute dramatisation of surveillance as physical violation, in which Ferdinand's uninvited entry into the Duchess's private chamber collapses the boundary between patriarchal authority and sexual intrusion. **\[AO1: conceptual topic sentence — frames the scene as dramatisation of a thesis, not plot summary\]**
>
> **\[2\]** Ferdinand's question — "Virtue, where art thou hid? What hideous thing / Is it that doth eclipse thee?" — deploys interrogative rhetoric not to seek answers but to assert moral judgement, and the astronomical metaphor of "eclipse" elevates his disgust from personal rage to a cosmic register, as though the Duchess's sexuality has disordered the natural world itself. **\[AO2: method (interrogative rhetoric, metaphor) → specific word ("eclipse") → effect (elevates, cosmicises) → thematic significance (patriarchal authority claims universal validity)\]**
>
> **\[3\]** That Ferdinand enters carrying a poniard — a weapon with unmistakable phallic associations — transforms the surveillance scene into one charged with the threat of sexual violence, making explicit what the play has implied throughout: that the brothers' need to monitor the Duchess's body is inseparable from their desire to control it. **\[AO2: staging and props → symbolic reading → thematic argument about surveillance and bodily control\]**
>
> **\[4\]** This invasion of the bedchamber must be read within a Jacobean culture in which a widow's domestic space was one of the few domains where she exercised genuine autonomy — Ferdinand's intrusion is not merely personal tyranny but a systematic reassertion of patriarchal jurisdiction over the one space the Duchess has claimed as her own. **\[AO3: embedded context — widow's legal/domestic autonomy — used to deepen the analytical point, not appended as background\]**
>
> **\[5\]** Webster thus constructs surveillance not as observation from a distance but as a form of occupation: to watch the Duchess is to invade her, and to invade her is to assert that her body, her desire, and her domestic world are ultimately not her own. **\[AO1: conceptual conclusion — elevates the specific moment to a thesis-level claim about the play's treatment of surveillance\]**

**What makes this Level 5:** This paragraph works at the conceptual level throughout — it does not narrate the scene but analyses what it *means*. AO2 is precise: "eclipse" is not just identified as a metaphor but analysed for its specific *effect* (cosmic register, natural disorder). AO3 is embedded as an interpretive lens (the widow's autonomy), not listed as a separate fact. The conclusion does not summarise — it synthesises, offering a definition of surveillance that is argued, not asserted. Every sentence earns its place.

#### MODEL PARAGRAPH 1B: Bosola's Apricot Stratagem — The Body Under Surveillance (A/A\*)

> **\[1\]** Bosola's apricot stratagem (2.1) exemplifies Webster's method of making surveillance corporeal — the act of spying becomes inseparable from the act of manipulating the Duchess's body, as the fruit is deployed not to nourish but to extract a truth the Duchess's brothers believe they are owed. **\[AO1: conceptual topic sentence linking surveillance to bodily control\]**
>
> **\[2\]** The apricots function as a dramatic prop whose significance lies entirely in what they reveal rather than what they are — they induce vomiting and labour symptoms, turning the Duchess's pregnancy from a concealed secret into a visible, physical fact, and Bosola's aside to the audience positions him as a clinical observer whose commentary reduces the Duchess from a sovereign figure to a body to be decoded. **\[AO2: prop analysis → dramatic function → aside as technique → effect on characterisation\]**
>
> **\[3\]** His language in this scene shifts into prose — "A whirlwind strike off these bawd-farthingales, / For, but in a modesty of honour, / They serve but as a cover for the belly" — where the bawdy register and the aggressive imagery of stripping ("whirlwind strike off") figure his surveillance as a desire to expose, to lay bare the truth that clothing, courtesy, and rank have concealed. **\[AO2: prose register, bawdy diction, imagery of exposure → thematic significance of surveillance as stripping\]**
>
> **\[4\]** The scene draws on a Jacobean medical and cultural assumption that the body could not sustain deception indefinitely — humoral theory held that concealed emotions and conditions would inevitably manifest physically, making the pregnant body a text that could be read by those with the knowledge (or the cunning) to do so. **\[AO3: embedded context — humoral theory and bodily legibility — integrated as interpretive framework\]**
>
> **\[5\]** In this episode, Webster reveals the fundamental violence of the surveillance project: it is not enough for Bosola to observe the Duchess from the outside — her body must be forced to confess what she has chosen to conceal, making the act of watching indistinguishable from the act of assault. **\[AO1: conceptual synthesis — redefines surveillance as bodily assault\]**

**What makes this Level 5:** The paragraph treats the apricots as a dramatic device, not a plot point — the analysis is about what the prop *does* in the play's argument about surveillance. AO2 moves through multiple methods (props, prose register, aside, bawdy diction, imagery of exposure) but all are directed toward a single interpretive claim. AO3 (humoral theory) is not a detour into historical background but the analytical key that makes the scene legible. The final sentence redefines surveillance in a way that advances the essay's thesis.

### GRADE B CONTRAST — Paragraph 1A Rewritten at Grade B

> Ferdinand enters the Duchess's bedchamber in Act 3 Scene 2, which shows that he is controlling and intrusive. He says "Virtue, where art thou hid?" which is a rhetorical question that suggests he is angry with the Duchess for her secret marriage. The metaphor of "eclipse" shows that he thinks her actions have darkened her virtue. He also carries a poniard which shows the threat of violence. In the Jacobean period, men had a lot of control over women, especially in aristocratic families. This scene shows how surveillance is used as a form of power in the play.

*Why this is Grade B, not A/A:*\*

This version is relevant and supported — it identifies the right scene, the right quote, and the right historical period. But it operates at the level of observation, not argument. "Shows that he is controlling" identifies a character trait rather than analysing a dramatic method. "Which is a rhetorical question" names the technique without exploring its *effect*. "In the Jacobean period, men had a lot of control over women" is bolted-on context — it is factually accurate but analytically inert because it does not explain *how* the context shapes *this specific moment*. The conclusion ("surveillance is used as a form of power") restates the question rather than advancing a thesis. To move from B to A, the student needs to shift from identifying features to arguing about their significance — from *what* to *so what*.

### FROM PLAN 2 (Female Agency and Transgression) — Two A/A\* Model Paragraphs

#### MODEL PARAGRAPH 2A: The Wooing Scene — The Duchess as Active Subject (A/A\*)

> **\[1\]** Webster's wooing scene (1.1) is structurally radical because it positions the Duchess as the initiator of courtship, reversing the dramatic grammar of romantic comedy and tragedy alike — she proposes, she orchestrates, and she dictates the terms of her own desire. **\[AO1: conceptual topic sentence — "structurally radical" frames the scene in terms of dramatic convention, not plot\]**
>
> **\[2\]** Her acknowledgement — "The misery of us that are born great: / We are forced to woo because none dare woo us" — compresses the paradox of aristocratic female agency into a couplet: the word "forced" positions her freedom as a form of compulsion, and "born great" presents her rank simultaneously as privilege and confinement, so that the very status that empowers her also isolates her from the courtship rituals available to other women. **\[AO2: paradox → specific word analysis ("forced", "born great") → effect (privilege as confinement) → thematic significance\]**
>
> **\[3\]** The scene's staging reinforces this reversal: the Duchess controls the ring, the gesture of kneeling, and the witnessing — she manages the theatrical elements of her own proposal with the deliberateness of a director, and Antonio is rendered linguistically passive, his shorter responses marking his subordination within the scene's power dynamics. **\[AO2: staging, props (the ring), structural analysis of dialogue length → effect on power dynamic\]**
>
> **\[4\]** A Jacobean widow occupied a uniquely ambiguous position — she possessed greater legal autonomy than any other category of woman (she could hold property, enter contracts, and choose her own husband), yet this very freedom made her an object of cultural suspicion, because female desire exercised without male authorisation threatened the patriarchal logic on which aristocratic inheritance depended. **\[AO3: embedded context — widow's legal status — positioned as the interpretive key to the scene's dramatic tension\]**
>
> **\[5\]** The wooing scene thus establishes the central paradox of the play: the Duchess exercises genuine agency, but she does so within a structure that will ultimately destroy her for it — her freedom is real, but it is also, in the world Webster constructs, fatal. **\[AO1: conceptual conclusion — synthesises into the play's governing paradox\]**

**What makes this Level 5:** The paragraph opens with a claim about dramatic convention ("structurally radical"), not a description of events. AO2 analyses paradox at the level of individual words ("forced", "born great") and at the level of staging (who controls the ring, who kneels, who speaks more). AO3 is the analytical engine of the paragraph — the widow's legal status is not mentioned as background but deployed to explain *why the scene is dramatically charged*. The conclusion does not summarise — it articulates the play's governing paradox.

#### MODEL PARAGRAPH 2B: Ferdinand's Language and the Patriarchal Containment of Female Desire (A/A\*)

> **\[1\]** Ferdinand's response to his sister's marriage is revealing less for what it says about the Duchess than for what it exposes about the patriarchal logic that cannot accommodate female desire — his language does not simply condemn her choice but reveals the ideological framework that makes her agency unthinkable. **\[AO1: conceptual topic sentence — the analytical focus is Ferdinand's language as ideological exposure, not character study\]**
>
> **\[2\]** His metaphor — "Foolish men, / That e'er will trust their honour in a bark / Made of so slight weak bulrush as is woman" — reduces the Duchess from a political subject to a material: "bulrush" is natural, fragile, and disposable, and the enjambment across "bark / Made of" creates a syntactic suspension that enacts the precariousness Ferdinand claims to fear, as the line itself threatens to collapse under the weight it carries. **\[AO2: metaphor → specific word ("bulrush") → enjambment → effect (syntactic enactment of thematic anxiety)\]**
>
> **\[3\]** Critically, Ferdinand's anger generalises: the shift from the specific Duchess to "woman" as a category reveals that his objection is not to this particular marriage but to female autonomy as such — the definite article is absent, and "woman" becomes a universal, an essentialised weakness that makes all women equally unfit to bear aristocratic honour. **\[AO2: grammatical analysis — article usage, generalisation → ideological implication\]**
>
> **\[4\]** Ferdinand's rage must be understood within a Jacobean culture in which aristocratic women's marriages were instruments of dynastic strategy — the Duchess's choice of Antonio, her steward, does not merely offend Ferdinand's personal sensibility but threatens the entire system of rank, inheritance, and bloodline purity on which his authority rests. **\[AO3: embedded context — marriage as dynastic instrument, class hierarchy — used to explain the structural stakes of Ferdinand's fury\]**
>
> **\[5\]** Webster thus positions Ferdinand's language not as the speech of a tyrannical individual but as the voice of a system — patriarchy speaks through him, and his violence is not aberrant but structural, the inevitable response of a hierarchy confronted with the one thing it cannot absorb: a woman who acts. **\[AO1: conceptual conclusion — Ferdinand as vehicle of ideology, not merely a character\]**

**What makes this Level 5:** This paragraph's analytical sophistication lies in its refusal to treat Ferdinand as a character study — he is analysed as a vehicle for ideological expression. AO2 is unusually precise: it analyses not just the metaphor but the grammar (the absent article, the generalisation from specific to universal). Enjambment is not just identified but argued to *enact* the thematic anxiety. AO3 deepens the analysis by showing that Ferdinand's rage is structurally produced, not personally motivated. The conclusion offers a genuinely conceptual claim — patriarchy as system, not individual tyranny.

### PARAGRAPH SKELETONS — Plans 3, 4, and 5

#### SKELETON 3A: Bosola as Malcontent (from Plan 3, Paragraph 1)

**Topic sentence:** Webster introduces Bosola through the malcontent tradition, establishing him as a figure whose intelligence has been degraded by service to corrupt power — a man who sees clearly but cannot act cleanly.

**Key quote:** MAL\_Q003 — "I fell into the galleys in your service, where, for two years together, I wore two towels instead of a shirt" (1.1)

**Bullet structure:**

-   Physical imagery grounds resentment in bodily suffering (AO2: corporeal detail, prose register)

-   Bosola's prose vs courtiers' verse signals social exclusion (AO2: register as characterisation)

-   Malcontent as Jacobean stage type — reflects real grievances about patronage and preferment (AO3)

-   Conclude: Bosola's complicity is structurally produced, not a personal moral failing — the court corrupts its instruments

#### SKELETON 4A: The Madmen's Masque (from Plan 4, Paragraph 1)

**Topic sentence:** Webster stages the masque of madmen as a deliberate instrument of psychological torture, in which the conventions of courtly entertainment are perverted into a mechanism of cruelty — the Duchess is forced to become an audience for her own destruction.

**Key quote:** MAL\_Q035 — "Nothing but noise and folly / Can keep me in my right wits, whereas reason / And silence make me stark mad" (4.2)

**Bullet structure:**

-   Paradox inverts expected logic — noise = sanity, silence = madness (AO2: paradox, inverted expectations)

-   Anti-masque form: Webster takes the masque (associated with courtly order, Jonson) and fills it with grotesque content (AO2: genre subversion)

-   Jacobean Bedlam as entertainment — spectacle of madness was culturally familiar (AO3)

-   Conclude: the masque exposes the irrationality of the court, not of the Duchess — the supposed madness is a mirror for systemic corruption

#### SKELETON 5A: The Duchess's Death — Spiritual Resistance (from Plan 5, Paragraph 3)

**Topic sentence:** The Duchess's death scene is remarkable for its quietness — Webster refuses the spectacular violence typical of revenge tragedy and instead stages dying as a form of dignified withdrawal, granting the Duchess authorial control over her final moments.

**Key quote:** MAL\_Q039 — "Pull, and pull strongly, for your able strength / Must pull down heaven upon me; / Yet stay, heaven gates are not so highly arched / As princes' palaces; they that enter there / Must go upon their knees" (4.2)

**Bullet structure:**

-   Imperative mood ("Pull") gives the Duchess command even of her executioners (AO2: imperative, power through language)

-   Modulation from defiance to humility within a single speech — emotional arc compressed into five lines (AO2: tonal shift)

-   Religious diction reframes death as spiritual passage; kneeling inverts worldly hierarchy (AO2: religious register, symbolic inversion)

-   Ars moriendi tradition — the good death in early modern Christian culture; Duchess's composure positions her as morally superior (AO3)

-   Conclude: death becomes the Duchess's final act of agency — she controls how she dies even when she cannot control that she dies
