## 6b. AO2 METHODS TOOLKIT for *The Duchess of Malfi*

### Method 1 – Extended Metaphor

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Extended Metaphor \| \| **Definition** \| A metaphor that is sustained across multiple sentences or lines, developing a comparison in detail to create a rich dramatic idea \| \| **Effect Description** \| Builds a complex conceptual framework that readers can hold in mind, allows for multiple angles of the same idea, and creates a sense of intellectual depth through layered comparison \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q001:* Antonio's fountain metaphor – ideal court as "a common fountain whence should flow pure silver drops". The fountain image extends to include poison ("released near the head") and disease spreading throughout ("bathed the whole land") \| \| **Usage Example** \| In analysing Antonio's fountain imagery, examine how each element – fountain, silver drops, poison, head, disease – builds the overall meaning: that courtly corruption is systemic and inevitable \|

### Method 2 – Animal Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Animal Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language that draws comparisons to animals, usually to dehumanise or emphasise the predatory, savage, or instinctual nature of human behaviour \| \| **Effect Description** \| Reduces human behaviour to its most basic, instinctual level and creates disgust or heightens the sense of violence or danger. Can also indicate how corruption normalises behaviour that is animalistic \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q002:* Bosola's horse-leech simile – "Could I be one of their flattering panders, I would hang on their ears like a horse-leech till I were full, and then drop off". The image compares courtly service to parasitic feeding \| \| **Usage Example** \| Ferdinand's frequent use of wolf imagery ("I am in blood after a wolf") creates a sense that his violence isn't aberrant but natural; it also seeds the idea for his later lycanthropic madness \|

### Method 3 – Imperative Mood

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Imperative Mood \| \| **Definition** \| A verb mood that expresses commands, requests, or instructions, often implying a power dynamic where the speaker is authoritative and expects obedience \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates a sense of authority, urgency, or compulsion. Also can expose the speaker's assumption of power over others – and therefore can reveal the fragility of such power when disobeyed \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q009:* Ferdinand to Bosola: "Be yourself; / Keep your old garb of melancholy; 'twill express / You envy those that stand above your reach, / Yet strive not to come near 'em". Ferdinand constructs Bosola as an instrument through imperatives \| \| **Usage Example** \| The Duchess's commands in 1.1 – "I do give you the special charge / Care of my lord Cardinal..." etc – operate as imperatives that assert her authority if oddly politely and ironically. She later subjects herself from her brothers \|

### Method 4 – Fragmented Syntax

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Fragmented Syntax \| \| **Definition** \| Incomplete sentences, broken clauses, and abrupt changes in grammatical structure, often indicating emotional disturbance, urgency or intellectual disintegration \| \| **Effect Description** \| Disrupts reading rhythm and shows when a character's emotional state overwhelms their capacity for coherent thought. Also creates an intimate effect, as the audience experiences the fragmentation directly \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q073:* Ferdinand's mad state – "It was desire of revenge... I'm now to origin of it all..." The elliptical, broken syntax reflects his undoing \| \| **Usage Example** \| Ferdinand's final speech ("Cover her face; mine eyes dazzle; she died young") reveals his disintegration through broken semicolon-separated clauses that cannot form coherent thoughts \|

### Method 5 – Sententiae

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Sententiae \| \| **Definition** \| Moralising maxims or sayings, often presented as universal truths but in context revealing their emptiness, hypocrisy, or inapplicability \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates irony between the intended moral weight and the context of its use, exposes characters that claim moral authority while acting immorally \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q087:* The Cardinal: "I have heard grief named the eldest child of sin. You were too much i' th' light"; custom hath the same effect on most people". A general maxim from the man who murders his sister \| \| **Usage Example** \| The Duchess of Malfi's use of sententiae often occurs in the lips of the most corrupt characters – showing how moralising language can be used to disguise immoral intentions \|

### Method 6 – Prose / Verse Register Shifts

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Prose / Verse Register Shifts \| \| **Definition** \| Movement between blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) and prose (no fixed metrical structure), often signalling social rank or changes in emotional intensity \| \| **Effect Description** \| Verse creates a sense of social and aesthetic elevation, while prose feels more naturalistic, intimate, or realistic. Register shifts can indicate social hierarchy and, conversely, can reveal the gap between public form and private intention \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q052:* Bosola's prose register marks his exclusion from courtly power while also providing a more direct, unmediated mode of expression for his moral commentary \| \| **Usage Example** \| Bosola and the servant figures speak primarily in prose, while the nobility speak in verse – but the audience quickly learns that the elevated form can conceal savage amoral content \|

### Method 7 – Ironic Juxtaposition

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Ironic Juxtaposition \| \| **Definition** \| Placing two contrasting ideas, images, or statements alongside each other to emphasise the gap between appearance and reality, or between expectation and outcome \| \| **Effect Description** \| Draws attention to the comparison between contrasting elements in a way that exposes irony or hypocrisy. Can also create dramatic tension by emphasising contradictions \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q050:* The ironic juxtaposition of the Cardinal's "sickness and his only drama with Julia – the audience knows his verbal illness is actually his sexual affair, revealing clerical corruption as normal \| \| **Usage Example** \| The play consistently juxtaposes elevated moral language (piety, fearfulness, honour, and virtue) against the actual behaviour (cruelty, hypocrisy, violence) of those who claim it \|

### Method 8 – Clausura/Caesura

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Clausura/Caesura \| \| **Definition** \| A strong pause or cesura in the middle of a metrical line, often used to create emphasis, show internal conflict and reflect split emotions \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates a dramatic pause that forces attention on what comes before and after the break. Can ignore internal conflict, indecision, or emotional division that cannot be expressed continuously \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q073:* "Cover her face; mine eyes dazzle; she died young". The semicolons create caesura that fragment Ferdinand's speech, reflecting his emotional disintegration at seeing what he caused \| \| **Usage Example** \| The Duchess of Malfi's consistent use of caesura, particularly in emotionally charged speeches, creates an impression of conversation halting and starting – inevitably builds tension \|

### Method 9 – Self-revelation/Aside

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Self-revelation/Aside \| \| **Definition** \| A dramatic convention where a character speaks directly to the audience or expresses internal thoughts, often revealing true motives or desires concealed from other characters on stage \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates intimacy with the audience, establishes dramatic irony by revealing information other characters lack, and undermines appearances, as audiences know what characters don't \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q051:* The Cardinal: "I'm grown to need a soldier and a churchman. This self-diagnosis reveals that he has been consciously merging violence and religion, rather than abandoning one for the other \| \| **Usage Example** \| Bosola's frequent asides serve as moral commentary on the action and establish him as the play's diagnostician of corruption, even as he becomes complicit in it \|

### Method 10 – Body Horror/Grotesque Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Body Horror/Grotesque Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language and imagery that focuses on the physical body in distorted, decomposing, grotesque, or violent forms, often emphasising the gap between external beauty and internal decay \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates discomfort and disgust in the audience, forces attention on the mortality and fragility of human flesh, and creates a sense of the material, temporal nature of human existence \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q084:* "A reason searched out of the Devil's anatomy" Ferdinand reduces Julius's - his - noun to a "green mummy", a dead body, and also to "worm-seed", bait for worms \| \| **Usage Example** \| The play is riddled with corporeal imagery – "worm-seed", "green mummy"...This impregnates the play with a senseof corruption, the emphasis is on the death and ruinage that emptiness can actively corrupt \|

### Method 11 – Interrogative Rhetoric

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Interrogative Rhetoric \| \| **Definition** \| Rhetorical questions—questions asked not to gain information but to assert a point more forcefully, often implying that the answer should be obvious \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates emphasis and force, makes the audience feel implicated in finding the answer, and often exposes the absurdity of the situation or accusations being expressed \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q026:* Ferdinand: "Virtue, where art thou hid? What hideous thing / Is it that doth eclipse thee?". Ferdinand's questions are not genuine enquiries but rhetorical assertions of moral judgement \| \| **Usage Example** \| Webster's frequent use of rhetorical questions creates the play's oppressive dynamic: justify, don't flinch, and intimidate are forced to make them mass-impendent, and bypasses reason \|

### Method 12 – Euphemism

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Euphemism \| \| **Definition** \| A mild or vague expression substituted for a harsher or more direct one, especially when discussing sexuality, violence or bodily functions and taboo subjects \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates a sense of concealment that often draws attention to what is being hidden by achieving it. Can also indicate social decorum and implicate the audience in understanding what really is being discussed \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q054:* The Cardinal has "entertained" Julia – a polite euphemism for sexual affair that makes clerical adultery feel like social hospitality \| \| **Usage Example** \| The Duchess of Malfi's frequent use of euphemisms around female sexuality, death, and violence ironically draws attention to the brutal reality that social decorum serves to mask \|

### Method 13 – Light and Dark Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Light and Dark Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| A pattern of imagery that contrasts light, brightness, and illumination against darkness, shadow and obscurity, often to reflect knowledge v. ignorance, good v. evil, or beauty v. decay \| \| **Effect Description** \| Establishes a stark binary or opposition and allows for subsequent complexity when equations are overturned. Also create systematic involvement with death, hiding, violence, or struggle \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q053:* Ferdinand "Virtue, where art thou hid? What hideous thing / Is it that doth eclipse thee?" uses "eclipse" to understand darkness surrounding reality against virtue \| \| **Usage Example** \| Webster's light/dark imagery hovers around the Duchess (torch of a court filled with dark hidden activities) because light becomes associated with feminine vulnerability rather than traditional goodness \|

### Method 14 – Repetition and Parallelism

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Repetition and Parallelism \| \| **Definition** \| Repeating words, phrases, or grammatical structures to create emphasis, rhythm, or thematic connection, often building intensity or highlighting obsession \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates hypnotic or insistent effect, can show obsessive thinking, builds emotional intensity through accumulation, and creates patterns that link related ideas \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q068:* The Duchess's repeated "I" statements in her final scene – "I pray thee look thou giv'st my little boy... I pray thee, look thou giv'st my little boy..." The repetition shows her maternal focus even in death \| \| **Usage Example** \| Ferdinand's repetitive language around blood and wolves creates a pattern that reinforces his savage nature and prepares for his lycanthropic breakdown \|

### Method 15 – Natural Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Natural Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language that draws on elements from the natural world – plants, animals, weather, seasons – often to create contrast with the artificial courtly environment or to suggest natural law vs. human corruption \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates sense of what is natural, organic, or pure in contrast to artifice and corruption. Can also suggest cycles, growth, decay, and the inevitability of natural processes \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q045:* The Duchess's comparison of herself to "the wild benefit of nature" suggests her marriage is natural while court restrictions are artificial \| \| **Usage Example** \| Natural imagery around the Duchess (birds, growth, spring) contrasts with the artificial, poisonous imagery surrounding the court, suggesting her alignment with natural law rather than corrupt human institutions \|

### Method 16 – Physical/Spatial Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Physical/Spatial Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language that emphasises physical space, containment, enclosure, and spatial relationships, often reflecting psychological states or power dynamics through geographical metaphors \| \| **Effect Description** \| Makes abstract psychological or social concepts concrete through spatial terms. Can show entrapment, liberation, hierarchy, or distance in ways audiences can visualise \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q062:* The Duchess imprisoned becomes a spatial image of her broader entrapment by patriarchal authority – her physical confinement reflects social restrictions \| \| **Usage Example** \| The play's frequent imagery of prisons, traps, and enclosed spaces creates a claustrophobic atmosphere that reflects the characters' psychological entrapment in cycles of corruption and revenge \|

### Method 17 – Religious/Devotional Language

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Religious/Devotional Language \| \| **Definition** \| Vocabulary and imagery drawn from religious contexts – prayers, salvation, damnation, saints, sin – often used ironically to highlight moral corruption or spiritual emptiness \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates tension between spiritual ideals and material reality, often exposing hypocrisy when religious language is used by corrupt characters. Can also suggest genuine spiritual struggle \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q078:* The Cardinal's religious vocabulary while planning murder creates devastating irony that exposes clerical corruption as systemic \| \| **Usage Example** \| The pervasive religious language throughout the play, particularly from the Cardinal, creates dramatic irony as audiences see spiritual vocabulary used to mask or justify immoral actions \|

### Method 18 – Musical/Acoustic Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Musical/Acoustic Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language relating to sound, music, harmony, discord, and silence, often used to suggest emotional states, social harmony/discord, or the relationship between public performance and private reality \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates auditory landscape that reinforces emotional or thematic content. Can suggest harmony vs. discord, performance vs. authenticity, or the power of sound to reveal or conceal truth \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q041:* The echo scene uses acoustic imagery to suggest supernatural judgment and the persistence of truth even when suppressed \| \| **Usage Example** \| Musical imagery around the Duchess (her "harmony") contrasts with discordant sounds associated with her brothers, suggesting her as a source of natural order in a chaotic court \|

### Method 19 – Economic/Commercial Language

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Economic/Commercial Language \| \| **Definition** \| Vocabulary drawn from trade, commerce, debt, payment, and exchange, often used to suggest the commodification of human relationships or moral corruption through material concerns \| \| **Effect Description** \| Reduces human relationships to economic transactions, exposes how money corrupts social bonds, and suggests the materialistic basis of supposedly noble or spiritual claims \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q037:* Bosola's language of "payment" and "reward" for his spying shows how loyalty has become a commercial transaction rather than genuine allegiance \| \| **Usage Example** \| The play's frequent commercial imagery reveals how court relationships operate on principles of exchange and debt rather than genuine affection or moral obligation \|

### Method 20 – Medical/Disease Imagery

\| Field \| Content \| \| -:- \| -:- \| \| **Method** \| Medical/Disease Imagery \| \| **Definition** \| Language relating to sickness, medicine, cure, infection, and healing, often used metaphorically to suggest moral corruption as a kind of disease that spreads through social systems \| \| **Effect Description** \| Creates sense that corruption is contagious and systemic rather than individual, suggests some problems require surgical solutions, and implies the possibility (or impossibility) of cure \| \| **Example from MAL** \| *MAL/Q001:* Antonio's fountain metaphor includes disease imagery – poison "bathing the whole land" – suggesting corruption spreads like infection \| \| **Usage Example** \| Medical imagery throughout the play presents corruption as epidemic rather than individual failing, suggesting systemic problems that require dramatic intervention to cure \|

### Summary: The Complete AO2 Toolkit

This toolkit covers the 20 major dramatic and linguistic methods Webster employs throughout *The Duchess of Malfi*. Each method serves the play's exploration of corruption, power, gender, and moral complexity. Students should:

1.  **Identify the method** in specific quotations

2.  **Analyse the immediate effect** on audience understanding

3.  **Connect to broader themes** about court corruption, female agency, and moral decay

4.  **Compare with similar methods** in other parts of the play

5.  **Evaluate Webster's overall dramatic purpose** in employing these techniques

The methods work in combination – extended metaphors often include animal imagery, while religious language frequently employs euphemism. Advanced analysis should examine how multiple methods operate simultaneously within single quotations to create complex dramatic effects.
